In the fall of 1973, the University of California, Berkeley admitted about 44% of the men who applied to its graduate programs and about 35% of the women. With more than 12,000 applicants, a gap that big looks hard to explain away, and it raised an obvious question: were departments treating women unfairly?

The statistician Peter Bickel and two colleagues looked closer, and in 1975 they published a now-famous analysis in *Science*. Department by department, the gap didn't hold up. You're going to rebuild their argument using the real numbers for Berkeley's six largest departments, which the paper identified only by letter. The question to keep in mind is which of these two paths explains the gap:

```mermaid caption="Two possible routes from an applicant's gender to the admission decision"
flowchart LR
    G["Applicant's gender"] -->|"direct: departments treat applicants differently"| A["Admitted?"]
    G -->|"indirect"| D["Which department they applied to"]
    D -->|"departments differ in how selective they are"| A
```

## Step 1: Enter the data in tidy form

Each row is one gender in one department, with counts, not percentages. Counts let you recompute any rate later; percentages alone would make it impossible to combine departments correctly.

```python run
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

BLUE, ORANGE, INK, MUTED = "#2a78d6", "#eb6834", "#0b0b0b", "#52514e"
colors = {"Men": BLUE, "Women": ORANGE}
plt.rcParams.update({"axes.spines.top": False, "axes.spines.right": False})

rows = [
    ("A", "Men", 825, 512), ("A", "Women", 108, 89),
    ("B", "Men", 560, 353), ("B", "Women", 25, 17),
    ("C", "Men", 325, 120), ("C", "Women", 593, 202),
    ("D", "Men", 417, 138), ("D", "Women", 375, 131),
    ("E", "Men", 191, 53), ("E", "Women", 393, 94),
    ("F", "Men", 373, 22), ("F", "Women", 341, 24),
]
berkeley = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["dept", "gender", "applied", "admitted"])
berkeley["rate"] = berkeley["admitted"] / berkeley["applied"]

assert berkeley["applied"].sum() == 4526, "total applicants should match the published table"
print(berkeley.round(3))
```

The `assert` is a cheap guard against typos: if any count were mistyped, the total wouldn't match the 4,526 applicants in the published table, and the notebook would stop right here instead of producing a wrong chart later.

## Step 2: Compute the pooled rates

"Pooled" means you add up the counts across all departments first, then divide. That's what the headline number did.

```python run alt="Bar chart of pooled admission rates across the six departments: men 44.5 percent, women 30.4 percent, on an axis from 0 to 100." caption="Pooled across the six departments, men were admitted far more often"
pooled = berkeley.groupby("gender")[["applied", "admitted"]].sum()
pooled["rate"] = pooled["admitted"] / pooled["applied"]
print(pooled.round(3))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4.5, 3.6))
bars = ax.bar(pooled.index, pooled["rate"] * 100, color=[colors[g] for g in pooled.index], width=0.55)
ax.bar_label(bars, fmt="%.1f%%", padding=3, color=INK)
ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
ax.set_ylabel("Admitted (%)")
ax.set_title("Pooled: a 14-point gap", loc="left")
```

In these six departments the gap is even wider than in the university as a whole: 44.5% against 30.4%. If you stopped here, you'd conclude that these departments favored men.

## Step 3: Look department by department

`pivot` reshapes the tidy table so each department is a row and each gender a column, which makes the comparison easy to read and to plot.

```python run alt="Grouped bar chart of admission rates by department. In A, men 62 percent and women 82 percent. B, 63 and 68. C, 37 and 34. D, 33 and 35. E, 28 and 24. F, 6 and 7. Women's bars are higher in A, B, D, and F." caption="Within departments, women did as well as or better than men in four of six"
by_dept = berkeley.pivot(index="dept", columns="gender", values="rate")
by_dept["women minus men"] = by_dept["Women"] - by_dept["Men"]
print((by_dept * 100).round(1))
print("\nDepartments where women's rate was higher:", (by_dept["women minus men"] > 0).sum(), "of", len(by_dept))

x = np.arange(len(by_dept))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.8))
for offset, gender in [(-0.2, "Men"), (0.2, "Women")]:
    ax.bar(x + offset, by_dept[gender] * 100, width=0.38, color=colors[gender], label=gender)
ax.set_xticks(x, [f"Dept {d}" for d in by_dept.index])
ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
ax.set_ylabel("Admitted (%)")
ax.legend(frameon=False)
ax.set_title("Department by department, the gap mostly disappears", loc="left")
```

The direction flips. Women's admission rate was higher in A, B, D, and F, and lower in C and E by three or four points. In department A, women were admitted 82% of the time against 62% for men. Every department's numbers are real, and so is the pooled gap. Both can be true at once, and the next step shows how.

## Step 4: Find the lurking variable

Look at *where* each group applied. Two numbers per department tell the story: how selective it is (its admission rate for everyone) and what share of its applicants were women.

```python run alt="Scatter plot with one circle per department, sized by number of applicants. The x-axis is the department's overall admission rate and the y-axis is the percent of its applicants who were women. A and B sit at the right with high admission rates (about 64 percent) and few women (12 and 4 percent). C, D, E, and F sit to the left with admission rates from 6 to 35 percent and 47 to 67 percent women." caption="The departments women applied to most were the hardest to get into"
dept = berkeley.groupby("dept")[["applied", "admitted"]].sum()
dept["overall rate"] = dept["admitted"] / dept["applied"]
women_applied = berkeley[berkeley["gender"] == "Women"].set_index("dept")["applied"]
dept["share women"] = women_applied / dept["applied"]
print((dept[["overall rate", "share women"]] * 100).round(1))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.scatter(dept["overall rate"] * 100, dept["share women"] * 100, s=dept["applied"] / 2,
           color=BLUE, alpha=0.45, edgecolors=BLUE)
for name, row in dept.iterrows():
    ax.annotate(name, (row["overall rate"] * 100, row["share women"] * 100), ha="center", va="center", color=INK)
ax.set_xlim(0, 75)
ax.set_ylim(0, 75)
ax.set_xlabel("Department admission rate, everyone (%)")
ax.set_ylabel("Applicants who were women (%)")
ax.set_title("Women applied mostly to the selective departments", loc="left")
```

Departments A and B admitted almost two-thirds of applicants, and only a small share of their applicants were women. Departments C through F admitted far fewer people, and that's where most women applied. Department is the **lurking variable** (a confounder from Module 6's Notes): it's linked to gender (who applied where) *and* to the outcome (how likely admission was).

## Step 5: See the flip as a weighted average

Here's the arithmetic behind the paradox. A group's pooled rate is the average of its department rates, **weighted by where that group applied**:

pooled rate = Σ (share of the group's applications to the department × the group's admission rate in that department)

Men's weights pile up on A and B, with their high rates. Women's weights pile up on C through F, with their low rates. So you can ask a fair question: what would women's pooled rate be if they had applied to departments in the same proportions as men?

```python run alt="Bar chart with three bars on a 0 to 100 percent axis: men, actual, 44.5 percent; women, actual, 30.4 percent; women if they had applied like men, 51.6 percent." caption="Same department rates, different mix of applications"
apps = berkeley.pivot(index="dept", columns="gender", values="applied")
weights = apps / apps.sum()

check = (weights["Women"] * by_dept["Women"]).sum()
women_if_like_men = (weights["Men"] * by_dept["Women"]).sum()
print(f"Women, recomputed from weights:     {check:.1%}")
print(f"Women, if applied with men's mix:   {women_if_like_men:.1%}")

labels = ["Men, actual", "Women, actual", "Women, men's mix"]
values = [pooled.loc["Men", "rate"], pooled.loc["Women", "rate"], women_if_like_men]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.6))
bars = ax.bar(labels, np.array(values) * 100, color=[BLUE, ORANGE, ORANGE], width=0.55)
bars[2].set_hatch("//")
bars[2].set_edgecolor("white")
ax.bar_label(bars, fmt="%.1f%%", padding=3, color=INK)
ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
ax.set_ylabel("Admitted (%)")
ax.set_title("Apply like men did, and women's rate beats men's", loc="left")
```

The first line reproduces women's pooled 30.4%, which confirms the formula. The second is a **standardized rate**: women's own department-by-department success, applied to men's mix of applications. It comes out at 51.6%, higher than men's 44.5%. The gap in the headline came from where people applied, not from how each department treated the applicants in front of it.

:::warning
**Averaging percentages instead of counts.** `by_dept["Women"].mean()` gives about 41.7%, which matches nothing: it treats department B's 25 women the same as department C's 593. Pool by summing counts, or weight each rate by its share of applicants, as above.
:::

## Step 6: Write a claim the data supports

Here's a careful summary of what these numbers show, in the template from the Notes:

> In fall 1973, across Berkeley's six largest graduate departments, men were admitted at 44.5% and women at 30.4%. Within departments, women's rates were higher in four of the six and slightly lower in two. The pooled gap is explained by women applying mostly to departments that admitted few applicants of either gender.

And here's what it does **not** show:

- **That no one was ever treated unfairly.** These are counts, with no data on applicants' qualifications. A department could have treated individuals unfairly in either direction without it showing up in totals.
- **Why women applied to more selective departments.** Bickel and his colleagues argued that the cause lay earlier, in which fields women had been steered toward before they ever applied. That's a question about a different stage of the pipeline, needing different data.
- **Anything about other years or universities.** Six departments in one year are one snapshot.

## The finished program

This standalone cell rebuilds the data and the whole analysis, and prints a short report. Run it on a fresh runtime and you'll get the same numbers.

```python run alt="Two-panel figure. Left: pooled admission rates, men 44.5 percent, women 30.4 percent. Right: admission rates by department A through F for men and women side by side, with women equal or higher in A, B, D, and F." caption="The full Berkeley analysis in one figure"
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

BLUE, ORANGE, INK = "#2a78d6", "#eb6834", "#0b0b0b"
colors = {"Men": BLUE, "Women": ORANGE}

rows = [
    ("A", "Men", 825, 512), ("A", "Women", 108, 89), ("B", "Men", 560, 353), ("B", "Women", 25, 17),
    ("C", "Men", 325, 120), ("C", "Women", 593, 202), ("D", "Men", 417, 138), ("D", "Women", 375, 131),
    ("E", "Men", 191, 53), ("E", "Women", 393, 94), ("F", "Men", 373, 22), ("F", "Women", 341, 24),
]
berkeley = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["dept", "gender", "applied", "admitted"])
assert berkeley["applied"].sum() == 4526

pooled = berkeley.groupby("gender")[["applied", "admitted"]].sum()
pooled["rate"] = pooled["admitted"] / pooled["applied"]
rates = berkeley.assign(rate=berkeley["admitted"] / berkeley["applied"]).pivot(index="dept", columns="gender", values="rate")
apps = berkeley.pivot(index="dept", columns="gender", values="applied")
standardized = ((apps["Men"] / apps["Men"].sum()) * rates["Women"]).sum()

print(f"Pooled: men {pooled.loc['Men', 'rate']:.1%}, women {pooled.loc['Women', 'rate']:.1%}")
print(f"Women higher in {(rates['Women'] > rates['Men']).sum()} of {len(rates)} departments")
print(f"Women's rate with men's application mix: {standardized:.1%}")

fig, (left, right) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.8), width_ratios=[1, 2.4])
bars = left.bar(pooled.index, pooled["rate"] * 100, color=[colors[g] for g in pooled.index], width=0.55)
left.bar_label(bars, fmt="%.1f%%", padding=3, color=INK)
left.set_title("Pooled", loc="left")
x = np.arange(len(rates))
for offset, gender in [(-0.2, "Men"), (0.2, "Women")]:
    right.bar(x + offset, rates[gender] * 100, width=0.38, color=colors[gender], label=gender)
right.set_xticks(x, [f"Dept {d}" for d in rates.index])
right.legend(frameon=False)
right.set_title("By department", loc="left")
for ax in (left, right):
    ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
    ax.set_ylabel("Admitted (%)")
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
fig.suptitle("Berkeley 1973: the pooled gap reverses inside most departments", x=0.01, ha="left")
fig.tight_layout()
```

:::quiz
Q: Why did Step 1 store applicant and admitted **counts** rather than just each department's admission percentage?
- [ ] Percentages can't be plotted as bars
  They can. The problem is combining them.
- [x] Counts let you pool departments correctly; percentages alone lose how many people each one represents
  Pooling needs the totals. With only percentages, department B's 25 women would count as much as department C's 593.
- [ ] pandas can't divide one column by another
  It can, as `berkeley["admitted"] / berkeley["applied"]` shows.

Q: What did the standardized rate in Step 5 hold fixed?
- [ ] Each department's overall admission rate for everyone
  It used women's own rates in each department, not the combined rates.
- [x] The mix of departments applied to, set to men's mix for both groups
  Using the same weights for both groups removes the effect of where people applied, leaving only how they fared within departments.
- [ ] The total number of applicants
  Totals don't change the rate; the weights (shares of applications) are what matter.

Q: A friend reads your analysis and says, "So Berkeley proved there was no discrimination." What's the best reply?
- [ ] Right, the department rates settle it
  The data has no information on applicants' qualifications, so it can't rule out unfair treatment of individuals.
- [x] The data shows the pooled gap comes from where people applied; it can't settle everything about fairness
  That's the supported claim. It also leaves open why women applied mostly to the most selective departments.
- [ ] No, because the pooled numbers show bias against women
  The pooled gap is real, but the department breakdown shows it's explained by application patterns, not by departments' decisions.
:::

:::takeaways
- Simpson's paradox: a trend in every subgroup can reverse when the groups are pooled, because the groups differ in size and in their baseline rates.
- Keep counts, not just percentages, so you can pool and re-weight correctly.
- A pooled rate is a weighted average of group rates. Different weights can flip the comparison.
- A standardized rate puts both groups on the same mix, isolating the within-group difference.
- The careful claim names what the data explains and what it leaves open.
:::
