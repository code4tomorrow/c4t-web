In 2012 the *New England Journal of Medicine* published a short paper by the cardiologist Franz Messerli with a startling chart. Across 23 countries, the more chocolate people ate per person, the more Nobel Prizes the country had won per person. The correlation was r = 0.79, which is very strong.

Messerli himself called the result "hypothesis-generating only." But news sites ran headlines like "Eating chocolate produces Nobel prize winners." Nobody had measured whether any laureate ate chocolate. The data were country averages, and rich countries both buy more chocolate and fund more research. This lesson gives you the tools to see through a claim like that in about thirty seconds.

## What r measures, and what it misses

The **Pearson correlation coefficient**, r, measures how closely two numeric variables follow a straight line. It runs from −1 (a perfect downhill line) through 0 (no straight-line pattern) to +1 (a perfect uphill line). Here are five datasets of 150 points each:

```python run alt="Five scatter plots in a row. r = 0.88 is a tight uphill cloud. r = 0.41 is a loose uphill cloud. r = 0.03 is a round blob. r = -0.66 is a downhill cloud. The last, r = 0.04, is a clear U-shaped curve." caption="The last panel has a strong pattern, and r misses it"
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

BLUE, ORANGE, INK, MUTED = "#2a78d6", "#eb6834", "#0b0b0b", "#52514e"
plt.rcParams.update({"axes.spines.top": False, "axes.spines.right": False})
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

def correlated(r, n=150):
    x = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
    y = r * x + np.sqrt(1 - r**2) * rng.normal(0, 1, n)
    return x, y

panels = [correlated(0.9), correlated(0.4), correlated(0.0), correlated(-0.7)]
u_x = rng.uniform(-2, 2, 150)
panels.append((u_x, u_x**2 + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 150)))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 5, figsize=(12, 2.6))
for ax, (x, y) in zip(axes, panels):
    ax.scatter(x, y, s=8, color=BLUE, alpha=0.6)
    ax.set_title(f"r = {np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]:.2f}", loc="left")
    ax.set_xticks([])
    ax.set_yticks([])
fig.tight_layout()
```

The last panel is the warning. y depends completely on x, yet r is about zero, because the pattern is a curve, not a line: the left half slopes down and the right half slopes up, and they cancel. **r near 0 means "no straight-line relationship," not "no relationship."** And r says nothing about *why* two things move together, which is the bigger problem.

## Four reasons two variables move together

When you see a correlation between X and Y, there are four broad explanations, and "X causes Y" is only one of them:

1. **X causes Y.** Studying more raises test scores.
2. **Y causes X** (reverse causation). Messerli himself noted that smarter populations might buy more chocolate, rather than the other way round.
3. **Something else causes both** (a **confounder**). National wealth plausibly raises both chocolate sales and research funding.
4. **Chance.** Compare enough variables and some will line up by luck. Per capita cheese consumption once tracked deaths by bedsheet entanglement remarkably closely.

## Watch a confounder build a fake relationship

Simulation lets you know the truth for certain, because you wrote it. Here, temperature drives both ice cream and sunscreen sales at a beach shop, and **neither one affects the other at all**:

```python run
days = 365
temp = rng.normal(20, 7, days)
ice_cream = (40 + 6 * temp + rng.normal(0, 25, days)).clip(0).round()
sunscreen = (5 + 2.2 * temp + rng.normal(0, 12, days)).clip(0).round()
beach = pd.DataFrame({"temp_c": temp.round(1), "ice_creams": ice_cream, "sunscreen": sunscreen})
print(beach.corr().round(2))
```

`DataFrame.corr()` gives r for every pair of columns. Ice cream and sunscreen correlate at 0.63, and someone looking at only those two columns might start a "sunscreen promotes snacking" theory.

To check, **hold the confounder still**. One way is to remove the part of each variable that temperature explains (fit a line against temperature with `np.polyfit`, then keep the leftovers, called residuals) and correlate what's left:

```python run alt="Two scatter plots. Left: ice cream sales against sunscreen sales, colored from light to dark blue by temperature, forming an uphill cloud with r = 0.63, the hottest days in the top right. Right: the same days after removing temperature's effect from both, a shapeless round cloud with r = -0.04." caption="Remove temperature, and the relationship vanishes"
def remove_effect(y, x):
    slope, intercept = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
    return y - (slope * x + intercept)

left_ice = remove_effect(beach["ice_creams"], beach["temp_c"])
left_sun = remove_effect(beach["sunscreen"], beach["temp_c"])

fig, (raw, adjusted) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.8))
dots = raw.scatter(beach["ice_creams"], beach["sunscreen"], c=beach["temp_c"], cmap="Blues",
                   s=14, edgecolors=MUTED, linewidths=0.2)
fig.colorbar(dots, ax=raw, label="Temperature (°C)")
raw.set_title(f"Raw: r = {beach['ice_creams'].corr(beach['sunscreen']):.2f}", loc="left")
raw.set_xlabel("Ice creams sold")
raw.set_ylabel("Sunscreen bottles sold")

adjusted.scatter(left_ice, left_sun, s=14, color=BLUE, alpha=0.6)
adjusted.axhline(0, color=MUTED, linewidth=0.8)
adjusted.axvline(0, color=MUTED, linewidth=0.8)
adjusted.set_title(f"Temperature removed: r = {np.corrcoef(left_ice, left_sun)[0, 1]:.2f}", loc="left")
adjusted.set_xlabel("Ice creams, above or below expected")
adjusted.set_ylabel("Sunscreen, above or below expected")
fig.tight_layout()
```

On the left, darker dots (hotter days) sit in the top right: the whole pattern is temperature. On the right, once temperature is accounted for, a day with surprisingly high ice cream sales tells you nothing about sunscreen. In real data you rarely know every confounder, which is why careful researchers prefer **randomized experiments**: flipping a coin to decide who gets the treatment breaks every hidden link at once.

:::warning
**"Controlled for X" only covers X.** Removing temperature fixed this simulation because temperature was the only confounder. With real data there may be others you never measured. Write "after accounting for temperature, the correlation disappeared," not "we proved ice cream and sunscreen are unrelated."
:::

## A bigger sample won't fix a biased one

In 1936 the *Literary Digest* magazine polled ten million Americans about the presidential election, mostly drawn from its own readers, car registrations, and telephone directories. About 2.4 million replied. It predicted Alf Landon would win 57% of the vote. Franklin Roosevelt won about 61% and carried every state except Maine and Vermont.

In 1936, people with cars and phones were wealthier than average and leaned toward Landon, and the people who bothered to mail back a ballot leaned even further. Here's a simulation of the first problem. A quarter of voters are on the lists, and they favor Roosevelt far less than everyone else:

```python run alt="Line chart of estimated Roosevelt support against sample size from 100 to 20,000 on a log scale. A dashed line marks the true value near 63 percent. The random-sample line wobbles near the truth and settles onto it. The list-only line stays near 40 percent at every sample size." caption="More data from the wrong people gives a more precise wrong answer"
voters = 100_000
on_lists = rng.random(voters) < 0.25
for_roosevelt = rng.random(voters) < np.where(on_lists, 0.40, 0.70)
truth = for_roosevelt.mean()

everyone, listed = np.arange(voters), np.flatnonzero(on_lists)
sizes = [100, 300, 1_000, 3_000, 10_000, 20_000]
random_est = [for_roosevelt[rng.choice(everyone, n, replace=False)].mean() for n in sizes]
listed_est = [for_roosevelt[rng.choice(listed, n, replace=False)].mean() for n in sizes]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.6))
ax.axhline(truth * 100, color=INK, linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
ax.annotate(f"truth: {truth:.1%}", (sizes[0], truth * 100), xytext=(0, 6), textcoords="offset points", color=INK)
ax.plot(sizes, np.array(random_est) * 100, color=BLUE, marker="o", linewidth=2)
ax.plot(sizes, np.array(listed_est) * 100, color=ORANGE, marker="o", linewidth=2)
ax.annotate("random sample", (sizes[-1], random_est[-1] * 100), xytext=(0, -16), textcoords="offset points", ha="right", color=INK)
ax.annotate("car and phone lists only", (sizes[-1], listed_est[-1] * 100), xytext=(0, 8), textcoords="offset points", ha="right", color=INK)
ax.set_xscale("log")
ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
ax.set_xlabel("People polled (log scale)")
ax.set_ylabel("Estimated Roosevelt support (%)")
```

The random sample of 1,000 lands within a few points of the truth. The list-only sample of 20,000 is off by more than 20 points, and polling 200,000 from the same lists would land in the same wrong place. **Sample size shrinks random error; it does nothing about bias.** Ask who could end up in the data, and who couldn't.

## Survivorship bias: the data that didn't come back

During World War II, the statistician Abraham Wald worked with the US military on where to add armor to bombers. Returning planes were full of bullet holes in the wings and body, and the obvious plan was to armor those spots. Wald's point was that these were the planes that *survived*. Planes hit in the engines mostly didn't return to be counted, so the places with no holes were the ones that needed armor.

![Outline of a World War II bomber covered with red dots marking where returning planes had been hit: many on the wings, tail, and middle of the body, and few on the engines and cockpit](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b2/Survivorship-bias.svg "Illustration: Martin Grandjean, McGeddon, US Air Force, CC BY-SA 4.0")

The same trap shows up everywhere data gets filtered by an outcome: studying only successful startups to learn what makes startups succeed, averaging the scores of students who chose to submit them to a test-optional college, or judging old buildings by the ones still standing. Before analyzing, ask what process decided which rows made it into your file.

## When splitting the data flips the answer

The strangest trap gets the whole next lesson. In **Simpson's paradox**, a trend that holds in every subgroup reverses when the groups are combined, because a confounder is spread unevenly across them. You'll rebuild the famous real case, UC Berkeley's 1973 graduate admissions.

## Writing a claim that fits the data

Each of these traps has the same cure: say exactly what the data shows, and no more. A useful template:

> In **[who and when]**, **[X]** and **[Y]** were **[direction]** related (**r = …**). This doesn't show that X causes Y; **[confounder]** could explain both. Because the data came from **[sample]**, it may not hold for **[other people]**.

Compare "Chocolate makes nations smarter" with "Across 23 countries, chocolate consumption per person and Nobel laureates per person were strongly correlated (r = 0.79). National wealth could explain both, and country averages say nothing about individual chocolate eaters." The second is less exciting and much more likely to be true.

:::quiz
Q: In a study of 200 students, the correlation between hours of sleep and reaction time is r = 0.02. What can you conclude?
- [ ] Sleep has no effect on reaction time
  r only detects straight-line patterns. A curve (too little and too much sleep both slow you down) could give r near 0.
- [x] There's no straight-line relationship in this sample; plot it before saying more
  That's all r = 0.02 tells you. A scatter plot would show whether there's a curved pattern r can't see.
- [ ] More sleep causes slower reactions
  r is nearly zero, and even a large r wouldn't establish cause.

Q: A city finds that neighborhoods with more libraries have lower crime rates. Which is a confounder that could explain both?
- [x] Neighborhood income, which can fund libraries and is linked to lower crime
  A confounder causes both variables. Wealthier areas may have more libraries and less crime for separate reasons.
- [ ] The number of library books checked out
  That's a consequence of having libraries, not a common cause of libraries and crime.
- [ ] The day of the week the data was collected
  That doesn't plausibly cause both how many libraries exist and how much crime there is.

Q: An online poll gets 50,000 votes on whether school should start later; 85% say yes. A random survey of 800 students finds 61%. Which should you trust more?
- [ ] The online poll, because 50,000 is much bigger than 800
  Size reduces random error, not bias. People who feel strongly are far more likely to click on an online poll.
- [x] The random survey, because everyone had an equal chance to be included
  A random sample of 800 is usually within a few points of the truth. The online poll's error comes from who chose to answer, and more votes don't fix that.

Q: A study of famous entrepreneurs finds most of them took big risks early. Why is "taking big risks leads to success" a weak conclusion?
- [ ] The sample of famous entrepreneurs is too small
  Even a large sample of famous entrepreneurs would have the same problem.
- [x] It only looks at people who succeeded; many people who took big risks failed and aren't in the data
  That's survivorship bias. You'd need to know what happened to everyone who took big risks, not just the winners.
- [ ] Entrepreneurs don't report their risks accurately
  Possibly, but the main flaw is who got counted at all.
:::

:::takeaways
- r measures straight-line association from −1 to 1. It misses curves and says nothing about cause.
- A correlation can come from X causing Y, Y causing X, a confounder causing both, or chance.
- Removing a confounder's effect can make a correlation vanish, but only for the confounders you measured.
- Bias doesn't shrink with sample size. A small random sample beats a huge biased one.
- Ask what decided which rows made it into your data. Survivors, volunteers, and responders differ from everyone else.
- Write claims that name the sample, the strength, and the alternative explanations.
:::
