For years, papers on predicting civil war onset reported that random forests and other complex models far outperformed logistic regression. Then Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan reran those papers from their published replication materials. Every claim of superiority failed to reproduce. The gains came from data leakage (one paper imputed missing values in the training and test data together; another used proxies for the outcome), and once that was fixed the complex models did no better than logistic regression ([their write-up](https://reproducible.cs.princeton.edu/)). No new data, no new method, just careful rerunning. That's what a reproduction can do.

Most reproductions are less dramatic: you get a number that's close but not equal and must decide what that means. A documented "we got 88.5%, they reported 91.2%, and here's whether that gap matters" is a contribution. Hiding it, or quietly tuning until the numbers match, is not.

## Four ways to get "the same result"

People use these words loosely, and fields disagree (the ACM once used "reproducible" and "replicable" the opposite way). This course follows *The Turing Way*, which sorts results by whether the data and the analysis are the same as the original:

```mermaid caption="The Turing Way's grid: change the data, the analysis, or both"
flowchart LR
    subgraph same_data [Same data]
        R["Reproducible: same analysis, same answer"]
        B["Robust: different analysis, similar answer"]
    end
    subgraph new_data [Different data]
        P["Replicable: same analysis, similar answer"]
        G["Generalisable: different analysis, similar answer"]
    end
    R --> P
    B --> G
```

- **Reproduction** reruns the same analysis on the same data. With the authors' code, it's computational reproduction; from the paper's description alone, it's reimplementation (much more informative, and much harder).
- **Robustness** keeps the data but changes reasonable choices: another splitter, another library, another seed.
- **Replication** applies the same method to new data, such as a newer survey or another hospital.

A result can reproduce perfectly and still fail to replicate, which usually means the finding was specific to its dataset.

## Choose a result you can actually check

A good target for a student reproduction has five properties:

1. **One specific number** in a table or figure, not "our method works well."
2. **Public data,** ideally the exact version the paper used, with a stated sample size.
3. **A method described well enough to rebuild:** model, features, preprocessing, split, metric.
4. **Compute you have.** A logistic regression on 500 rows: yes. Retraining a model that took 64 GPUs: pick one table row that doesn't need that.
5. **A claim that matters.** If the number were wrong, someone's conclusion would change.

A **partial reproduction** of one well-chosen row beats an unfinished attempt at the whole paper. Write down what you're attempting, and what you aren't, before you start.

## Decide what "reproduced" means before you run anything

If you choose the tolerance after seeing your number, you'll choose one that flatters it. Write it down first. A principled rule uses the uncertainty in *both* numbers: the paper's accuracy came from a finite test set too.

```python run
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_confint, confint_proportions_2indep

def compare_to_claim(claimed, n_paper, my_correct, n_mine):
    """Is a reproduced accuracy consistent with a published one, given both test-set sizes?"""
    paper_correct = round(claimed * n_paper)
    paper_ci = proportion_confint(paper_correct, n_paper, method="wilson")
    my_ci = proportion_confint(my_correct, n_mine, method="wilson")
    gap_ci = confint_proportions_2indep(my_correct, n_mine, paper_correct, n_paper, method="newcomb")
    verdict = "consistent within sampling error" if gap_ci[0] <= 0 <= gap_ci[1] else "a discrepancy to explain"
    print(f"paper {claimed:.3f} [{paper_ci[0]:.3f}, {paper_ci[1]:.3f}]   "
          f"mine {my_correct / n_mine:.3f} [{my_ci[0]:.3f}, {my_ci[1]:.3f}]")
    print(f"  gap {my_correct / n_mine - claimed:+.3f}, 95% CI [{gap_ci[0]:+.3f}, {gap_ci[1]:+.3f}] -> {verdict}")

compare_to_claim(0.912, n_paper=400, my_correct=354, n_mine=400)
compare_to_claim(0.950, n_paper=2000, my_correct=1790, n_mine=2000)
```

The **Wilson interval** is a better-behaved CI for a proportion than ± 2 SE, especially near 0 or 1. The **Newcombe interval** combines two Wilson intervals into one for the difference. In the first case, a 2.7-point shortfall on 400 images is well within what two independent test sets could produce. In the second, 5.5 points on 2,000 images is not; something about the method or data differs, and your job is to find out what.

Two cautions. The function assumes independent test sets; if you used the paper's exact split, the comparison should be paired, which needs their per-example predictions. And "consistent" isn't "confirmed": it only means this test can't tell the numbers apart. Say how wide the interval was.

## Keep a discrepancy log

Every difference between your setup and the paper's goes in a table *as you find it*, including ones you think don't matter:

| # | Difference | Paper | Reproduction | Expected effect | Tested? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Library | MATLAB 2015 | scikit-learn 1.8 | small | no |
| 2 | Scaling | "normalized" (unspecified) | z-score inside each fold | unknown | yes: tried min-max too |
| 3 | Data version | 13 variables | 13 variables, documented in dataset notes | none | n/a |
| 4 | Split seed | not reported | 10 seeds, reported mean ± SD | none on mean | yes |

The "Tested?" column is the discipline. If you try three scaling variants, report all three and which one you'd defend in advance, not just the closest to the paper. Hunting through variants until one matches is the forking-paths problem from Module 6 wearing a lab coat.

## Report with a checklist, not from memory

The **REFORMS checklist** (Kapoor and colleagues, *Science Advances*, 2024) was written by 19 researchers from computer science, data science, mathematics, the social sciences, and biomedical sciences, specifically for ML-based science. It has 32 items in 8 sections: study design, computational reproducibility, data quality, data preprocessing, modeling, data leakage, metrics and uncertainty quantification, and generalizability and limitations. Fill it in while designing the study: items such as "what population is the claim about?" are easier to answer honestly before the data shape your thinking. Attach it as a supplement. Clinical prediction models have their own standard, TRIPOD+AI, and many ML venues require a paper checklist at submission.

Two parts of the write-up deserve special care.

**Methods** should let a stranger rerun you. Compare:

```text
Vague:    Data were normalized and a random forest was trained and evaluated with cross-validation.
Checkable: Features were z-scored using means and SDs from each training fold. A random forest
           (scikit-learn 1.8, 60 trees, min_samples_leaf=3) was evaluated with 4-fold
           StratifiedGroupKFold grouped by farm, repeated over 4 seeds; we report mean ± SD
           across seeds. Code and the exact package versions are in the linked repository.
```

**Limitations** should name specific threats and their likely direction, not apologize in general. "Our sample may not be representative" says nothing. "All 16 farms are in one state's corn belt, so accuracy on sandy coastal soils is untested and likely lower" tells a reader where the claim stops.

## Figures that don't mislead

The most common misleading ML figure is a bar chart of three accuracies with the y-axis starting just below the smallest one. Bars are read by length, so a 2-point difference looks like a tripling. A dot plot with intervals shows the same numbers honestly:

```python run alt="Two panels showing the same three accuracies, 0.912, 0.918, and 0.931. Left: bars on an axis from 0.905 to 0.935, so the third bar looks about three times taller than the first. Right: dots with 95% Wilson intervals on an axis from 0.85 to 0.97; all three intervals overlap heavily." caption="Same numbers. Left: truncated bars exaggerate. Right: dots with intervals."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

models = ["baseline", "method A", "method B"]
correct = np.array([365, 367, 372])
n_test = 400
acc = correct / n_test
ci = np.array([proportion_confint(c, n_test, method="wilson") for c in correct])

fig, (bad, good) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3))
bad.bar(models, acc, color=["#999999", "#4c72b0", "#c44e52"])
bad.set_ylim(0.905, 0.935)
bad.set_title("Truncated bars")
bad.set_ylabel("test accuracy")

good.errorbar(models, acc, yerr=[acc - ci[:, 0], ci[:, 1] - acc], fmt="o", capsize=5, color="k")
good.set_ylim(0.85, 0.97)
good.set_title("Dots with 95% Wilson intervals, n = 400")
fig.tight_layout()
```

The rules: start bar axes at zero, or use dots when they can't; draw and label intervals; put compared conditions on one axis; and with only a few observations per group, plot them all instead of a bar.

:::warning
**Tuning the reproduction until it matches.** If the first faithful attempt gives 88.5% and you then try twelve variants and report the one that hits 91.2%, you haven't reproduced anything; you've found one path among many that lands near the target. Pre-register the main attempt and its tolerance, log every variant, and report the faithful attempt as the primary result.
:::

:::quiz
Q: You rerun a paper's code on its own dataset and get the same table. Then you apply the same code to next year's data and the effect disappears. How should you describe the original result?
- [x] Reproducible but not replicated
  Same data and analysis gave the same answer; new data with the same analysis did not.
- [ ] Robust but not reproducible
  Robustness is about changing the analysis on the same data, which you didn't do.
- [ ] Not reproducible
  You did reproduce it; the failure came when the data changed.

Q: A paper reports 0.950 on 2,000 test images. Your faithful reimplementation gets 0.895 on a fresh 2,000-image split, and the Newcombe interval for the gap is −0.072 to −0.039. What do you write?
- [ ] "Consistent with the original within error"
  The gap's interval excludes zero by a wide margin.
- [x] "A 5.5-point shortfall that sampling error doesn't explain," then the discrepancy log and what you tested
  The gap is real for this setup, so the useful contribution is narrowing down why.
- [ ] Nothing until you find settings that reach 0.95
  Searching for a matching variant turns the reproduction into a forking-paths exercise.

Q: Which methods sentence lets someone else rerun the evaluation?
- [ ] "Models were evaluated with cross-validation."
  It gives no splitter, number of folds, grouping, seeds, or summary statistic.
- [x] "5-fold StratifiedGroupKFold by patient, 10 seeds; we report mean ± SD across seeds."
  Every choice that changes the number is stated.
- [ ] "We followed standard best practices for evaluation."
  "Standard" means different things in every field; it can't be checked.
:::

:::takeaways
- Reproduction (same data and analysis), robustness (same data, new analysis), and replication (new data) answer different questions; say which one you did.
- Choose one specific, checkable number with public data and feasible compute; a careful partial reproduction is a real result.
- Fix the tolerance before running, using intervals for both the paper's number and yours; a Newcombe interval for the gap that excludes zero means there's something to explain.
- Log every difference and every variant you try; report the pre-specified attempt as primary.
- Fill in REFORMS while designing the study, write methods someone can rerun, name specific limitations, and draw dots with intervals instead of truncated bars.
:::

- [REFORMS: Consensus-based Recommendations for Machine-learning-based Science](https://reforms.cs.princeton.edu/): the checklist, its guidelines, and a fillable template; the [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07832) is free to read.
