In 2018, a team at Mount Sinai trained a neural network to detect pneumonia in chest X-rays from two hospital systems, and it scored an AUC of 0.93 on held-out images. Then they asked a stranger question: could the network tell *which hospital* an X-ray came from? It could, for 99.95% of the National Institutes of Health images and 99.98% of Mount Sinai's. That mattered because pneumonia made up 34.2% of the Mount Sinai images and 1.2% of the NIH ones. Simply ranking images by hospital, with no medicine at all, scored an AUC of 0.861. On X-rays from a third hospital system the model's AUC fell to 0.815. ([Zech et al., 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1002683).)

Nothing in the code was wrong. The data carried a fingerprint of where it came from, the label was tied to that fingerprint, and the model used it. Auditing data means finding those fingerprints before your model does.

## Provenance: questions to ask before you train anything

Provenance is the history of a dataset: who collected it, how, and what happened to it since. Ask these before downloading, and record the answers in a file next to the data:

- **Collection.** Which instruments, sites, dates, and protocols? Were any changed partway through?
- **Selection.** Who or what was included, and who was left out? Volunteers, a single clinic, only the samples that passed quality control?
- **Labels.** Who assigned them, using what criteria, and blind to what? Labels from a later test, an expert panel, or text-mined reports differ in quality.
- **Assembly.** Is this one study, or several datasets merged? Do the same subjects appear more than once?
- **Processing and license.** What was filtered, normalized, or resized before you got it, and are you allowed to use it this way?

The last bullet of "assembly" sank a whole literature. A systematic review of COVID-19 imaging models screened 2,212 studies, kept 62 for detailed review, and judged none of them of potential clinical use ([Roberts et al., 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-021-00307-0)). One recurring problem was "Frankenstein datasets", public collections stitched together from other collections, sometimes containing the same images twice. Another was using a pediatric pneumonia dataset as the "not COVID" group, so a model could score well by recognizing children.

## Missing values have a pattern, and the pattern is information

Statisticians sort missingness into three kinds. **MCAR** (missing completely at random): a vial got dropped, unrelated to anything. **MAR** (missing at random): missingness depends on something you *did* record, like a logger that fails in cold weather when you logged the temperature elsewhere. **MNAR** (missing not at random): missingness depends on the missing value itself, like a sensor that maxes out and records nothing when readings are highest.

This simulated water-quality dataset has the second and third kinds. The turbidity sensor saturates above 16 NTU and reports nothing, and the lake station's thermometer fails in cold water.

```python run title="water.py"
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n = 600
site = rng.choice(["river", "lake", "reservoir"], n)
temp = rng.normal(18, 6, n)
turbidity = rng.lognormal(2.2, 0.6, n)
bloom = (0.12 * turbidity + 0.15 * (temp - 18) + rng.normal(0, 1, n)) > 1.6   # algal bloom yes/no

water = pd.DataFrame({"site": site, "temp_c": temp.round(1),
                      "turbidity": turbidity.round(1), "bloom": bloom})
water.loc[water["turbidity"] > 16, "turbidity"] = np.nan                     # sensor saturates: MNAR
cold_lake = (water["site"] == "lake") & (water["temp_c"] < 12)
water.loc[cold_lake & (rng.random(n) < 0.8), "temp_c"] = np.nan              # cold logger failure: MAR

print(water.groupby("site")[["temp_c", "turbidity"]].apply(lambda d: d.isna().mean()).round(2))
print("\nbloom rate when turbidity is missing vs present:")
print(water.groupby(water["turbidity"].isna())["bloom"].mean().round(2))
print(f"\nbloom rate, all rows: {water['bloom'].mean():.3f}   complete rows only: {water.dropna()['bloom'].mean():.3f}")
```

Two things stand out. Temperature is missing only at the lake, which a per-site table shows in one line and an overall percentage hides. And samples with missing turbidity bloomed 70% of the time, against 38% for the rest. Dropping incomplete rows, the default in many tutorials, throws away the samples most likely to bloom, and the bloom rate in what's left falls from 0.435 to 0.395. A model trained on complete rows learns from a world with fewer blooms than the real one. Always tabulate missingness by site, by batch, and by outcome before deciding what to do with it.

## Batch effects: when the instrument leaves a fingerprint

A **batch** is any group of samples processed together: one plate, one sequencing run, one day, one technician. Batches differ in small systematic ways, and with dozens of measured features those small differences add up to a pattern a model can find. Here are 90 samples of 20 gene-expression measurements processed in three runs, with no biological difference between runs at all.

```python run alt="Scatter plot of 90 samples on the first two principal components; the samples form three separate clusters, one per processing run" caption="PCA of samples with no biological differences: they still cluster by processing run"
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

genes = [f"gene_{i:02d}" for i in range(20)]
runs = []
for run, offset in zip(["run 1", "run 2", "run 3"], [0.0, 0.8, -0.6]):
    run_shift = rng.normal(offset, 0.3, len(genes))          # each run nudges every gene a little
    values = rng.normal(0, 1, (30, len(genes))) + run_shift
    runs.append(pd.DataFrame(values, columns=genes).assign(run=run))
expression = pd.concat(runs, ignore_index=True)

pcs = PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(expression[genes])
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4))
for (run, group), marker in zip(expression.groupby("run"), ["o", "s", "^"]):
    ax.scatter(pcs[group.index, 0], pcs[group.index, 1], marker=marker, s=24, label=run)
ax.set_xlabel("PC 1")
ax.set_ylabel("PC 2")
ax.legend(frameon=False)
```

PCA finds the directions along which samples vary most, and the biggest one here is the processing run. A second test makes the same point numerically: if a classifier can predict the batch from the features far better than chance, the batch is in your data.

```python run title="predict_batch.py"
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

accuracy = cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000), expression[genes], expression["run"], cv=5).mean()
print(f"predicting the run from gene values: {accuracy:.2f} accuracy (chance = 0.33)")
```

A batch effect on its own adds noise. It becomes a disaster when the batch lines up with the thing you're studying, as the hospital did with pneumonia in the X-ray study.

## A confounded design can't be rescued afterwards

Suppose you process all 12 treated samples on Monday and all 12 controls on Tuesday. Monday's run reads 1.5 units high. The true treatment effect is 1.0.

```python run title="design.py"
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

def experiment(balanced, seed=1):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    rows = []
    for i in range(24):
        day = i // 12                             # day 0 or day 1, 12 samples each
        treated = i % 2 if balanced else day      # balanced: alternate within each day
        value = 10 + 1.0 * treated + 1.5 * day + r.normal(0, 1)
        rows.append({"day": day, "treated": treated, "value": value})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

confounded = experiment(balanced=False)
balanced = experiment(balanced=True)
print("treated and day always agree in the confounded design:", (confounded["treated"] == confounded["day"]).all())
for name, data, formula in [("confounded", confounded, "value ~ treated"),
                            ("balanced", balanced, "value ~ treated + C(day)")]:
    fit = smf.ols(formula, data=data).fit()
    low, high = fit.conf_int().loc["treated"]
    print(f"{name:<11} effect = {fit.params['treated']:.2f}  (95% CI {low:.2f} to {high:.2f})")
```

The confounded design reports an effect of about 2.3, which is the treatment and Monday's offset welded together. No statistical method can pull them apart, because every treated sample is also a Monday sample, so there's no data on treated Tuesday samples at all. The balanced design, with treatment split evenly within each day, recovers an effect near 1.0 and lets you adjust for day.

```mermaid caption="Design decides whether batch can be separated from treatment"
flowchart LR
    T["Treatment"] --> Y["Measurement"]
    B["Batch or day"] --> Y
    B -. "confounded design: batch picks treatment" .-> T
    R["Randomize within each batch"] --> T
```

That's the case for three habits from experimental design. **Randomize** which samples get which treatment, using code with a seed, not judgment. **Block** by batch: balance treatments within each plate, day, or site, so batch can be estimated and removed. **Include controls**: negative controls (no treatment), positive controls (a known effect, to show the assay can detect one), and if possible the same reference sample on every plate, which measures each batch's offset directly.

## How many samples do you need?

Before running an experiment, ask how many samples it takes to detect the effect you care about. A **power analysis** answers it from three inputs: the effect size (Cohen's *d*), the false-positive rate you'll accept (alpha, usually 0.05), and the chance of detecting a real effect that you want (power, usually 0.8).

```python run title="power.py"
from statsmodels.stats.power import TTestIndPower

analysis = TTestIndPower()
for d in [0.2, 0.5, 0.8]:
    n_per_group = analysis.solve_power(effect_size=d, alpha=0.05, power=0.8)
    print(f"d = {d}: {np.ceil(n_per_group):.0f} samples per group")
print(f"power with 20 per group at d = 0.5: {analysis.solve_power(effect_size=0.5, nobs1=20, alpha=0.05):.2f}")
```

`solve_power` solves for whichever argument you leave out. Halving the effect size roughly quadruples the samples needed. And a typical student-sized experiment, 20 per group, has about a one-in-three chance of detecting a medium effect, so a "no significant difference" from it means very little. The effect size you plug in should come from a pilot study or the literature, never from wishful thinking.

:::warning
**Two audit mistakes that look like rigor.** First, "correcting" batch effects in a confounded design: tools like ComBat estimate each batch's offset, and when batch equals treatment they remove the treatment effect along with it. Second, counting rows instead of independent units. Five photos of each of 20 plants is 20 plants, not 100 samples; treating them as 100 makes intervals too narrow and lets the same plant appear in both training and test sets.
:::

:::quiz
Q: A model trained on X-rays from two hospitals scores well internally but poorly at a third hospital. The model can also identify the hospital an image came from. What's the most likely explanation?
- [x] Disease prevalence differed between the hospitals, so recognizing the hospital was a shortcut to predicting the label
  That's the pattern Zech et al. found: hospital alone gave an AUC of 0.861 because prevalence was 34.2% at one site and 1.2% at the other.
- [ ] The third hospital's images were too low resolution
  Possible in general, but it doesn't explain why the model can identify hospitals so well.
- [ ] The model was trained for too few epochs
  Undertraining lowers internal scores too; here internal scores were high.

Q: A soil moisture probe stops recording when the soil is waterlogged. What kind of missingness is that, and what does dropping those rows do?
- [ ] MCAR; dropping rows just loses a little precision
  The missingness depends on the moisture itself, so it isn't random.
- [x] MNAR; dropping the rows removes the wettest conditions and biases any estimate toward drier soil
  When the missing value causes the missingness, complete-case analysis distorts the picture.
- [ ] MAR; adjusting for site fixes it
  Site doesn't explain it. The missing value itself does.

Q: All treated cell cultures were imaged on microscope A and all controls on microscope B. What can analysis do about it?
- [ ] Add microscope as a covariate in the regression
  Microscope and treatment are identical columns, so the model can't estimate both.
- [ ] Apply batch correction to remove the microscope effect
  Correction would remove the treatment effect too, since they're indistinguishable.
- [x] Nothing reliable; the experiment needs to be redone with treatments split across both microscopes
  Confounding by design is fixed by design: randomize and block.

Q: Your pilot suggests d = 0.4. Roughly how does the required sample size compare with d = 0.8?
- [ ] About the same
  Smaller effects need many more samples.
- [ ] About twice as many
  Sample size scales with one over d squared, not one over d.
- [x] About four times as many
  Halving d roughly quadruples n: about 100 per group instead of 26.
:::

:::takeaways
- Record provenance (collection, selection, labels, assembly, processing, license) before training on any dataset.
- Tabulate missingness by site, batch, and outcome; MNAR data dropped as "incomplete" biases what's left.
- Check for batch effects with PCA and by trying to predict the batch from the features.
- A design where batch lines up with treatment can't be fixed by analysis. Randomize, block by batch, and include controls.
- Run a power analysis before collecting data; small experiments often can't detect the effects they're looking for.
:::
