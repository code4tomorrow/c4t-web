---
title: Mark up a recipe page with landmarks, lists, and a table
summary: Build a complete recipe page with a header and navigation, an article with a captioned photo, an at-a-glance table, ingredient and method lists, a tips aside, and a footer.
---

:::objectives
- Lay out a whole page with `<header>`, `<nav>`, `<main>`, `<article>`, `<section>`, `<aside>`, and `<footer>`.
- Put navigation links in a list, and link them to sections with `id`s.
- Caption a photo with `<figure>` and `<figcaption>`, including the credit its license asks for.
- Use a table for summary data, `<ul>` for ingredients, and `<ol>` for steps.
- Check the structure with the browser's accessibility tools.
:::

Recipe sites are a perfect semantic HTML workout: they have a site header, a menu, a main article, grouped lists, numbered steps, a little data table, and side tips. Here's the page you'll build, still with no CSS of your own:

```html preview height="640" title="recipe/index.html (finished)"
<header>
  <p><strong>Weekend Kitchen</strong></p>
  <nav>
    <ul>
      <li><a href="#ingredients">Ingredients</a></li>
      <li><a href="#method">Method</a></li>
      <li><a href="#tips">Tips</a></li>
    </ul>
  </nav>
</header>

<main>
  <article>
    <h1>Fluffy blueberry pancakes</h1>
    <p>Thick, soft pancakes with blueberries in every bite. About 25 minutes from bowl to plate, and the batter forgives lumps.</p>

    <figure>
      <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Blueberry_pancakes_%283%29.jpg/330px-Blueberry_pancakes_%283%29.jpg" alt="A stack of golden pancakes dotted with blueberries and dusted with powdered sugar" width="330" height="221">
      <figcaption>Photo: Janine from Mililani, Hawaii (CC BY 2.0), via Wikimedia Commons</figcaption>
    </figure>

    <table>
      <caption>At a glance</caption>
      <thead>
        <tr><th scope="col">Prep</th><th scope="col">Cook</th><th scope="col">Makes</th></tr>
      </thead>
      <tbody>
        <tr><td>10 minutes</td><td>15 minutes</td><td>About 10 pancakes</td></tr>
      </tbody>
    </table>

    <section id="ingredients">
      <h2>Ingredients</h2>
      <h3>Dry</h3>
      <ul>
        <li>1½ cups (190 g) all-purpose flour</li>
        <li>2 tablespoons sugar</li>
        <li>1 tablespoon baking powder</li>
        <li>½ teaspoon salt</li>
      </ul>
      <h3>Wet</h3>
      <ul>
        <li>1¼ cups (300 ml) milk</li>
        <li>1 egg</li>
        <li>3 tablespoons melted butter, plus a little for the pan</li>
      </ul>
      <h3>To finish</h3>
      <ul>
        <li>1 cup (150 g) blueberries, fresh or frozen</li>
      </ul>
    </section>

    <section id="method">
      <h2>Method</h2>
      <ol>
        <li>Whisk the dry ingredients together in a large bowl.</li>
        <li>In a second bowl, whisk the milk, egg, and melted butter.</li>
        <li>Pour the wet mix into the dry one and stir <strong>just until no dry flour shows</strong>. Lumps are fine.</li>
        <li>Let the batter rest for 5 minutes while a pan heats over medium heat.</li>
        <li>Grease the pan, pour in ¼ cup of batter, and scatter a few blueberries on top.</li>
        <li>Flip when bubbles pop on the surface and the edges look dry, after about 2 minutes. Cook 1 to 2 minutes more.</li>
      </ol>
    </section>

    <aside id="tips">
      <h2>Tips</h2>
      <p>Add frozen blueberries straight from the freezer. Thawed ones bleed and turn the batter purple.</p>
    </aside>
  </article>
</main>

<footer>
  <p>Weekend Kitchen is a student cooking project. Ask an adult before using the stove if you're new to it.</p>
</footer>
```

Scroll inside the result to see all of it, and click the three menu links: each one jumps to its section.

## Step 1: Set up the project

Make a new project folder:

```text
recipe/
├── index.html
└── images/
    └── pancakes.jpg
```

Download the photo from [its Wikimedia Commons page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Blueberry_pancakes_%283%29.jpg), save it into `images/`, and rename it `pancakes.jpg`. Open the `recipe` folder in VS Code, create `index.html`, type `!` and press Enter, and set the title to `Fluffy blueberry pancakes | Weekend Kitchen`. Then click **Go Live**.

## Step 2: Sketch the landmarks first

Before writing any content, lay out the regions inside `<body>`. Comments mark where everything will go:

```html
<header>
  <!-- site name and menu -->
</header>

<main>
  <article>
    <!-- the recipe: title, photo, table, ingredients, method, tips -->
  </article>
</main>

<footer>
  <!-- credits -->
</footer>
```

The recipe is an `<article>` because it would make sense on its own, say, shared to another cooking site. The `<header>` and `<footer>` belong to the whole site, so they sit *outside* `<main>`. On a real site they'd repeat on every page, while `<main>` changes.

## Step 3: The header and its navigation

```html
<header>
  <p><strong>Weekend Kitchen</strong></p>
  <nav>
    <ul>
      <li><a href="#ingredients">Ingredients</a></li>
      <li><a href="#method">Method</a></li>
      <li><a href="#tips">Tips</a></li>
    </ul>
  </nav>
</header>
```

The site name is a `<p>`, not a heading. The page's `<h1>` should name what *this* page is about, the pancakes, and the site name is the same on every page.

The menu is a `<ul>` of links inside `<nav>`. The order of a menu doesn't change its meaning, so it's unordered. Right now it shows as a bulleted list; in Module 5 you'll turn lists like this into a row of links with CSS. The links point at `id`s you'll add in Steps 6 to 8, so they won't jump anywhere until then.

## Step 4: Title, introduction, and a captioned photo

Inside `<article>`:

```html
<h1>Fluffy blueberry pancakes</h1>
<p>Thick, soft pancakes with blueberries in every bite. About 25 minutes from bowl to plate, and the batter forgives lumps.</p>

<figure>
  <img src="images/pancakes.jpg" alt="A stack of golden pancakes dotted with blueberries and dusted with powdered sugar" width="330" height="221">
  <figcaption>Photo: Janine from Mililani, Hawaii (CC BY 2.0), via Wikimedia Commons</figcaption>
</figure>
```

`<figure>` groups an image (or a chart, or a code sample) with its caption, and `<figcaption>` is that caption. Screen readers connect the two.

The caption also does a legal job. This photo's license, **CC BY 2.0**, lets anyone use it for free *as long as they credit the photographer*. Whenever you use someone else's image, check its license and give the credit it asks for.

## Step 5: An at-a-glance table

Prep time, cook time, and yield are a tiny grid of data, with headings and values, so they belong in a table. Add this after the figure:

```html preview height="150"
<table>
  <caption>At a glance</caption>
  <thead>
    <tr><th scope="col">Prep</th><th scope="col">Cook</th><th scope="col">Makes</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>10 minutes</td><td>15 minutes</td><td>About 10 pancakes</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
```

Without CSS the table has no borders, and that's fine. The header cells are bold and centered by default, and the structure is all there for when you style it in Module 4.

## Step 6: Ingredients as grouped lists

```html preview height="400"
<section id="ingredients">
  <h2>Ingredients</h2>
  <h3>Dry</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>1½ cups (190 g) all-purpose flour</li>
    <li>2 tablespoons sugar</li>
    <li>1 tablespoon baking powder</li>
    <li>½ teaspoon salt</li>
  </ul>
  <h3>Wet</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>1¼ cups (300 ml) milk</li>
    <li>1 egg</li>
    <li>3 tablespoons melted butter, plus a little for the pan</li>
  </ul>
  <h3>To finish</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>1 cup (150 g) blueberries, fresh or frozen</li>
  </ul>
</section>
```

Each group gets an `<h3>` heading and its own `<ul>`, because within a group you can gather things in any order. The whole block is a `<section>`: a themed part of the article that starts with a heading. Its `id="ingredients"` is what the menu link jumps to.

The fractions `½` and `¼` are real characters, typed or pasted straight in. They display correctly because the skeleton has `<meta charset="UTF-8">`.

## Step 7: The method as a numbered list

Steps only work in order, so this is an `<ol>`. Add it after the ingredients section:

```html
<section id="method">
  <h2>Method</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Whisk the dry ingredients together in a large bowl.</li>
    <li>In a second bowl, whisk the milk, egg, and melted butter.</li>
    <li>Pour the wet mix into the dry one and stir <strong>just until no dry flour shows</strong>. Lumps are fine.</li>
    <li>Let the batter rest for 5 minutes while a pan heats over medium heat.</li>
    <li>Grease the pan, pour in ¼ cup of batter, and scatter a few blueberries on top.</li>
    <li>Flip when bubbles pop on the surface and the edges look dry, after about 2 minutes. Cook 1 to 2 minutes more.</li>
  </ol>
</section>
```

You never type the numbers. If you add a step in the middle later, every step after it renumbers itself.

`<strong>` marks the one instruction people most often get wrong (over-mixing makes tough pancakes). That's importance, so `<strong>` is the right element rather than just "bold".

## Step 8: Tips and the footer

The tip is useful but not part of the main instructions, which makes it an `<aside>`. Put it at the end of the article, then fill in the footer:

```html
    <aside id="tips">
      <h2>Tips</h2>
      <p>Add frozen blueberries straight from the freezer. Thawed ones bleed and turn the batter purple.</p>
    </aside>
  </article>
</main>

<footer>
  <p>Weekend Kitchen is a student cooking project. Ask an adult before using the stove if you're new to it.</p>
</footer>
```

Save, and click each menu link in your browser. All three should jump now.

## Step 9: Check the structure the way assistive tech sees it

Browsers build an **accessibility tree** alongside the DOM: a simpler version listing each element's role and name, which is what screen readers read.

-   **Chrome:** in the Elements panel, select `<main>`, then open the **Accessibility** tab in the side pane (next to Styles and Computed; it may be behind the `»` button). Under **Computed Properties** you'll see its role is `main`. Select `<nav>` and you'll see `navigation`.
-   **Firefox:** right-click the page and choose **Inspect Accessibility Properties** to open the **Accessibility** panel, which lists every element's role.

Then paste the file into the [W3C validator](https://validator.w3.org/#validate_by_input). It catches a missing `</li>` or a `<li>` placed outside a list.

What to watch for: Kevin Powell compares `<ol>` and `<ul>` and nests one list inside another. Notice that the inner list always sits inside an `<li>`.
:::

:::quiz
Q: Why is the site name "Weekend Kitchen" a `<p>` instead of the `<h1>`?
- [ ] Because headings aren't allowed inside `<header>`
  They are allowed. The reason is about what the page is about.
- [x] Because the `<h1>` should name this page's topic, the pancakes, not the site
  The site name repeats on every page, while each page's `<h1>` says what makes that page different.
- [ ] Because `<p>` text is smaller
  Size is never the reason. CSS can make a `<p>` as large as you like.

Q: Why are the ingredients in `<ul>`s but the method in an `<ol>`?
- [x] You can gather ingredients in any order, but the steps only work in sequence
  `<ol>` says "the order matters", and the numbers help a cook keep their place.
- [ ] `<ol>` can only be used once per page
  You can use as many ordered lists as you need.
- [ ] Ingredients need bullets for decoration
  The choice is about meaning. CSS can change or remove the bullets and numbers.

Q: Why does the tip go in an `<aside>` rather than another `<section>`?
- [ ] Because `<aside>` puts content in a sidebar automatically
  Without CSS, an aside appears in the normal flow like everything else. The element describes meaning, not position.
- [x] Because it's related extra advice, not part of the core recipe
  `<aside>` marks content that's connected to its surroundings but could be skipped without losing the main point.
- [ ] Because a page can have only two sections
  There's no limit on sections.
:::

:::takeaways
- Sketch the landmarks first: site `<header>` and `<footer>` outside `<main>`, the page's real content inside it.
- A self-contained piece like a recipe is an `<article>`. Its themed parts are `<section>`s that start with headings.
- Menus are lists of links inside `<nav>`, and `href="#id"` links can target sections.
- `<figure>` and `<figcaption>` keep an image and its caption together, and the caption is a good home for a license credit.
- Use a table only for data in rows and columns, `<ul>` when order doesn't matter, and `<ol>` when it does.
:::

## Ideas to extend it

-   Add a "Rate this recipe" form at the end of the article: a `<fieldset>` with the legend "How were they?" and five radio buttons sharing `name="rating"`, each with its own label.
-   Add a nutrition table with nutrient names as row headers (`<th scope="row">`) and amounts per pancake as data.
-   Make a second recipe page, add a "Recipes" link to the menu on both pages, and link them to each other.
