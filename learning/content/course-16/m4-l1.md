A friend moves to your city and asks which pizza place they'll like. You could study cheese chemistry, or you could think of the three people you know whose taste is most like your friend's and recommend whatever most of them like. That second plan is **k-nearest neighbors**, and you already used it in Module 1 without opening it up.

Now your friend asks how long their bike commute will be. Nearby examples would work here too, but there's another way: draw the straight line that best fits past trips (distance against minutes) and read the answer off the line. That's **linear regression**. This lesson takes both ideas apart.

## k-NN: let the closest examples vote

To predict for a new example, k-nearest neighbors does three things:

1. Measure the distance from the new example to every training example.
2. Keep the **k** closest.
3. For classification, take a majority vote of their labels. For regression, average their values.

That's the whole algorithm. Calling `fit` just stores the training data (organized for fast searching); the real work happens at `predict` time. "Distance" is ordinary straight-line distance: for two features, it's Pythagoras, the square root of (difference in x)² + (difference in y)².

Here are eight labeled points and one new one. Compute every distance, keep the three smallest, and vote:

```python run
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter

points = np.array([[1, 1], [2, 1], [1, 3], [3, 2], [5, 4], [6, 5], [5, 6], [6, 3]])
labels = np.array(["red", "red", "red", "red", "blue", "blue", "blue", "blue"])
new = np.array([4, 3])

distances = np.sqrt(((points - new) ** 2).sum(axis=1))
print("distances:", distances.round(2))

nearest = np.argsort(distances)[:3]
print("3 nearest points:", points[nearest].tolist(), labels[nearest].tolist())
print("vote:", Counter(labels[nearest]).most_common(1)[0][0])
```

`np.argsort` returns the positions of the distances from smallest to largest, so `[:3]` picks the three nearest. One is red and two are blue, so blue wins. Notice the two closest points are tied at 1.41, one of each color: with k = 2 the vote would be a tie, which is why odd values of k are popular.

scikit-learn reaches the same answer, and its `kneighbors` method shows you which neighbors it used:

```python run
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

knn = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=3).fit(points, labels)
print(knn.predict([new]))
dist, index = knn.kneighbors([new])
print("neighbor positions:", index[0].tolist(), " distances:", dist[0].round(2).tolist())
```

The same three positions, the same distances. (Ties between equal distances can be broken differently, so on other data the order may vary.)

## How k changes the boundary

The **decision boundary** is the line where the model's answer flips from one class to the other. To see it, color every spot on a grid by what the model would predict there. Here are 300 points in two interleaved crescents, with some noise, and three choices of k:

```python run alt="Three side-by-side plots of two interleaved crescent-shaped groups of points. With k equals 1 the boundary is jagged, with small islands of color around single points that sit inside the other group. With k equals 15 the boundary is a smooth S-shaped curve between the crescents. With k equals 100 the boundary is nearly a straight diagonal that cuts through both crescents." caption="Small k follows every noisy point; large k smooths away the real shape."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.inspection import DecisionBoundaryDisplay
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X_moons, y_moons = make_moons(n_samples=300, noise=0.35, random_state=0)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X_moons, y_moons, random_state=0, stratify=y_moons)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 3.8))
for ax, k in zip(axes, [1, 15, 100]):
    model = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k).fit(X_train, y_train)
    DecisionBoundaryDisplay.from_estimator(
        model, X_moons, response_method="predict", grid_resolution=300, cmap="coolwarm", alpha=0.3, ax=ax
    )
    ax.scatter(X_train[:, 0], X_train[:, 1], c=y_train, cmap="coolwarm", s=12, edgecolor="k", linewidth=0.3)
    ax.set_title(f"k = {k}")
    print(f"k = {k:3d}  train accuracy {model.score(X_train, y_train):.3f}  test accuracy {model.score(X_test, y_test):.3f}")
fig.tight_layout()
```

Read the printout next to the pictures:

- **k = 1** has 100% training accuracy, as you saw in Module 3: every point is its own nearest neighbor. The boundary wraps around individual noisy points, making little islands that won't hold up on new data.
- **k = 15** draws a smooth S-curve that follows the real crescents. It makes some training mistakes on purpose, and it scores best on the test set.
- **k = 100** averages over nearly half of the 225 training points for every prediction, so the boundary is too stiff to follow the curve, and both scores drop.

Small k is too sensitive to noise; large k is too blunt. The best k lies somewhere in between, and the only way to find it is to try several and compare them on data the model didn't train on (you'll do that in the practice).

## Predicting numbers with a line

k-NN can predict numbers too (`KNeighborsRegressor` averages the neighbors' values). But when the target rises or falls steadily with a feature, a straight line describes that more simply. Take the diabetes data again, this time with the real, unscaled body mass index:

```python run
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

diabetes = load_diabetes(as_frame=True, scaled=False)
bmi = diabetes.data[["bmi"]]
progression = diabetes.target

line = LinearRegression().fit(bmi, progression)
slope = line.coef_[0]
intercept = line.intercept_
print(f"progression = {slope:.2f} * bmi {intercept:+.2f}")
print("prediction for bmi 30:", round(line.predict(pd.DataFrame({"bmi": [30]}))[0], 1))
```

A line has two numbers. The **slope** (`coef_`) says how much the prediction rises for each extra unit of the feature: about 10 progression points per BMI point. The **intercept** (`intercept_`) is where the line would cross zero BMI, which no living person has, so read it as "the number that puts the line in the right place", not as a fact about patients.

## Residuals: how wrong the line is, point by point

For each patient, the **residual** is the actual value minus the line's prediction. Positive means the line guessed too low; negative means too high. Here they are as vertical gaps, drawn for 60 patients so the picture stays readable:

```python run alt="Scatter plot of BMI against disease progression for 60 patients, with a red regression line rising from left to right and a thin gray vertical segment connecting each point to the line." caption="Each gray segment is one residual. Linear regression makes the squares of these gaps as small as possible."
sample = diabetes.data.sample(60, random_state=1).index
x_s, y_s = bmi.loc[sample, "bmi"], progression.loc[sample]
predicted_s = slope * x_s + intercept

plt.figure(figsize=(6.5, 4.2))
plt.vlines(x_s, predicted_s, y_s, color="gray", linewidth=0.8)
plt.scatter(x_s, y_s, s=16, zorder=3)
grid = np.linspace(18, 42, 2)
plt.plot(grid, slope * grid + intercept, color="crimson", linewidth=2)
plt.xlabel("body mass index")
plt.ylabel("disease progression")
plt.title("A fitted line and its residuals")
```

To score the whole line with one number, square every residual and average them. That's the **mean squared error** (MSE), the line's **loss**:

```python run
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error

residuals = progression - line.predict(bmi)
print("first 5 residuals:", residuals.head().round(1).tolist())
print("MSE by hand:      ", round((residuals ** 2).mean(), 1))
print("MSE from sklearn: ", round(mean_squared_error(progression, line.predict(bmi)), 1))
```

Squaring does two jobs: it makes every miss count as positive (so misses above and below don't cancel out), and it punishes one big miss more than several small ones. "Fitting a line" means finding the slope and intercept with the smallest possible MSE. No other line through this data has a lower one.

## How a model finds the best line: gradient descent

`LinearRegression` finds the best line with an exact formula. Most models, including the neural networks behind image and language tools, have no such formula. They learn with **gradient descent**, and a line is the clearest place to see how it works.

Here's the intuition. Picture the loss as a hilly landscape, where every spot is one possible line and the height is its MSE. You're standing somewhere in fog and want to reach the bottom. You can't see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, and repeat. Steep ground means you're far from the bottom, so take a big step; flat ground means you're close.

Let's make that precise for a line through the origin, `prediction = w * x`, on three points that lie exactly on `y = 2x`. The slope of the loss with respect to `w` (its **gradient**) is the average of `2 * (w * x - y) * x`. The update rule is: new `w` = old `w` − learning rate × gradient. Step through five updates and watch `w` creep toward 2:

:::trace Five steps of gradient descent toward w = 2
```python
xs = [1, 2, 3]
ys = [2, 4, 6]
w = 0.0
learning_rate = 0.05
for step in range(5):
    gradient = 0
    for x, y in zip(xs, ys):
        gradient += 2 * (w * x - y) * x
    gradient = gradient / len(xs)
    w = w - learning_rate * gradient
    print(step, round(w, 3))
```
:::

The first gradient is large and negative (the line is far too flat), so `w` jumps from 0 to 0.933. Each later gradient is smaller, because the line is getting closer, so the steps shrink: 1.431, 1.697, 1.838, 1.914. The **learning rate** sets the step size. Too small and it takes forever; too big and you leap over the valley.

The same loop with two numbers to learn (slope and intercept) fits the BMI line. Here's its loss after every step, next to the line at a few moments along the way. BMI is standardized first, because gradient descent, like k-NN, works much better when features are on a similar scale:

```python run alt="Two plots. Left: mean squared error against gradient descent step, falling steeply from about 29,000 to under 5,000 within 10 steps and then flattening near 3,900. Right: the diabetes data with four lines for steps 0, 2, 5, and 40, starting flat at zero and rotating and rising until the last one fits the data." caption="The loss drops fast at first, then levels off as the line settles into place."
z = ((bmi["bmi"] - bmi["bmi"].mean()) / bmi["bmi"].std(ddof=0)).to_numpy()
target = progression.to_numpy()

w, b, rate = 0.0, 0.0, 0.1
losses, snapshots = [], {}
for step in range(41):
    error = (w * z + b) - target
    losses.append((error ** 2).mean())
    if step in (0, 2, 5, 40):
        snapshots[step] = (w, b)
    w -= rate * 2 * (error * z).mean()
    b -= rate * 2 * error.mean()

fig, (left, right) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
left.plot(losses, marker="o", markersize=3)
left.set_xlabel("step")
left.set_ylabel("mean squared error")
left.set_title("Loss going down")
right.scatter(z, target, s=8, alpha=0.4, color="gray")
zz = np.linspace(z.min(), z.max(), 2)
for step, (w_s, b_s) in snapshots.items():
    right.plot(zz, w_s * zz + b_s, linewidth=2, label=f"step {step}")
right.set_xlabel("bmi (standardized)")
right.set_title("The line at a few steps")
right.legend()
print(f"after 40 steps: slope {w:.2f}, intercept {b:.2f}, MSE {losses[-1]:.0f}")
```

Step 0 is a flat line at zero, with a huge loss. Within a few steps the line has jumped up to the middle of the data and tilted, and by step 40 it has settled where the loss can't go much lower. You'll write this loop yourself in the next lesson and check it against `LinearRegression`.

## Which one when?

| | k-nearest neighbors | Linear regression |
|---|---|---|
| Predicts | categories (`KNeighborsClassifier`) or numbers (`KNeighborsRegressor`) | numbers |
| What `fit` does | stores the training rows | finds the best slope(s) and intercept |
| Needs scaled features | yes, distances depend on units | not for `LinearRegression`; yes for gradient descent |
| Handles curved patterns | yes, it follows the data locally | no, it's one straight line (or flat surface) |
| Predicts outside the training range | no, it can only repeat values it has seen | yes, the line keeps going, for better or worse |
| Easy to explain | "these 5 similar cases said so" | "each extra unit adds this much" |

:::warning
**Using a classifier for a number.** `KNeighborsClassifier` treats every distinct target value as its own category. Give it a target with decimals, like these progression scores shifted by 0.5, and scikit-learn refuses:

```python run raises
KNeighborsClassifier().fit(bmi, progression + 0.5)
```

"Continuous" is scikit-learn's word for a target of decimal numbers. Use `KNeighborsRegressor` or `LinearRegression` for those. (With whole numbers, it won't even raise an error; it will quietly treat 151 and 152 as unrelated classes, so pick the model by what the target means.)

**Trusting a line far from the data.** The BMI line keeps rising forever, and at a BMI of 10 it predicts a negative progression score. A line is only trustworthy across the range of data it was fitted on.
:::

:::quiz
Q: k-NN with k = 1 gets 100% training accuracy and 85% test accuracy. With k = 9 it gets 92% and 91%. What's going on?
- [ ] k = 1 is better, because its training accuracy is higher
  Training accuracy for k = 1 is always perfect, because each point is its own neighbor. The test score is what counts.
- [x] k = 1 follows noise in the training data; k = 9 smooths it out and does better on new data
  Averaging over more neighbors stops single odd points from deciding the answer.
- [ ] k = 9 is just luckier with this test set
  A 6-point gap together with a jagged-versus-smooth boundary is a pattern, not luck.

Q: A line is `price = 3.2 * size + 50`. One house has size 100 and sold for 400. What's its residual?
- [ ] 370
  That's 3.2 × 100 + 50, which is the prediction, not the residual.
- [x] 30
  The residual is actual minus predicted: 400 − 370 = 30. The line guessed $30 too low.
- [ ] −30
  That would be predicted minus actual. The usual definition is actual minus predicted.

Q: In gradient descent, the gradient for `w` comes out as a large positive number. What does the update do?
- [x] Decreases `w` by a large amount
  The update subtracts learning rate × gradient, so a big positive gradient means a big step down.
- [ ] Increases `w` by a large amount
  A positive gradient means the loss rises as `w` rises, so downhill is toward smaller `w`.
- [ ] Leaves `w` alone, because a large gradient means the loss is already small
  A large gradient means steep ground, which means you're far from the bottom.

Q: You want to predict a house's price from its size, and some new houses are bigger than any in your data. Which model is the safer start?
- [ ] k-NN regression, because it handles curves
  For a house bigger than all the training houses, k-NN can only average the biggest ones it has seen, so it can't predict a higher price.
- [x] Linear regression, because the line continues past the largest training house
  Extending a line a little past the data is reasonable. Extending it very far is risky for any model.
- [ ] Neither; size can't predict price
  Size is one of the strongest predictors of price.
:::

:::takeaways
- k-NN stores the training rows and, for each new row, lets the k nearest ones vote (classification) or averages them (regression).
- Small k makes a jagged boundary that fits noise; large k makes a blunt one. Pick k by comparing scores on unseen data.
- Linear regression predicts `slope × feature + intercept`, choosing the slope and intercept that make the mean squared error smallest.
- A residual is actual minus predicted; MSE averages the squared residuals.
- Gradient descent repeatedly steps against the gradient of the loss. The learning rate sets the step size.
:::
