:::objectives
- Check your idea against the capstone requirements before you start building.
- Choose a project at the right difficulty and describe it in one sentence and one loop.
- Cut scope with a Must / Should / Could list until the Musts fit comfortably in your time.
- Write a one-page design document that answers the questions you'd otherwise answer by accident.
- Plan four milestones with a clear "done when" for each, and list where every asset comes from.
:::

Most first games don't fail because of a bug. They fail because of their size. "An open-world RPG with crafting and multiplayer" never ships. "A platformer where you dodge drones to collect five keys, across three short levels" does, and a finished small game teaches you more, and impresses people more, than half of a big one.

This module is your own game, built with everything from Modules 2 to 7. Here you choose it and plan it. The next lesson builds it in milestones, and the last one tests, polishes, and presents it.

## What the finished game must include

Every capstone has these. The right column says where you learned each one.

| Requirement | What counts | Module |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Controls | Keyboard (and mouse if you like) through the Input System, explained on screen or on the page | 3 |
| Physics and collision | Rigidbody 2D movement, colliders, and at least one trigger | 4 |
| Camera | A follow camera (Cinemachine) or fixed framing that shows threats before they arrive | 4 |
| Animation | A player Animator with at least three states driven by parameters | 5 |
| UI | A HUD, plus at least one menu (pause, game over, or title) | 5 |
| Audio | Music and at least three sound effects tied to game events | 5 |
| Enemy or obstacle logic | At least one state machine with two or more states | 6 |
| Levels | Two short levels, or one level with a complete loop from start to an end screen | 6 |
| Saving | Something that survives quitting: progress, a best score, or settings | 7 |
| Build | A Web build playable on itch.io (or a desktop build plus a recorded playthrough) | 7 |
| Presentation | An itch.io page with screenshots, a 30 to 60 second trailer, and a short live demo | 8 |

## Five ideas, from steady to stretch

Pick one, or use one as a template for your own. "New" is what you'd learn beyond the course.

| Idea | Difficulty | Core mechanic | New |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Coin Run**: collect 20 coins across two levels to open the exit, dodging patrol drones; best time saved | Steady | Platforming and timing | Almost nothing, so you can spend the time on polish |
| **Lantern Keep**: a top-down stealth escape where guards with vision cones patrol and keys open doors | Standard | Avoid being seen | Top-down movement (gravity scale 0), doors and keys |
| **Sky Courier**: climb forever on platforms that spawn above you while the floor rises; best height saved | Standard | Climb under pressure | Spawning and cleaning up level chunks |
| **Arena Survivor**: survive five waves of enemies, choosing one upgrade between waves; unlocked upgrades saved | Stretch | Fight and choose | Upgrade UI, enemies the player can defeat |
| **Grapple Ruins**: a small explorable map where a dash ability, found halfway through, opens new areas; three save slots | Stretch | Explore and unlock | Ability gating, a map that loops back on itself |

If you're unsure, choose one level easier than you think you can manage. Finishing early leaves time for polish, which is what makes a game feel good. Finishing late leaves nothing.

## Shrink it until it looks too small

Start with two sentences. The **pitch** says what the player does and why it's interesting: "Dodge sentry drones to grab five keys before the reactor melts down." The **core loop** is the set of verbs the player repeats every few seconds:

```mermaid caption="The core loop for Coin Run. If a loop step isn't fun in a grey box, more content won't save it."
flowchart LR
    A[Spot coins and drones] --> B[Plan a route]
    B --> C[Run and jump]
    C --> D{Hit?}
    D -->|yes| E[Lose a heart, respawn at checkpoint]
    D -->|no| F[Grab coin, score goes up]
    E --> A
    F --> A
```

Then sort every feature you can imagine into three lists:

- **Must**: the game isn't a game without it. This should be short.
- **Should**: makes it clearly better, and you'll do it if Musts are done.
- **Could**: nice ideas you're happy to drop.

Now estimate the hours for each Must and double the total, because everyone underestimates. If the doubled total doesn't fit in your time with room to spare, move Musts down until it does. This feels painful and is the most important planning step.

## The one-page design document

A design doc isn't paperwork. It's the list of decisions you'd otherwise make by accident at 11 p.m. Copy this template into a file called `DESIGN.md` in your project folder and fill it in. Keep it to one page.

```text
TITLE:
PITCH (one sentence):
CORE LOOP (3-5 verbs):

PLAYER
  Controls:            (keys and what they do)
  Moves/abilities:     (speeds, jump height, anything special)
  Health and failure:  (hearts? one hit? what happens on death?)

WORLD
  Levels:              (how many, one line each: what it teaches/tests/twists)
  Enemies/obstacles:   (each one: states, what warns the player, how to beat it)
  Pickups/goals:       (what the player collects or reaches, and why)

FEEDBACK
  Animation states:    (Idle, Run, Jump, Hurt, ...)
  HUD and menus:       (score, health, pause, game over, title)
  Sounds:              (music track, effect for each important event)

SAVING
  What persists:       (checkpoint, best score, settings) and where (PlayerPrefs / JSON)

SCOPE
  Must:
  Should:
  Could:

ASSETS AND CREDITS
  (every sprite, sound, font, and music track, with its source and license)

RISKS
  (the 2-3 things most likely to go wrong, and your plan for each)
```

The **Levels** line is where Module 6 pays off. Write each level as a sentence like "Level 1 teaches jumping over spikes, tests it with gaps, and twists it with a drone over the last gap." If you can't write that sentence, the level isn't designed yet.

## Four milestones

Plan your time as four milestones. Each has a definition of done, so "almost finished" can't hide.

```mermaid caption="Capstone milestones. Don't start a milestone until the previous one is done."
timeline
    title Capstone milestones
    Grey box : Player moves, jumps, and collides : Level blocked out with plain shapes : Camera follows
    Vertical slice : One level fully playable : An enemy, HUD, sounds, animation : Someone else has played it
    Content complete : Every level and menu exists : Saving works : No new features after this
    Ship : Web build on itch.io : Page, screenshots, trailer : Bugs from playtests fixed
```

The **vertical slice** is the most useful milestone. It's one small piece of the game at finished quality, and it tells you early whether the game is fun and how long a level really takes to build. If the slice took three times longer than you planned, cut levels now, not the night before the deadline.

## Art, sound, and credits you're allowed to use

You don't need to draw or compose anything, but every asset needs a license that lets you use it. Good free sources:

- [Kenney](https://kenney.nl/assets): thousands of sprites, tiles, UI pieces, and sounds, all CC0 (no credit required).
- [OpenGameArt](https://opengameart.org/): sprites and music under several licenses. Check each one; CC-BY means you must credit the author.
- [Freesound](https://freesound.org/): sound effects, many CC0 or CC-BY. Filter by license when you search.
- [sfxr.me](https://sfxr.me/) and [BeepBox](https://www.beepbox.co/): make your own effects and chiptune music in the browser.

Add a credits line for every asset you didn't make, even CC0 ones, to your design doc and, later, to your itch.io page. If you use AI-generated art or sound, itch.io's quality guidelines ask you to say so clearly in the project description.

:::quiz
Q: Your Must list adds up to 20 hours of estimates, and you have 30 hours in total. What should you do?
- [ ] Start building; 20 fits in 30
  Doubled, the estimate is 40 hours, which doesn't fit. Estimates are almost always too low.
- [x] Move Musts to Should until the doubled estimate fits, with time left for testing and polish
  Doubling corrects for the usual underestimate, and the last milestone needs real time too.
- [ ] Skip the vertical slice to save time
  The slice is what tells you early that you need to cut, so skipping it makes overruns worse.

Q: Which of these is a core loop?
- [ ] "A sci-fi world with a deep story about a reactor"
  That's setting. A loop is what the player does every few seconds.
- [x] "Spot drones, plan a route, run and jump, grab a key"
  These are repeated verbs, and each feeds the next.
- [ ] "Three levels and a boss"
  That's content structure, not the moment-to-moment loop.

Q: You found a great sprite pack on OpenGameArt licensed CC-BY 4.0. What do you need to do?
- [ ] Nothing, because it's on a free site
  Free to download isn't the same as no conditions; CC-BY has one.
- [x] Credit the author as the license asks, on your game's page and in your credits
  CC-BY lets you use and change the work as long as you give credit.
- [ ] Ask permission by email before using it
  The license already grants permission, on the condition that you credit the author.
:::

:::takeaways
- Check your idea against the requirements table before you build anything.
- A pitch and a core loop come first; content comes after the loop is fun.
- Sort features into Must, Should, and Could, double your estimates, and cut until the Musts fit.
- A one-page design doc records decisions on purpose, including how each level teaches, tests, and twists.
- Every milestone has a definition of done, and every asset has a source and a license in your credits.
:::
