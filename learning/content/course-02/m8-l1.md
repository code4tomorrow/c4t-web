Most unfinished Scratch games die the same way. There's a gorgeous title screen, a detailed plan for twenty levels, and a player sprite that still can't jump properly. Three weeks later the project hasn't been opened.

A three-level game that works beats a twenty-level game that doesn't. This capstone asks for something small and complete, and the plan you make today is what keeps it small.

## The brief

Build a game in Scratch with at least three levels (or waves, rounds, or chapters). It must have all eight of these:

| # | Requirement | What it looks like | Where you learned it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Start and end states | A title screen, plus a win ending and a lose ending | Modules 3 and 7 |
| 2 | Instructions | Controls and goal on the title screen or in a story intro | Module 7 |
| 3 | Progress the player can see | Level number, score, lives, or a timer on screen | Module 3 |
| 4 | Collisions that matter | Walls, hazards, pickups, or hits that change the game | Module 5 |
| 5 | Sound or animation | Costume animation, effects, or sounds for actions | Modules 2 and 3 |
| 6 | A custom block | At least one My Block you wrote and use | Module 6 |
| 7 | Clones | At least one sprite that makes and deletes clones | Module 6 |
| 8 | Rising or adaptive difficulty | Levels that get harder, difficulty that reacts to the player, or both | Module 7 |

You can reuse any project from this course as a starting point. Remixing your own Crystal Climb or Ghost Patrol is allowed, as long as the finished game goes well beyond the Build-along version.

## Five ideas, from gentle to ambitious

Pick one, or invent your own at a similar size. Stars show roughly how much new code you'll need.

**Crystal Climb: Three Caves** ★. Extend your Module 5 platformer into three levels with the level system from Module 7's Practice. Add bat clones that patrol each cave, more of them on each level, and a helper platform after three falls. The next lesson builds this one as its worked example, so it's the safest choice.

**Ghost Patrol: Siege** ★. Take the Module 7 version further: a boss wave, a power-up that drops from defeated ghosts (as clones), tough ghosts, and the top-5 high-score table from Module 6.

**Lantern Dive** ★★. A top-down underwater game. A diver swims in four directions with wall sliding (Module 5 Practice) through a cave, collecting pearl clones before the oxygen timer runs out. Each level is deeper: a darker backdrop, less oxygen, and faster jellyfish clones drifting across the path.

**Spell Duel** ★★. A story-driven quiz battle. Each level is a new opponent with a short scene before the fight. Right answers cast a spell (a particle burst custom block) that drains the opponent's power; wrong answers let the opponent strike back. Question difficulty adapts like the Module 7 quiz.

**Haunted Path** ★★★. A small tower defense game. Ghosts follow a winding path whose corners are stored in two lists (`path x` and `path y`), and each clone remembers which corner it's heading for. Click to place lantern clones that cost coins and zap nearby ghosts using `distance to`. Waves come from lists.

:::tip
Choose the idea where you already know how to build the core action, the thing the player does every second. If you can't picture the script for "the diver swims" or "the lantern zaps a ghost," pick a smaller idea.
:::

## Fill in a one-page plan

Copy this into a document or onto paper and fill in every line. Leaving a line blank is fine; leaving it blank by accident is how games stall in week two.

```text
GAME NAME:
PITCH (one sentence: who you are, what you do, how you win):

CONTROLS:
HOW YOU WIN:                     HOW YOU LOSE:

LEVELS (what changes each time):
  Level 1:
  Level 2:
  Level 3:

SPRITES (name: job):
VARIABLES (name: all sprites / this sprite only: what it means):
LISTS (name: one item per ___):
MESSAGES (name: who sends it, who listens):
CUSTOM BLOCK(S) (name: what it does):
CLONES (which sprite, why, and when they're deleted):
DIFFICULTY (what gets harder, and does anything adapt?):

MUST HAVE:
NICE TO HAVE:
ONLY IF TIME:
```

Here's the start of a filled-in plan for Lantern Dive, so you can see the level of detail that helps:

```text
PITCH: You're a diver collecting glowing pearls in a flooded cave before your air runs out.
CONTROLS: arrow keys to swim
HOW YOU WIN: collect every pearl on level 3     HOW YOU LOSE: oxygen reaches 0
LEVELS: 1 = 5 pearls, 60 s of air, 2 slow jellyfish
        2 = 7 pearls, 50 s, 3 jellyfish
        3 = 9 pearls, 45 s, 5 fast jellyfish
SPRITES: Diver: player; Cave: walls, one costume per level;
         Pearl: clone factory; Jelly: clone factory, hazard
VARIABLES: oxygen (all sprites), pearls left (all sprites), level (all sprites),
           speed (Jelly, this sprite only)
LISTS: pearl count, air time, jelly count, jelly speed (one item per level)
MESSAGES: load level, begin play, level done, game over, you win
CUSTOM BLOCK: spawn pearls (count) in Pearl, with run without screen refresh
CLONES: pearls (deleted when collected or on load level), jellyfish (same)
```

## Draw the states

Every plan should include the state diagram, because each arrow becomes a broadcast you'll have to write. Most multi-level games fit this shape; rename the boxes for yours.

```mermaid caption="The usual shape of a multi-level game. Your arrows are your messages."
stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Title
    Title --> LoadLevel: space pressed
    LoadLevel --> Playing: begin play
    Playing --> LoadLevel: level done, more levels left
    Playing --> Won: level done on the last level
    Playing --> GameOver: lives or time run out
    Won --> [*]
    GameOver --> [*]
```

## Cut it down to a game you'll finish

Sort every feature into three groups before you write any code.

- **Must have**: the eight requirements, plus the one action that makes your game *your* game. If any of these is missing, it isn't done.
- **Nice to have**: things that make it better but that the game works without, like a high-score table or a fourth level.
- **Only if time**: everything else. Most of these won't happen, and that's fine.

A good test: could you explain your must-have list to a friend in 30 seconds? If it takes longer, move something down a group.

:::warning
**Art first, code later is a trap.** It's tempting to spend the first session drawing ten perfect costumes. Use rough rectangles and library sprites until the game works, then swap in good art. If you change your mind about the design, you've lost five minutes of scribbles instead of an afternoon of drawing.
:::

## Check the plan before you build

Read your plan once more and make sure you can answer yes to all of these:

1. Does every one of the eight requirements show up somewhere in the plan?
2. Do your levels differ by *data* (numbers in lists, costumes) rather than by copies of whole scripts?
3. Does every clone have a line saying when it's deleted?
4. Does every variable have a starting value that the green flag sets?
5. Is the must-have list short enough that you could finish it in about half your available time?

If a "no" comes up, fix the plan now. It's much cheaper to change a sentence than a script.

:::takeaways
- The brief has eight requirements; map each one to a part of your game before you start.
- Pick an idea whose core action you already know how to build.
- A one-page plan with sprites, variables, lists, messages, and states saves hours of rework.
- Levels should differ by data, not by copied scripts.
- Must have, nice to have, only if time: build the first group completely before touching the second.
:::
