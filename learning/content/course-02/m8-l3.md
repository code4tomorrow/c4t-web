:::objectives
- Test every path through your game with a written test plan.
- Run a playtest where you watch and take notes instead of helping.
- Fix problems in order: broken, unfair, confusing, then plain.
- Add polish in separate scripts so it never slows the gameplay.
- Write clear instructions and credits, and present the game in two minutes.
:::

You've played your game a few hundred times. You know the jump in The Mines needs a running start, that space starts the game, and that bats never fly near the left wall. Nobody else knows any of that. You're the one person in the world who can't see what's confusing about your game, which is why this lesson is mostly about other people.

## Run your own test plan first

Before anyone else plays, check every path yourself. Copy this table and mark each row pass or fail. The `n` key from the skeleton makes rows 3 and 4 quick.

| # | Test | How | Pass if |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Fresh start | Click the green flag | Title shows, nothing moves, lives and level are reset |
| 2 | Second game | Finish a game, then click the green flag again | Starts on level 1 with full lives |
| 3 | Every level loads clean | Skip to each level | Player starts in open air, the goal is visible |
| 4 | No leftover clones | Watch each new level for 5 seconds | Only this level's enemies appear |
| 5 | Lose path | Lose every life on purpose | Lose ending shows, then the game stops |
| 6 | Win path | Play to the end without the `n` key | Win ending shows |
| 7 | Key mashing | Hammer every key on the title screen and between levels | Nothing starts early or breaks |
| 8 | Edges | Push into every edge of the stage | Player stays on the stage or respawns |
| 9 | Adaptive part | Play one level badly on purpose | The help or difficulty change kicks in |
| 10 | Sounds | Do each action once | Each sound plays once, at the right moment |

To catch clone leaks (row 4), make a temporary `clones` variable. Add `change [clones v] by (1)` as the first block under each `when I start as a clone`, and `change [clones v] by (-1)` just before every `delete this clone`, including the one inside `load level`. Its monitor should drop to 0 when a level loads and never creep upward over a long game.

## Watch someone else play

Find a playtester who hasn't seen your game: a friend, a sibling, a parent. Then follow three rules that feel strange but work:

1. **Say one sentence before they start:** "This is my game, and I can't help while you play."
2. **Hands off, mouth shut.** Don't point, don't explain, don't say "oh, you have to…". Every time you want to, write it down instead. Those are your most important notes.
3. **Watch their face and their hands.** Note where they hesitate, where they die, where they laugh, and where they frown.

When they finish (or give up), ask three questions:

- What was confusing?
- When was it the most fun?
- If you could change one thing, what would it be?

Keep a bug log as you go:

```text
WHAT HAPPENED                          WHERE            HOW OFTEN    TYPE
Didn't know space starts the game      title screen     every time   confusing
Died to a bat right after respawning   The Mines        2 times      unfair
Pip stuck in the wall after landing    The Summit       once         broken
```

## Fix in the right order

Not every note is equally urgent. Work down this list, and after each fix, rerun the rows of your test plan that it could affect.

1. **Broken**: the player gets stuck, the game can't be finished, or something crashes. Fix these first, always.
2. **Unfair**: deaths that feel like the game's fault, such as an enemy appearing on top of the player or a jump that's barely possible.
3. **Confusing**: the player didn't know what to do. Usually fixed with a clearer instruction, a sign, or a sound when something important happens.
4. **Plain**: it works, but it feels flat. That's what polish is for.

Change one thing at a time. If you change three things and the game breaks, you won't know which change did it.

## Polish without slowing the game

Game designers call the small reactions that make a game feel alive **juice**: a sound, a flash, a wobble, a burst of particles. In Scratch, the trick is to put each effect in its *own* script, started by a broadcast, so the movement loop never has to wait.

```scratch
when I receive [ouch v]
repeat (3)
  set [ghost v] effect to (60)
  wait (0.1) seconds
  set [ghost v] effect to (0)
  wait (0.1) seconds
end
```

Inside Pip's life-loss `if`, add `broadcast (ouch v)`. Pip flickers for 0.6 seconds while the main loop keeps running at full speed. If you'd put the `repeat` straight into the loop, Pip would freeze in place every time it got hit.

More effects worth a few minutes each:

- A particle burst (Module 6 Practice) where the goal is collected.
- The level's name shown for a moment at the start of each level.
- A quick fade between levels, using `change [brightness v] effect by` on the Stage.
- A different sound for winning and losing.

## Write the project page

A Scratch project page has two text boxes: **Instructions** and **Notes and Credits**. Players read the first one before clicking the green flag, so make it short and practical.

```text
INSTRUCTIONS
Reach the crystal at the end of each cave. Three caves, five lives.
Arrow keys: walk and jump. Space: start.
Tip: fall three times on a level and the cave will help you.

NOTES AND CREDITS
Made for the Creative Coding with Scratch capstone.
Sprites and sounds from the Scratch library.
Platformer ideas from the Crystal Climb build-along.
```

If you used anything from another project, such as art, music, or a script, name the project and its creator in Notes and Credits. Remixing is welcome on Scratch; leaving out credit isn't.

Before sharing, check with a parent or guardian, and leave out anything personal: your real name, school, town, and photos of yourself. The [Scratch Community Guidelines](https://scratch.mit.edu/community_guidelines) explain what's expected of everyone who shares. You don't have to share at all; showing the game in person or sending the saved file counts just as much.

## Present it in two minutes

Practice this once out loud:

1. **The pitch (15 seconds).** One sentence: who you are, what you do, how you win.
2. **The demo (45 seconds).** Play the first level live. Use the `n` key to show a later one.
3. **One script you're proud of (30 seconds).** A custom block or a clone factory works well. Say what it does and why you built it that way.
4. **One bug you fixed (20 seconds).** What happened, how you found it, what fixed it.
5. **What's next (10 seconds).** The one thing you'd add with another week.

## The rubric

| Criterion | 1: Beginning | 2: Developing | 3: Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Requirements | 4 or fewer of the 8 | 5 to 7 of the 8 | All 8 |
| Game flow | Starts straight into play, or has no ending | Has title and endings, but some transitions break | Title, instructions, levels, and both endings all work every time |
| Levels and difficulty | Levels feel the same | Later levels are harder | Difficulty rises with at least one rest, or adapts to the player |
| Code quality | Copied scripts, unnamed sprites | Some custom blocks and clones; mostly clear names | Levels come from data; custom blocks and clones used where they save repetition |
| Feedback and polish | Few sounds or effects | Sounds for main actions | Sound and visual feedback for every important event, without slowing play |
| Testing | No test plan | Test plan or playtest, not both | Test plan run, playtest held, and fixes made from the notes |
| Presentation and page | Missing instructions | Instructions or credits missing details | Clear instructions and credits; two-minute presentation covers all five parts |

## Where to go next

Everything you built in this course has a name in text-based languages. The forever loop is a **game loop**, `y speed` is a **velocity**, lists are **arrays**, custom blocks are **functions**, and clones are **objects**. If you try Python with Pygame or JavaScript with p5.js next, you'll recognize every one of them; you'll just be typing them instead of snapping them together.

And keep making Scratch games. Remix a project you admire to see how it works, or join a game jam studio to build something new in a week.

:::takeaways
- Test every path yourself with a written plan before anyone else plays.
- During a playtest, say nothing and write down every moment you wanted to help.
- Fix broken, then unfair, then confusing, then plain, one change at a time.
- Put effects in their own scripts, started by broadcasts, so gameplay never waits.
- A short Instructions box, honest credits, and a two-minute demo finish the job.
:::
