Your rock-paper-scissors model scores 100% on Teachable Machine's own test. Then your friend tries it and it calls their rock "Paper, 88%". Both results are true. They answer different questions: the first asks "does it work on pictures like the ones I trained on?", and the second asks "does it work for the people who will actually use it?"

You're going to answer the second question properly. By the end you'll have a one-page **model report card** like this:

```text
MODEL REPORT CARD: Rock / Paper / Scissors / Nothing
Tests run:        36 (4 conditions x 3 gestures x 3 tries)
Overall accuracy: 75%
Best condition:   Same desk, 100%
Worst condition:  Friend's hand, 56% (rock and scissors read as paper)
Threshold:        Below 80% the game says "Not sure, try again"
                  Catches 5 of 9 wrong answers; costs 5 of 27 right ones
Fix tried:        Added samples from 2 more people and dim light; worst condition rose to 89%
Use it for:       A game among friends. Not for anything that matters.
```

The charts in this lesson use one tester's log. Your numbers will differ, and that's the point: run your own tests and compare.

## Step 1: Train the model

Open Teachable Machine, choose **Image Project**, then **Standard image model**.

1.  Rename **Class 1** to `Rock` and **Class 2** to `Paper`. Click **Add a class** twice and name the new cards `Scissors` and `Nothing`.
2.  For each gesture, click **Webcam**, then press and hold **Hold to Record** while moving your hand around the frame: closer, farther, tilted. Aim for about 80 samples per class.
3.  For **Nothing**, record the empty background for about the same number of samples.
4.  Click **Train Model** and stay on the tab until it says **Model Trained**.

The **Nothing** class matters here. Without it, the model has to call an empty frame Rock, Paper, or Scissors, and your stress test would be full of nonsense answers before it even starts.

## Step 2: Read Teachable Machine's own report, and doubt it

Under the **Training** card, click **Advanced**, then **Under the hood**. Click **Calculate accuracy per class** and **Calculate confusion matrix**.

You'll probably see numbers at or near 100%. Teachable Machine got them by holding back 15% of your samples as test samples, never training on them, and checking the model on them afterwards. That's a fair method, with one catch. Your held-back samples were recorded in the same seconds, the same light, and with the same hand as the training samples. They're near-copies. The test answers "did it learn my recordings?", not "will it work tomorrow, for someone else?"

So you'll write a harder test.

## Step 3: Plan the stress test

Pick four **conditions**, each one a change the model might meet in real use:

| Condition | What changes |
| --- | --- |
| Same desk | Nothing. This is your baseline. |
| Dim light | Turn off the main light, keep a lamp on |
| New background | Move to a different room or hold a patterned shirt behind your hand |
| Friend's hand | Someone else makes the gestures: different size, skin tone, rings, nails |

For each condition, show each gesture three times: 4 × 3 × 3 = 36 tests. Before each one, say what you expect. Then hold the gesture still for two seconds and write down the **top class** and its **percentage**.

:::warning
Ask before you put someone else's hand in front of your camera, and tell them the samples stay in your browser unless you save. You don't need their face in the frame at all. Module 6 covers consent properly; for now, ask, explain, and keep faces out.
:::

## Step 4: Write the log

Here's one tester's log. Each line is one condition and one gesture, followed by the three results.

```python run
log_text = """
Same desk,      Rock,     Rock 99, Rock 98, Rock 97
Same desk,      Paper,    Paper 99, Paper 96, Paper 98
Same desk,      Scissors, Scissors 97, Scissors 95, Scissors 99
Dim light,      Rock,     Rock 94, Rock 91, Rock 88
Dim light,      Paper,    Paper 90, Paper 85, Scissors 58
Dim light,      Scissors, Paper 62, Scissors 71, Paper 55
New background, Rock,     Rock 92, Nothing 64, Rock 85
New background, Paper,    Paper 93, Paper 89, Paper 95
New background, Scissors, Scissors 84, Rock 71, Scissors 90
Friend's hand,  Rock,     Paper 88, Rock 76, Paper 81
Friend's hand,  Paper,    Paper 74, Paper 69, Paper 83
Friend's hand,  Scissors, Paper 93, Scissors 66, Paper 90
"""

tests = []   # one entry per try: (condition, true gesture, prediction, confidence)
for line in log_text.strip().splitlines():
    condition, true, *tries = [part.strip() for part in line.split(",")]
    for attempt in tries:
        predicted, confidence = attempt.split()
        tests.append((condition, true, predicted, int(confidence)))

right = sum(1 for _, true, predicted, _ in tests if true == predicted)
print(f"{len(tests)} tests, {right} right, overall accuracy {right / len(tests):.0%}")
```

75%. Down from Teachable Machine's 100%, and that's a single number. The next three charts open it up. If you're using Colab, paste these cells into a notebook and replace the log with your own lines; if not, tally your results on paper and use the charts to see what your tallies mean.

## Step 5: Draw the confusion matrix

Each row is what you showed the model; each column is what it said. Correct answers sit on the diagonal from top left.

```python run alt="Confusion matrix heatmap. Rows are the true gesture, columns the prediction. Rock: 9 right, 2 called Paper, 1 called Nothing. Paper: 11 right, 1 called Scissors. Scissors: 7 right, 4 called Paper, 1 called Rock." caption="Where the 9 mistakes went"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

true_classes = ["Rock", "Paper", "Scissors"]
predicted_classes = ["Rock", "Paper", "Scissors", "Nothing"]
counts = np.zeros((3, 4), dtype=int)
for _, true, predicted, _ in tests:
    counts[true_classes.index(true), predicted_classes.index(predicted)] += 1

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3.8))
plt.imshow(counts, cmap="Blues", vmin=0, vmax=12)
for row in range(3):
    for col in range(4):
        n = counts[row, col]
        plt.text(col, row, n, ha="center", va="center", fontsize=13,
                 color="white" if n > 6 else "#222")
plt.xticks(range(4), predicted_classes)
plt.yticks(range(3), true_classes)
plt.xlabel("Prediction (what the model said)")
plt.ylabel("Class (what you showed it)")
plt.title("Confusion matrix: 36 stress tests")
plt.tight_layout()
```

The Paper column is the story. Six of the nine mistakes are the model saying **Paper** when it wasn't. Scissors is the worst-recognized gesture (7 of 12), and most of its errors become Paper, which makes sense: two spread fingers in poor light look a lot like an open hand. When one column soaks up the mistakes, the model has learned a "default" answer it falls back on when unsure.

## Step 6: Split accuracy by condition

```python run alt="Bar chart of accuracy by condition: Same desk 100%, Dim light 67%, New background 78%, Friend's hand 56%. A dashed line marks the overall 75%." caption="The overall 75% is an average of very different situations"
conditions = ["Same desk", "Dim light", "New background", "Friend's hand"]
accuracy = []
for condition in conditions:
    rows = [t for t in tests if t[0] == condition]
    accuracy.append(100 * sum(1 for t in rows if t[1] == t[2]) / len(rows))

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3.8))
bars = plt.bar(conditions, accuracy, color="#2a78d6", width=0.6)
plt.bar_label(bars, fmt="%.0f%%", padding=3)
plt.axhline(100 * right / len(tests), color="#555", linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
plt.text(3.35, 100 * right / len(tests) + 2, "overall 75%", ha="right", color="#555")
plt.ylim(0, 110)
plt.ylabel("Accuracy (%)")
plt.title("Same model, four situations")
plt.tight_layout()
```

The model is perfect for the person who trained it and wrong almost half the time for the next person. This is Gender Shades at kitchen-table scale: the overall number (75%) describes nobody. It's too low for you and too high for your friend.

## Step 7: Choose a "not sure" threshold

A game can refuse to answer: *if the top class is below some percentage, say "Not sure, try again."* Where should that line go? Plot the confidence of every right answer and every wrong one:

```python run alt="Dot plot of confidence. Right answers range from 66% to 99%, mostly above 80%. Wrong answers spread from 55% to 93%, with five below 80% and four above. A dashed line marks 80%." caption="A threshold at 80% catches the unsure mistakes but not the confident ones"
right_conf = [c for _, t, p, c in tests if t == p]
wrong_conf = [c for _, t, p, c in tests if t != p]
threshold = 80

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3))
plt.scatter(right_conf, [1] * len(right_conf), s=70, color="#2a78d6", alpha=0.7)
plt.scatter(wrong_conf, [0] * len(wrong_conf), s=70, color="#eb6834", alpha=0.9)
plt.axvline(threshold, color="#333", linestyle="--")
plt.text(threshold - 1, 1.35, 'below 80%: say "not sure"', ha="right", color="#333")
plt.yticks([0, 1], ["Wrong", "Right"])
plt.ylim(-0.6, 1.6)
plt.xlabel("Confidence of the top class (%)")
plt.title("Where right and wrong answers fall")
plt.tight_layout()

caught = sum(1 for c in wrong_conf if c < threshold)
lost = [cond for cond, t, p, c in tests if t == p and c < threshold]
print(f"Wrong answers turned into 'not sure': {caught} of {len(wrong_conf)}")
print(f"Right answers turned into 'not sure': {len(lost)} of {len(right_conf)}")
print("Conditions of the lost right answers:", lost)
```

At 80%, five of the nine mistakes become a harmless "not sure". That's a real improvement. But look at the other four orange dots, sitting at 81% to 93%. They're all from the friend's hand, and the threshold can't touch them: the model is confidently wrong.

Now read the last printed line. Of the five right answers the threshold throws away, four belong to the friend. So the person the model already serves worst also gets asked to "try again" most. A threshold is a fair-looking rule that can still land unevenly. Raising it to 95% would catch every mistake and reject most of your friend's correct answers too.

## Step 8: Fix the data, retrain, retest

The charts point at the cause: the model only ever saw one hand in one light. With permission, record samples from two more people and in dim light, add them to the same classes, and click **Train Model** again. Then rerun all 36 tests with the same rules.

```python run alt="Grouped bar chart comparing accuracy before and after retraining. Same desk stays at 100. Dim light rises from 67 to 89, New background from 78 to 89, Friend's hand from 56 to 89." caption="One tester's results after adding varied examples"
after = [100, 89, 89, 89]   # this tester's retest: 9, 8, 8, and 8 right out of 9
x = np.arange(len(conditions))

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3.8))
b1 = plt.bar(x - 0.2, accuracy, 0.38, color="#2a78d6", label="Before: one person's hand")
b2 = plt.bar(x + 0.2, after, 0.38, color="#eb6834", label="After: three people, two kinds of light")
plt.bar_label(b1, fmt="%.0f", padding=2, fontsize=9)
plt.bar_label(b2, fmt="%.0f", padding=2, fontsize=9)
plt.xticks(x, conditions)
plt.ylim(0, 125)
plt.ylabel("Accuracy (%)")
plt.title("Retesting after adding varied examples")
plt.legend(frameon=False, loc="upper center", ncol=2)
plt.tight_layout()
```

The friend's-hand bar jumped the most, because that's where the training data was thinnest. You didn't change a single setting. You changed **who was in the data**, which is usually where fairness problems begin and where they're fixed.

One honest caveat: nine tests per condition is a small sample. A jump from 5 to 8 right answers is encouraging, not proof. A real product team would run hundreds of tests per group.

## The finished stress test

Your finished work is three things: the log (36 lines), the charts or hand tallies, and the report card at the top of this lesson filled in with *your* numbers. Write the last line ("Use it for") yourself. It's a judgement, and it's the most important line on the card.

:::quiz
Q: Teachable Machine reported 100% test accuracy, but your stress test found 75%. Why the gap?
- [ ] Teachable Machine's calculation has a bug
  Its method is fine. The issue is which samples it tested on.
- [x] Its test samples were recorded at the same time, in the same light, with the same hand as the training samples
  Held-back near-copies of training pictures can't reveal what happens with a new hand or room.
- [ ] Stress tests always lower accuracy by about 25%
  There's no fixed penalty. The gap depends on how different real use is from your recordings.

Q: A threshold of 80% caught 5 of 9 mistakes. Why didn't it catch the other 4?
- [ ] Those predictions were below 80%
  Then they'd have been caught. Check the dot plot: they sit to the right of the line.
- [x] The model was confidently wrong on the friend's hand, at 81% to 93%
  A threshold filters uncertain answers. It can't help when the model is sure and wrong.
- [ ] The threshold only works on the Same desk condition
  It applies to every test. It just can't separate confident mistakes from confident right answers.

Q: Which single change did the most to fix the worst condition?
- [ ] Raising the threshold to 95%
  That would hide mistakes by refusing most of the friend's answers, right or wrong.
- [ ] Training for more epochs on the original samples
  More passes over one person's hand teach it that hand more thoroughly, not other hands.
- [x] Adding samples from more people and lighting, then retraining
  The errors came from missing variety in the data, so adding that variety is the direct fix.
:::

:::takeaways
- Built-in test accuracy only shows how a model handles pictures like its training pictures. Real use needs a stress test.
- Plan tests by condition, predict before each one, and log the top class and its confidence.
- A confusion matrix shows which mistakes happen; one column soaking up errors means the model has a fallback answer.
- Split accuracy by condition or group. The average can describe nobody.
- A threshold catches uncertain mistakes but not confident ones, and its "try again" cost can fall hardest on the group already served worst.
:::
