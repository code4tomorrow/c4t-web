## Warm-up

A pizza shop wants to predict how many minutes late each delivery will be. It has a table with one row per past delivery and these columns: distance (km), time of day, raining (yes/no), driver's name, minutes late.

What is one example? Which columns are features? Which is the label? What kind of task is it?

:::solution
One example is one past delivery (one row). The features are distance, time of day, raining, and driver's name. The label is minutes late. Because the label is a number on a scale, it's regression.
:::

### Exercise 1: From a description to a table

A school's bird feeder has a camera. Each time something lands, the camera saves a photo, the time, and the temperature. A volunteer later writes the species under each photo. The club wants a model that names the species automatically.

Sketch the table with three made-up rows. Mark the features and the label.

:::hint
The volunteer's writing is the part the model must learn to produce.
:::

:::solution
| Photo | Time | Temperature (°C) | Species (label) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| photo_001.jpg | 07:42 | 4 | Blue jay |
| photo_002.jpg | 07:45 | 4 | Chickadee |
| photo_003.jpg | 12:10 | 9 | Squirrel |

The photo, time, and temperature are features. The species is the label, supplied by the volunteer. Notice that "Squirrel" is a label too: if squirrels visit, the model needs that class, or it will force every squirrel into some bird species, just like the empty room in Module 1.
:::

### Exercise 2: One table, three questions

A class keeps a table with these columns for each student:

`hours of sleep` · `minutes of homework per night` · `plays a sport` · `attended the review session` · `test score`

For each question, name the label column and say whether any other column should be removed:

1.  Predict a student's test score.
2.  Predict whether a student will attend the review session, a week before it happens.
3.  Group students by habits to plan study groups, with no particular answer in mind.

:::hint
For question 2, think about which columns exist a week before the session. For question 3, check whether there's a label at all.
:::

:::solution
1.  Label: **test score** (regression). Keep the rest as features.
2.  Label: **attended the review session** (classification). Remove **test score**: the test comes after the session, so a week before, nobody knows it.
3.  **No label**: this is clustering. All the habit columns are used to find groups. You might drop test score, so the groups are about habits rather than results, but that's a choice about what you want the groups to mean.
:::

### Exercise 3: Spot the feature from the future

On Monday morning, a school wants to predict which students will miss the Friday field-trip bus. A team proposes these features. Mark each one as usable or not, with a reason.

1.  Number of times the student was late this term.
2.  Friday's weather forecast, as known on Monday.
3.  Whether the student was absent on Thursday.
4.  Whether the student's permission slip has been returned.
5.  Whether the student was marked "missed bus" on Friday.

:::hint
Picture yourself on Monday morning. Which of these could you look up right now?
:::

:::solution
1.  **Usable.** It's known on Monday and plausibly related.
2.  **Usable.** The forecast exists on Monday, even if it's later wrong. (Friday's *actual* weather would not be usable.)
3.  **Not usable.** Thursday hasn't happened yet on Monday.
4.  **Usable**, if you record its status as of Monday. Probably the strongest feature.
5.  **Not usable, and dangerous.** That's the label itself. A model with this column would score perfectly in training and be useless in real life. This is leakage in its purest form.
:::

### Exercise 4: Predict what the model learns

A researcher collects 20 photos of huskies and 20 of wolves. Every wolf photo happens to have snow in the background. Almost none of the husky photos do. A model trained on these scores well on more photos taken the same way.

1.  What has the model most likely learned?
2.  What would it say about a husky photographed in snow?
3.  How would you fix the dataset?

:::hint
Look for something that's in every photo of one class and missing from the other, that isn't the animal.
:::

:::solution
1.  Most likely **snow versus no snow**. Snow is a large, easy-to-spot difference that separates the classes perfectly in this data.
2.  **Wolf.** The shortcut says "snow means wolf".
3.  Collect wolves without snow and huskies with snow, so the background appears in both classes and stops separating them. Then test on new photos chosen to break the shortcut.

This isn't hypothetical. In a well-known 2016 study ("Why Should I Trust You?"), researchers deliberately trained a husky-versus-wolf classifier on photos like these, and their explanation tool showed it was mostly using the snow. It's the same trap as the hand in your Round 3 sorter.
:::

### Exercise 5: Write a labeling rule

Three volunteers labeled the same 100 banana photos as **Ripe** or **Not ripe**. They disagreed on 23 photos: mostly bananas with a few brown spots, and bananas that were yellow with green tips.

Write a labeling rule all three could follow, and say what to do with photos that still don't fit.

:::hint
A good rule names something visible in the photo and settles the two problem cases explicitly.
:::

:::solution
One workable rule:

-   **Ripe**: the peel is mostly yellow (no green visible except possibly at the very stem) and has at most scattered brown spots.
-   **Not ripe**: any green on the body or tips.
-   **Exclude** (don't label): photos where the banana is mostly brown, blurry, or covered by something.

What matters is less the exact rule than having one, writing it down, and re-checking that labelers now agree. If the volunteers still disagree on a case, the model will learn a blur of their opinions on exactly those photos. Teachable Machine's home page has a "Bananameter" tutorial built around this very problem.
:::

### Exercise 6: Read a decision boundary

A mango sorter learned from 40 mangoes, each measured for firmness and skin color. The chart shows its decision boundary and four new mangoes, A to D.

```python run alt="Scatter plot of mangoes by firmness and skin color. Unripe training mangoes (blue circles) sit bottom right; ripe ones (orange triangles) sit top left. A diagonal boundary separates an orange region above from a blue region below. New mango A is deep in the ripe region, B deep in the unripe region, C sits right on the boundary, and D is at the far top right, rock hard and orange, far from any training mango but just inside the ripe region." caption="Four new mangoes: which predictions would you trust?"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
BLUE, ORANGE = "#2a78d6", "#eb6834"
# Mangoes: firmness (higher = harder) and skin color (0 = green, 10 = deep orange)
unripe = np.column_stack([rng.normal(7, 1.0, 20), rng.normal(3, 1.1, 20)])
ripe = np.column_stack([rng.normal(3.5, 1.0, 20), rng.normal(7, 1.1, 20)])
X = np.vstack([unripe, ripe])
y = np.array([0] * 20 + [1] * 20)
model = LogisticRegression().fit(X, y)

gx, gy = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(0, 13, 300), np.linspace(0, 11, 300))
pred = model.predict(np.column_stack([gx.ravel(), gy.ravel()])).reshape(gx.shape)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 4.4))
ax.contourf(gx, gy, pred, levels=[-0.5, 0.5, 1.5], colors=["#d6e6f8", "#fbe0d4"])
ax.contour(gx, gy, pred, levels=[0.5], colors="#555555", linewidths=1.5)
ax.scatter(*unripe.T, color=BLUE, marker="o", label="Unripe (training)")
ax.scatter(*ripe.T, color=ORANGE, marker="^", label="Ripe (training)")

new_mangoes = {"A": (2.5, 8.5), "B": (8, 2), "C": (5.3, 5.0), "D": (12, 9.5)}
for name, (firm, color) in new_mangoes.items():
    ax.scatter(firm, color, s=160, facecolor="white", edgecolor="#222", linewidth=1.5, zorder=3)
    ax.text(firm, color, name, ha="center", va="center", fontsize=9, fontweight="bold", zorder=4)

ax.set_xlabel("Firmness (0 = soft, 13 = rock hard)")
ax.set_ylabel("Skin color (0 = green, 10 = orange)")
ax.set_title("A mango sorter's decision boundary, and four new mangoes", loc="left")
ax.legend(frameon=False, loc="lower left")
ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
fig.tight_layout()
```

1.  What does the model predict for each of A, B, C, and D?
2.  Which two predictions would you trust least, and why?

:::hint
Trust drops in two different places: near the line, and far from every training mango. Find one of each.
:::

:::solution
1.  **A: Ripe** (orange region). **B: Unripe** (blue region). **C: Ripe, barely**: it sits almost on the line. **D: Ripe**, just inside the orange region.
2.  **C and D, for different reasons.**
    -   **C is on the boundary.** A tiny measuring error, like pressing slightly harder when testing firmness, would flip the answer. The model is close to a coin toss here.
    -   **D is far from every training mango.** No training mango was both rock hard and fully orange. Maybe it's a different variety, or a plastic decoration. The model still answers, because the boundary extends forever, but it has no experience to back the answer up. A model's predictions are most reliable where its training examples were.

Module 5 turns these two kinds of doubt into a careful look at confidence scores.
:::

### Challenge: Plan a door-watching dataset

Your class wants a Teachable Machine model that looks at a webcam photo of the classroom door and says **Open** or **Closed**. Plan the dataset on one page:

-   The classes (are two enough?) and a labeling rule for "half open".
-   How many samples per class, and what you'll vary while recording.
-   A secret test set: what it contains and when you'll record it.
-   One shortcut the model could learn by accident, and how your plan prevents it.

:::hint
Think about what else changes in the photos between times the door is open and closed. Who tends to be in the doorway? What does the light from the hallway do?
:::

:::solution
An example plan:

-   **Classes:** Open, Closed, and **Blocked** (someone standing in the doorway), so a person isn't forced into Open or Closed. Rule: "Open means you can see the hallway floor through the gap; otherwise Closed."
-   **Samples:** about 100 per class, recorded at different times of day, with hallway lights on and off, with the door open at several angles, and with people walking past in both classes.
-   **Secret test set:** 20 photos per class taken on a different day, never added to training.
-   **Shortcut to prevent:** hallway light. If the door is only ever open when the hallway lights are on, the model may learn "bright = open". Recording open-door samples with the hallway dark (and closed-door samples while light leaks under the door) breaks the link. A second shortcut: the clock on the wall shows different times in each class if you record all the Open samples first. Mixing the recording times fixes that.
:::
