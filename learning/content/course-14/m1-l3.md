:::objectives
- Save a project as a `.tm` file or to Google Drive, and open it again.
- Organize your course files so you can find any project later.
- Keep a model journal that records what you changed, what you predicted, and what happened.
- Work through the common camera, training, and prediction problems in a fixed order.
- Open Google Colab and run a cell (optional).
:::

Teachable Machine keeps everything in the open tab. That's why it's private, and it's also why the most common way to lose an hour of work is a single click on the wrong **×**. If you try to leave with unsaved samples, the page warns you: "You have unsaved data. Are you sure you'd like to exit?" Believe it.

This lesson sets up three habits that make the rest of the course smoother: save every project, write down every experiment, and debug in a fixed order instead of clicking around.

## Save a project two ways

Everything lives in the menu: click the button with three lines at the top left of a project page.

:::tabs
@tab Download a file (no account)
1.  Open the menu and choose **Download project as file**.
2.  Your browser saves a file ending in `.tm` to your Downloads folder. It holds every class and every sample.
3.  To pick up where you left off, open Teachable Machine, open the menu, and choose **Open project from file**. Pick the `.tm` file.

This is the right choice on a shared or school computer: you can carry the file on a USB stick, and nothing goes to any online account.
@tab Save to Google Drive
1.  Open the menu and choose **Save project to Drive**.
2.  The first time, it asks you to log in to Google Drive. Then it asks you to **Name your project**.
3.  To reopen, choose **Open project from Drive** from the menu, or from the New Project screen.
4.  After you change anything, save again. Drive keeps the version you last saved, not the one on your screen.

Drive saves are handy across computers, but they need a Google account (see the note on accounts below).
:::

You can also save just one class's examples. On a class card, the three-dot menu has **Download Samples**, which makes a zip of that class's pictures. It's handy for keeping a set of test pictures apart from your training samples.

:::note
Downloading a project file is different from **Export Model**. A project file keeps your *samples* so you can keep training. Export Model makes a finished model for use in websites and apps, and it isn't needed anywhere in this course.
:::

## Name files so future-you can find them

Make one folder for the course and one subfolder per module. Name each project with the module, the idea, and a version number:

```text
AI course/
  m1/
    m1-thumbs-v1.tm         two classes
    m1-thumbs-v2.tm         added a Nothing class
  m2/
    m2-clap-detector-v1.tm
  journal.docx
```

The version number is the important part. When an experiment makes things worse, you want the earlier version back, and `final-final-2.tm` won't tell you which one that was.

## Keep a model journal

Machine learning is mostly experiments: change one thing, retrain, test, compare. Without notes you'll forget which change helped, and after three rounds everything blurs together. Use a notebook, a document, or a spreadsheet, with one row per experiment:

| Date | Project | What I changed | My prediction | What happened | Why I think so |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oct 3 | m1-thumbs-v1 | Nothing: first model | Works on both hands | Right hand fine, left hand 50/50 | Every sample was my right hand |
| Oct 3 | m1-thumbs-v2 | Added a Nothing class, 60 samples | Empty room → Nothing | Nothing wins at 95% | It finally has a third answer |

Two columns do most of the work. **My prediction** forces you to commit before you look, which is how you find out whether your mental picture of the model is right. **Why I think so** turns a surprise into a lesson.

The journal also catches lopsided classes. Here's a chart of the sample counts from a project where one class got far fewer examples than the others:

```python run alt="Bar chart of samples per class: Thumbs up 84, Thumbs down 80, Nothing 20. The Nothing bar is much shorter." caption="A lopsided project: the Nothing class has a quarter as many samples"
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

classes = ["Thumbs up", "Thumbs down", "Nothing"]
samples = [84, 80, 20]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.2))
bars = ax.bar(classes, samples, color="#2a78d6", width=0.55)
ax.bar_label(bars, padding=3, color="#333")
ax.set_ylabel("Samples")
ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
ax.set_title("Samples per class", loc="left")
```

A model trained on this has seen four times as many hands as empty rooms. When it's unsure, it will tend to guess a hand. If your journal shows counts like these, record more samples for the short class before blaming anything else.

## Get unstuck in a fixed order

When something breaks, work down this chart instead of trying random fixes:

```mermaid caption="Find the stage that's failing, then fix that stage only"
flowchart TD
    A{Camera or mic view showing?} -->|no| B["Fix permissions (lesson 1)"]
    A -->|yes| C{Training finishes?}
    C -->|no| D[Stay on the tab, close others, fewer samples]
    C -->|yes| E{Predictions sensible?}
    E -->|no| F[Look at your examples]
    E -->|yes| G[Save, then test harder cases]
```

| Symptom | Most likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Could not open your camera. You may have denied access." | You clicked Block | Allow the site in browser settings, then reload |
| Black camera view | Another app holds the camera | Quit call apps completely, reload |
| Training never finishes | Tab was hidden or the computer is slow | Keep the tab in front; train with 50 to 100 samples per class |
| One class always wins | Unequal sample counts, or classes recorded in different places | Balance the counts; record all classes in the same spot |
| Perfect at your desk, wrong elsewhere | Examples were all from one spot | Add samples from other places and lighting |

Notice that the last three rows aren't fixed in the settings. They're fixed in the examples. That's the most common surprise for beginners, and it's the subject of Module 3.

When you ask someone for help, give them four things: what you did, what you expected, what happened, and a screenshot. Check the screenshot first; crop out other people and anything personal in the background.

## Optional: open Google Colab

Google Colab runs Python notebooks on Google's computers, in the browser, for free. Every chart in this course is drawn by a short Python program, and if you're curious, you can paste one into Colab and change the numbers. You'll never need it to complete a lesson.

1.  Go to `colab.research.google.com` and sign in with a Google account.
2.  Create a new notebook. Depending on the version, it's a **New notebook** button in the opening window or **File › New notebook in Drive**.
3.  Click inside the empty code cell and paste the chart program from above.
4.  Press **Shift + Enter**, or click the round play button at the left of the cell. The chart appears under the cell.

Change `20` to `80` in the program and run it again: the bars even out.

:::warning
**Colab asks you to sign in, or won't open at all.** Colab needs a Google account. Google requires you to be at least 13 (the age differs in some countries) to manage your own account; younger learners need a parent-managed account through Family Link. School Google accounts sometimes have Colab switched off by the school. All of this is fine to skip: Colab is a bonus, not a requirement.
:::

:::quiz
Q: You'll finish a project tomorrow on a different school computer, and you don't have a Google account. What should you do before closing the tab?
- [x] Use Download project as file and take the .tm file with you
  The file holds every class and sample, and Open project from file restores it on any computer.
- [ ] Click Export Model and download it
  An exported model can't be trained further in Teachable Machine; it doesn't keep your samples.
- [ ] Bookmark the page
  The bookmark only saves the address. The samples vanish when the tab closes.

Q: Your model works at your desk but fails in the kitchen. Which journal entry would help you most next time?
- [ ] "Didn't work, tried again"
  This records that something failed, but not what you changed or why.
- [x] "All samples recorded at my desk; predicted kitchen would work; it didn't; examples only show one place"
  It records the change, the prediction, the result, and a reason you can act on: add samples from other places.
- [ ] "Accuracy bad"
  Without the conditions and your prediction, you can't learn anything from it later.
:::

:::takeaways
- Save with Download project as file (a `.tm` file) or Save project to Drive; closing the tab without saving loses everything.
- Name projects with the module, the idea, and a version number.
- A journal row records the change, your prediction, the result, and why.
- Debug in order: camera, then training, then predictions. Most prediction problems are fixed by changing the examples.
- Colab is optional; it lets you rerun the course's charts if you're curious.
:::
