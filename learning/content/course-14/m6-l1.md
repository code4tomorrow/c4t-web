In August 2006, AOL published about 20 million search queries so researchers could study them. To protect its users, it replaced each person's name with a number. Within days, *New York Times* reporters read the searches of user No. 4417749 (landscapers in Lilburn, Georgia; people with the surname Arnold; a dog that pees on everything) and knocked on the door of Thelma Arnold, a 62-year-old widow in Lilburn. She confirmed the searches were hers.

Nobody at AOL typed her name into that file. Her searches *were* her name. Machine learning makes this problem bigger, because models are trained on enormous piles of exactly this kind of data.

## Learning patterns versus memorizing examples

When you train a Teachable Machine model, the result isn't a folder of your photos. Its FAQ describes the uploaded model as "the mathematical program that predicts which class you're showing it", and the samples themselves are never published with it. A model is millions of numbers tuned to separate your classes.

But "it's just numbers" doesn't mean "it remembers nothing." Models can **memorize**: store a specific training example closely enough to reproduce it. Memorization is most likely for examples that are rare, unusual, or repeated many times, and bigger models memorize more.

In 2021, Nicholas Carlini and researchers from Google, OpenAI, Apple, and several universities showed this on GPT-2, a language model trained on text from the web. By asking it to write freely and then searching its outputs for oddly specific text, they pulled out hundreds of passages copied word for word from its training data. One was a real person's full name, email address, phone number, and home address.

The rule of thumb that follows: **anything in the training data might come back out.** If you wouldn't post it publicly, think hard before training on it.

## "We removed the names" is not anonymous

Information that isn't a name but can combine to point at one person is called a **quasi-identifier**: ZIP code, birth date, gender, school, job title, even a list of movies you rated.

-   In 1997, Latanya Sweeney found the hospital records of Massachusetts governor William Weld in a "de-identified" state health dataset. She matched his ZIP code, gender, and birth date against a voter list she bought for $20. Using 1990 census data, she later estimated that 87% of Americans could likely be singled out by those three facts alone.
-   In 2008, Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov took the "anonymous" movie ratings Netflix had released for a prize contest and linked some of them to people's public reviews on IMDb, connecting those public profiles to private viewing histories.

Here's why it works. The chart builds a made-up town of 30,000 people and counts how many become the *only* person matching what you know, as you learn one more fact at a time:

```python run alt="Bar chart of the share of 30,000 people who are a unique match. ZIP code alone, plus gender, and plus birth year all give about 0%. Adding birth month gives a few percent. Adding the day of the month jumps to about 90%." caption="Each fact cuts the crowd; together they point at one person"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from collections import Counter

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 30_000                                  # people in a made-up town
zip_code = rng.integers(0, 5, n)            # 5 ZIP codes
gender = rng.integers(0, 2, n)              # 2 options on the form
year = rng.integers(1945, 2015, n)          # 70 possible birth years
month = rng.integers(1, 13, n)
day = rng.integers(1, 29, n)

def share_unique(*columns):
    rows = list(zip(*columns))
    counts = Counter(rows)
    return 100 * sum(1 for row in rows if counts[row] == 1) / n

labels = ["ZIP", "+ gender", "+ birth year", "+ birth month", "+ birth day"]
shares = [share_unique(zip_code),
          share_unique(zip_code, gender),
          share_unique(zip_code, gender, year),
          share_unique(zip_code, gender, year, month),
          share_unique(zip_code, gender, year, month, day)]

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3.8))
bars = plt.bar(labels, shares, color="#2a78d6", width=0.6)
plt.bar_label(bars, fmt="%.0f%%", padding=3)
plt.ylim(0, 110)
plt.ylabel("People who are the only match (%)")
plt.title("No names, but how many people can be singled out?")
plt.tight_layout()
```

The first three facts barely matter: thousands of people share a ZIP code and gender, and dozens share a birth year too. Then the full birth date arrives and most of the town becomes unique. That cliff is the danger. Each column looks harmless on its own; the combination is a fingerprint.

The defences are real but limited: **generalize** (birth year instead of birth date, a region instead of a ZIP code), **aggregate** (publish counts, not rows), or add carefully measured random noise, a method called **differential privacy** that the US Census Bureau used for the 2020 census. All of them trade some usefulness for privacy.

## Consent that means something

Consent is real when it is:

-   **Informed**: people know what's collected, why, who sees it, and how long it's kept.
-   **Specific**: it covers a stated purpose. "Data that may be useful later" isn't a purpose.
-   **Voluntary**: saying no doesn't cost them something they need, and there's an equal alternative.
-   **Revocable**: they can change their mind, and their data actually gets deleted.

"It was public" is not consent for every use. Clearview AI collected billions of photos from public websites and social media (over 30 billion, according to the Dutch data protection authority) to build a face search engine sold mainly to police. Nobody in those photos agreed to that. Regulators in France, Italy, and Greece each fined the company €20 million, the UK fined it £7.5 million in 2022, and the Netherlands fined it €30.5 million in 2024.

## Why face recognition is so disputed

Faces are unlike passwords: you can't change yours, and a camera can capture it from across a street without you knowing. That's why the same technology gets very different reactions depending on how it's used.

**Unlocking your own phone** compares your face to one stored template, on a device you own, after you chose to set it up. **Scanning a crowd** compares everyone who walks past against a database, and none of them chose anything.

The case against crowd scanning is concrete. In January 2020, Robert Williams was arrested at his home in Michigan, in front of his daughters, because face recognition software matched him to blurry shoplifting footage. It wasn't him. Gender Shades had already shown these systems err more on darker-skinned faces. San Francisco banned city agencies from using face recognition in 2019. In June 2020, IBM said it would stop offering general-purpose face recognition, Amazon paused police use of its Rekognition product, and Microsoft said it wouldn't sell to US police without a federal law.

The case for some uses is concrete too: finding missing children, identifying victims after disasters, unlocking a phone without typing a code. Most of the serious argument isn't "faces: yes or no" but **who uses it, on whom, with whose consent, and with what checks when it's wrong.**

## Kids' data and COPPA

In the US, the **Children's Online Privacy Protection Act** (COPPA) applies to websites, apps, and online services aimed at children under 13, and to any service that knows it's collecting from under-13s. The basics:

-   Get **verifiable parental consent** before collecting a child's personal information. A checkbox the child ticks doesn't count.
-   "Personal information" includes a photo, video, or audio file containing a child's image or voice, as well as names, contact details, and location.
-   Parents can review their child's data and have it deleted. Keep it only as long as needed.

The Federal Trade Commission enforces COPPA. In 2019, Google and YouTube paid $170 million to settle claims that YouTube collected data from children watching kid-directed channels without parental consent. Updated COPPA rules took effect in June 2025, adding requirements such as separate parental consent before sharing kids' data with third parties like advertisers, and a written policy on how long data is kept.

COPPA stops at 13, but good practice doesn't. Teens' data is still personal, schools have their own rules (FERPA in the US), and Europe's GDPR sets an age of digital consent between 13 and 16 depending on the country. None of this is legal advice; it's the minimum to know before you build anything that records people.

:::warning
**Three privacy mistakes beginners make**

1.  **"It's public, so it's fine to use."** Public means visible, not "agreed to any purpose." Ask whether the people would expect your use.
2.  **"We deleted the names, so it's anonymous."** Check for combinations of quasi-identifiers. If three or four columns together point at one person, it isn't anonymous.
3.  **"We'll delete it later."** Later never comes without a date. Write the deletion date down when you collect the data.
:::

:::quiz
Q: A school shares a spreadsheet of student survey answers with names removed but keeps grade, homeroom, gender, and birthday. What's the main risk?
- [ ] None, because there are no names
  Names aren't the only identifiers. Homeroom plus birthday probably points at one student.
- [x] The remaining columns together can single out individual students
  These are quasi-identifiers. In a homeroom of 30, very few students share a gender and a birthday.
- [ ] Spreadsheets can be hacked
  True of any file, but the specific problem here is that the "anonymous" data isn't anonymous.

Q: Your Teachable Machine model was trained on photos of your face. You click Upload my model. What gets published?
- [ ] Your face photos
  The FAQ says the samples you trained on are not published with the model.
- [x] The trained model, which is numbers rather than photos, but which can still recognize your face
  The photos stay private, yet the model itself carries information about you, so think before sharing it.
- [ ] Nothing, because Teachable Machine runs in your browser
  Training runs in the browser, but uploading publishes the model to Google's servers at a shareable link.

Q: Which of these is the strongest example of real consent?
- [ ] A sign at the door: "By entering, you agree to be filmed for AI research"
  Not voluntary if you need to enter, and not specific about use or retention.
- [ ] A form saying "We may use your recordings for future projects"
  "Future projects" isn't a specific purpose, and nothing says how long the data is kept.
- [x] A form explaining what's recorded, for which project, who sees it, when it's deleted, and how to withdraw, with an equal option to take part without being recorded
  It's informed, specific, voluntary, and revocable.

Q: A homework app for 9-year-olds records children reading aloud. Under COPPA, what must it do before recording?
- [ ] Ask the child to tick "I agree"
  Children under 13 can't give this consent themselves.
- [x] Get verifiable consent from a parent
  Voice recordings of a child are personal information, so a parent must agree first, in a way that confirms they really are the parent.
- [ ] Nothing, because voice isn't personal information
  COPPA explicitly covers audio files that contain a child's voice.
:::

:::takeaways
- Models learn patterns but can also memorize rare or repeated examples, so anything in training data might come back out.
- Removing names rarely anonymizes data. Combinations like ZIP, gender, and birth date single out most people.
- Real consent is informed, specific, voluntary, and revocable. "Public" and "useful later" don't qualify.
- Face recognition is disputed because faces can't be changed and can be captured without consent; the key questions are who uses it, on whom, and with what checks.
- COPPA requires verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information, including photos and voice, from children under 13.
:::
