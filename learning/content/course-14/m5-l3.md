## Warm-up: what does 51% mean?

A Cat/Dog model in Teachable Machine shows **Cat 51%, Dog 49%** for a photo. What should you conclude, and what should a program using this model do?

:::solution
The model can barely tell. 51 to 49 is a near tie, so the honest reading is "unsure", not "cat". It also doesn't mean there's a 51% chance of a cat; the bars just split 100 points between the two answers it knows. A careful program would treat anything this close as "not sure" and ask for another photo, or a person, instead of acting on it.
:::

### Exercise 1: Name the mistake

For each case, say whether it's a **false positive** or a **false negative**. Decide first what "positive" means in that system.

1.  A spam filter sends your coach's practice-time email to spam.
2.  An airport scanner lets a bag with a knife through.
3.  Face unlock opens your phone for your brother.
4.  A plagiarism checker flags an essay you wrote yourself.
5.  A smoke alarm stays silent while a pan catches fire.

:::hint
Ask: what is the system trying to detect? That's "positive". A false positive is a "yes" that should have been "no"; a false negative is a "no" that should have been "yes".
:::

:::solution
| Case | "Positive" means | Mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coach's email in spam | Spam | False positive |
| Knife missed | Dangerous item | False negative |
| Phone opens for brother | "This is the owner" | False positive |
| Original essay flagged | Copied work | False positive |
| Silent smoke alarm | Fire | False negative |

Number 3 trips people up. The phone isn't detecting "a stranger"; it's detecting "the owner", and it said yes to someone who isn't.
:::

### Exercise 2: Build a confusion matrix from a story

A plant app checks 200 leaf photos. 40 of the leaves really are diseased. The app flags 50 leaves as diseased, and 32 of those really are. Fill in the four cells, then answer: what is the accuracy? What share of diseased leaves did it catch? What share of its flags were right?

:::hint
Start with the cell you're given (32 flagged and diseased). The other three come from subtraction: 40 diseased in total, 50 flags in total, 200 leaves in total.
:::

:::solution
```python run
true_pos = 32                  # flagged and diseased
false_pos = 50 - 32            # flagged but healthy
false_neg = 40 - 32            # diseased but missed
true_neg = 200 - true_pos - false_pos - false_neg

print("               flagged   not flagged")
print(f"diseased       {true_pos:>5}   {false_neg:>9}")
print(f"healthy        {false_pos:>5}   {true_neg:>9}")
print(f"accuracy: {(true_pos + true_neg) / 200:.0%}")
print(f"diseased leaves caught: {true_pos / 40:.0%}")
print(f"flags that were right:  {true_pos / 50:.0%}")
```

Accuracy is 87%, which sounds good. But the app missed 1 in 5 diseased leaves, and more than a third of its warnings were false alarms. The last two numbers have names you'll meet later: the share caught is **recall** and the share of flags that are right is **precision**. Accuracy alone hides both.
:::

### Exercise 3: Pick the threshold

Each system gives a score from 0 to 1 and acts when the score is above a threshold. For each, would you choose **low** (around 0.2), **middle** (0.5), or **high** (around 0.9)? Give one sentence of reasoning.

1.  A school email spam filter.
2.  A smartwatch fall detector that automatically texts a family member.
3.  A party game that shouts "Dog!" when a dog walks past the camera.

:::hint
For each one, write down what a false positive costs and what a false negative costs, and who pays each cost.
:::

:::solution
1.  **High.** A false positive hides a real message, maybe an urgent one. A false negative is one more junk email. Lost real email is the worse mistake.
2.  **Low.** A missed fall can leave someone on the floor for hours. A false alarm means an "Are you okay?" text and a mildly annoyed reply. Many fall detectors also add a short countdown the wearer can cancel, which keeps the threshold low while cutting the false alarms.
3.  **Middle**, or whatever is most fun. Neither mistake hurts anyone. Not every system needs careful tuning, and recognising that is part of good judgement.
:::

### Exercise 4: The rare-disease puzzle

A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test catches 99% of people who have it, and wrongly says "positive" for 5% of healthy people. Your friend tests positive. Roughly what is the chance they actually have the disease: 99%, 50%, or about 2%?

:::hint
Imagine 100,000 people. How many have the disease, and how many of those test positive? How many are healthy, and how many of *those* test positive? Your friend is somewhere in the combined positive pile.
:::

:::solution
```python run alt="Bar chart of the 5,094 people who test positive: 99 are actually sick and 4,995 are healthy." caption="Out of 100,000 people tested, most positives are false alarms"
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

people = 100_000
sick = people // 1000                 # 100 people
healthy = people - sick               # 99,900 people
sick_positive = round(sick * 0.99)    # 99 caught
healthy_positive = round(healthy * 0.05)  # 4,995 false alarms

chance = sick_positive / (sick_positive + healthy_positive)
print(f"positive results: {sick_positive + healthy_positive}")
print(f"chance a positive person is sick: {chance:.1%}")

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3))
bars = plt.barh(["Healthy, tested positive", "Sick, tested positive"],
                [healthy_positive, sick_positive], color=["#eb6834", "#2a78d6"])
plt.bar_label(bars, padding=3)
plt.xlim(0, 6000)
plt.xlabel("People")
plt.title("Who is in the positive pile")
plt.tight_layout()
```

About **2%**. The test is good, but healthy people outnumber sick people 999 to 1, so even a small false-positive rate produces far more false alarms than true cases. This is why a positive screening result leads to a second, different test rather than a diagnosis. The same trap catches any detector hunting for something rare: cheating, fraud, a particular face in a crowd.
:::

### Exercise 5: Accuracy by group

A homework-helper app recognises spoken questions. Its makers tested 500 recordings and report "90% accurate". Here's the breakdown:

| Group | Recordings | Understood correctly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adults, native English speakers | 300 | 291 |
| Teens | 120 | 108 |
| Children under 10 | 50 | 33 |
| Adults learning English | 30 | 18 |

Compute each group's accuracy. Who is the app *for*, according to its name, and how well does it serve them?

:::hint
Divide the second column by the first for each row. Then compare the groups the app was tested on most with the groups it's meant to help.
:::

:::solution
```python run alt="Bar chart of accuracy by group: adults 97%, teens 90%, children under 10 66%, adults learning English 60%, with a dashed line at the overall 90%." caption="The 90% headline is mostly the adults"
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

groups = ["Adults\n(native)", "Teens", "Children\nunder 10", "Adults learning\nEnglish"]
recordings = [300, 120, 50, 30]
correct = [291, 108, 33, 18]
accuracy = [100 * c / r for c, r in zip(correct, recordings)]
overall = 100 * sum(correct) / sum(recordings)

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3.6))
bars = plt.bar(groups, accuracy, color="#2a78d6", width=0.6)
plt.bar_label(bars, fmt="%.0f%%", padding=3)
plt.axhline(overall, color="#555", linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
plt.text(3.4, overall + 2, f"overall {overall:.0f}%", ha="right", color="#555")
plt.ylim(0, 110)
plt.ylabel("Understood correctly (%)")
plt.tight_layout()
```

A homework helper is mostly for students, yet children under 10 get two-thirds of their questions understood and English learners only 60%. The average is high because 300 of the 500 test recordings came from the group it serves best. Real speech systems have shown this pattern: a 2020 study by Koenecke and colleagues found that speech-to-text services from five large companies made almost twice as many errors for Black speakers as for white speakers.
:::

### Exercise 6: Where did the bias come from?

Crash Course AI lists five sources of bias: data that reflects existing biases, unbalanced classes, data that doesn't capture what you actually care about, feedback loops, and deliberately bad data. Match each real case to one source.

1.  Amazon built an experimental résumé-screening model trained on résumés sent to the company over ten years, most of them from men. Reuters reported in 2018 that it learned to downgrade résumés containing the word "women's", and the company dropped it.
2.  A hospital program estimated how sick patients were from how much money had been spent on their care. Because less had historically been spent on Black patients with the same needs, it underrated how sick they were (Obermeyer and colleagues, *Science*, 2019).
3.  Microsoft's Tay chatbot (2016) learned from conversations on Twitter. Users flooded it with offensive messages, and within a day it was repeating them.
4.  A skin-condition app is trained on photos that are mostly of light skin.
5.  Police are sent where past arrests were recorded; more police there record more arrests; next year's model sends even more police there.

:::hint
For each, ask: was the data an accurate record of an unfair past, a lopsided sample, the wrong measurement, poisoned on purpose, or shaped by the model's own decisions?
:::

:::solution
1.  **Data that reflects existing biases.** The records were accurate, but they recorded a male-dominated industry, so the model learned to prefer what had been preferred.
2.  **Data that doesn't capture what you care about.** Spending was used as a stand-in for need, and the two weren't the same for every group.
3.  **Deliberately bad data.** People fed it harmful examples on purpose.
4.  **Unbalanced classes.** Too few examples of darker skin, the same problem Gender Shades found in face datasets.
5.  **Feedback loop.** The model's own output decides where the next data comes from, so its first guess keeps confirming itself.

Cases can mix sources. That's fine, as long as you can explain the main one.
:::

### Challenge: Write a fairness test plan

Your school library is thinking about a voice-controlled kiosk that answers "Where is this book?" Before anyone buys it, write a one-page test plan. Include: which groups of users to test, how many recordings per group, which conditions (noise, distance), what you'll measure, the biggest gap between groups you'd accept, and what happens when the kiosk isn't sure.

:::solution
A strong plan looks something like this. Yours can differ in the details, as long as every choice has a reason.

```text
WHO:      Students by age (11-12, 13-14, 15-18), staff, students learning English,
          students with speech differences (with their consent), soft and loud voices.
HOW MANY: At least 50 questions per group, the same 50 book titles for every group.
WHERE:    Quiet library, lunchtime noise, standing 30 cm and 1 m from the kiosk.
MEASURE:  Share of questions answered with the right shelf, per group and condition.
GAP:      No group more than 5 points below the best group. Over 5 points: don't buy yet.
NOT SURE: Below the confidence threshold, the screen shows a search box and a
          "Ask a librarian" button, never a guess. Count how often each group sees it.
REPEAT:   Re-run the test every term and after any software update.
```

The two lines people forget are the last two: measuring *who* gets the "not sure" fallback (you saw in the build-along that it can fall unevenly), and testing again after updates, because a model that passed once can change.
:::
