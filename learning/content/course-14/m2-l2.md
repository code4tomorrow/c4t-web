## Step 1: Write the rule first

On paper, finish this rule: *"If the sound is louder than ___, it's a clap."*

Now predict: which other sounds would also pass your rule? Think about a book dropping, a cough, a door closing, a knock on the desk.

Here's how one set of sounds measured on a 0 to 100 loudness scale. These are example numbers; your room would give different ones, but the pattern is the same:

```python run alt="Bar chart of loudness scores: Clap 82, Snap 66, Knock on desk 78, Cough 75, Door closing 88, Talking 58. A dashed line at 72 marks the rule; knock, cough, and door are all above it along with the clap." caption="Four sounds clear the bar, and only one of them is a clap"
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

sounds = ["Clap", "Snap", "Knock on desk", "Cough", "Door closing", "Talking"]
loudness = [82, 66, 78, 75, 88, 58]
threshold = 72

colors = ["#2a78d6" if name == "Clap" else "#b8b8b8" for name in sounds]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.4))
ax.bar(sounds, loudness, color=colors, width=0.6)
ax.axhline(threshold, color="#eb6834", linewidth=2, linestyle="--")
ax.text(5.45, threshold + 3, "Rule: louder than 72 = clap", ha="right", color="#333")
ax.set_ylabel("Loudness score")
ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
ax.set_title("Which sounds does the loudness rule call a clap?", loc="left")
fig.tight_layout()
```

Every bar above the orange line counts as a clap under the rule. The knock, the cough, and the door all pass, so the rule has three false alarms out of four alarms. Raise the line above 82 to stop them and you'd miss the clap too, while the door still gets through.

A single loudness number just doesn't carry enough information to separate these sounds. Keep that thought for Step 7.

## Step 2: Start an Audio Project and allow the microphone

1.  Go to `teachablemachine.withgoogle.com`, click **Get Started**, then **Audio Project**.
2.  The first card is already named **Background Noise**. Its note says an audio model needs at least 20 seconds of background noise, and that this class can't be deleted or disabled.
3.  Click **Mic** on that card. Your browser asks to use the microphone; allow it, exactly as you allowed the camera in Module 1. If you see "There was an error opening your microphone," the fixes are the same as for the camera, in the browser's site settings and the computer's privacy settings.

## Step 3: Record the background

Click **Record 20 Seconds** and stay quiet. Don't hold your breath or turn everything off; record the room as it normally sounds, fan and traffic included. When it finishes, click **Extract Sample**. The card fills with twenty one-second samples, each shown as a small spectrogram: time runs left to right, pitch runs bottom to top, and brighter means louder.

Why does Teachable Machine insist on this class? Because a sound model is listening all the time. Most of what it hears is nothing in particular. Without a Background Noise class, every quiet second would be forced into Clap or Snap, just like the empty room in your thumbs model.

## Step 4: Record the Clap class

1.  Rename **Class 2** to `Clap` with the pencil icon.
2.  Click **Mic**. The button reads **Record 2 Seconds**. Click it and clap once or twice inside the two seconds.
3.  Click **Extract Sample**. Each second becomes one sample.
4.  Repeat until the card shows at least 8 samples (it marks the minimum), and aim for about 20.

Vary them: some loud, some soft, some close to the microphone and some from arm's length, one hand cupped and then flat. A one-second sample with no clap in it teaches the wrong thing, so check them: click a sample to open it larger, where you can play it back and delete it.

## Step 5: Record the Snap class

Click **Add a class**, name it `Snap`, and record about 20 samples of finger snaps the same way. Can't snap? Use `Whistle` or `Tap` (tapping a pen on the desk) instead. Anything works, as long as it's a sound you can make on purpose many times.

## Step 6: Train it and test it like a skeptic

Click **Train Model** and keep the tab in front of you. When it's done, the Preview starts listening and the Output bars move as you make sounds.

Before each test, write your prediction in your journal. Then try it:

| Sound you make | You predict | Top bar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A clap | Clap | |
| A snap | Snap | |
| Saying the word "clap" | | |
| A knock on the desk | | |
| A cough | | |
| Clapping from across the room | | |

The first two should work. The interesting rows are the others, especially the knock and the cough. The model has three answers and must pick one, so a sound it never heard goes to whichever class it most resembles. A knock often lands in Clap. Saying "clap" usually doesn't, because the *word* sounds nothing like the *noise*.

Clapping from across the room tests something else: whether your samples covered distance. If it fails, you know which samples to add.

## Step 7: Why the model beats the rule

The model got a knock right that the rule got wrong. Here's why. The rule used one number: loudness. The model looks at the whole spectrogram, including how much of the sound sits in high pitches and how fast it fades. Plot just two measurements per sound and the difference shows:

```python run alt="Scatter plot. X axis is loudness, Y axis is the share of the sound that is high-pitched. Snaps cluster high on the chart, claps in the middle, knocks low. A vertical dashed line at loudness 72 cuts through all three groups." caption="The loudness rule is a vertical line; it can't separate groups stacked on top of each other"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

def sounds(loudness, high_pitch, n=15):
    return rng.normal(loudness, 6, n), np.clip(rng.normal(high_pitch, 0.06, n), 0, 1)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 4))
ax.scatter(*sounds(80, 0.55), color="#2a78d6", marker="o", label="Clap")
ax.scatter(*sounds(66, 0.82), color="#eb6834", marker="^", label="Snap")
ax.scatter(*sounds(76, 0.25), color="#1baf7a", marker="s", label="Knock")
ax.axvline(72, color="#888888", linestyle="--", linewidth=2)
ax.text(72.8, 0.02, "loudness rule", color="#555")
ax.set_xlabel("Loudness score")
ax.set_ylabel("Share of sound that is high-pitched")
ax.set_ylim(0, 1)
ax.legend(frameon=False, loc="upper right")
ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
ax.set_title("Add a second measurement and the sounds separate", loc="left")
```

Read it left to right first, the way the rule does. Claps, snaps, and knocks all spread across the dashed line, so no vertical line can split them. Now read it bottom to top. Knocks sit low (mostly low-pitched thuds), claps in the middle, snaps high. With two measurements, the groups barely overlap.

The real model uses far more than two measurements: every pitch band at every moment of the clip. And nobody had to choose them. Training found which patterns separate your classes. That's the advantage of learning from examples, and the reason it needed examples of every sound it should know.

## Same goal, different task

The model is a **classifier**: it picks one label from a fixed list. Change the question, and the task family changes with it:

-   "How many claps were in the last ten seconds?" needs a count, a number: closer to regression.
-   "Make a clapping rhythm that fits this song" needs something new: generation.
-   "Which clapping games would this kid like?" needs a ranked list for one person: recommendation.

The microphone and the sounds are the same in all four. What you want back decides the task.

:::quiz
Q: Why does Teachable Machine require a Background Noise class in every audio project?
- [ ] To measure how loud your microphone is
  It isn't a calibration step. It's a class like any other.
- [x] So ordinary room sound has its own answer instead of being forced into Clap or Snap
  The model listens constantly, and most seconds contain no clap or snap. Without this class, silence would always be labeled as one of them.
- [ ] Because the model can't train with only two classes
  Two classes can train fine. The requirement is about what the model hears between your sounds.

Q: You recorded every clap with your hands right next to the laptop. What should you expect when you clap from across the room?
- [ ] The same result, because a clap is a clap
  To the model, a distant clap is quieter and echoier. It only knows the claps you showed it.
- [x] Weaker or wrong predictions, because no training sample sounded like that
  The fix is to add clap samples recorded from farther away.
- [ ] Better results, because the sound has more room to spread
  More echo makes it less like your training samples, not more.

Q: A dog barks and the Clap bar jumps to 80%. What's the most sensible fix?
- [ ] Raise the microphone volume
  Volume won't give the model a correct answer to choose for a bark.
- [ ] Train for more epochs
  More training on the same three classes still leaves no class for barks.
- [x] Add an "Other sounds" class with barks, coughs, knocks, and talking
  The model can only answer with the classes it has. Giving it an "other" answer, with varied examples, is the fix.
:::

:::takeaways
- A loudness rule fails because one number can't separate sounds that differ in pitch and shape.
- A trained sound model looks at the whole spectrogram and finds the patterns that separate your classes.
- Background Noise gives the model an answer for "nothing happening".
- Sounds the model never heard are forced into the closest class, so test with them on purpose.
- The task family comes from the answer you want: the same sounds can feed classification, regression, generation, or recommendation.
:::
