## Warm-up: what would you check first?

> "A 2021 Stanford study found that 64% of teenagers use AI chatbots every day, according to lead researcher Dr. Ana Ruiz."

Which parts of this sentence would you check, and in what order?

:::solution
This sentence was invented for the exercise, and it's built the way convincing false claims often are. Check the **researcher and the study** first: search for "Ana Ruiz" with "Stanford" and "chatbots". If no such person or study turns up, the rest collapses. Then check the **number** (64%) and the **year**. 2021 is also a clue: widely used chatbots like ChatGPT only launched at the end of 2022, so a 2021 study of daily teen chatbot use is unlikely to exist.
:::

### Exercise 1: SIFT a viral post

A friend shares a screenshot of what looks like a news site headline: *"Famous singer says teens should be banned from the internet."* It has 50,000 shares. Write what you'd do at each SIFT step, including the exact searches you'd type.

:::hint
A screenshot strips away the link, so you can't click through to the source. That changes what "Investigate the source" and "Trace to the original" look like.
:::

:::solution
1.  **Stop.** The headline is designed to make you angry. Don't share it yet.
2.  **Investigate the source.** Which news site does the screenshot claim to be from? Go to that site directly (type its address yourself) and search for the headline there.
3.  **Find better coverage.** Search the claim, not the screenshot: `[singer's name] teens banned internet`. A real statement this extreme would be covered by many outlets. Also search a fact-check site, such as Google Fact Check Explorer.
4.  **Trace to the original.** Look for the original interview or post where the singer supposedly said it. If every result points back to the same screenshot, treat it as unconfirmed, and say so if you reply to your friend.

Screenshots of headlines are easy to fake. When the only evidence is the screenshot, the claim isn't verified.
:::

### Exercise 2: Plan for a voice-clone scam

A family friend gets a panicked call that sounds exactly like their son, saying he's been in a car accident and needs money sent right now. Write a three-rule plan a family could agree on *before* this happens.

:::hint
The Arup case was cracked by checking through another channel. What makes a check like that hard for a scammer to get around?
:::

:::solution
A good plan:

1.  **Hang up and call back** on the number you already have saved, or call another family member who'd know.
2.  **Agree on a family code word** that's never posted online. Anyone asking for money in an emergency must say it.
3.  **Urgency plus unusual payment is a red flag.** Gift cards, crypto, wire transfers, or "don't tell anyone" mean stop and verify, every time.

The plan works because it doesn't depend on hearing whether the voice is fake. A cloned voice can be perfect; a scammer still can't answer from your son's real phone or know a word that was never written down.
:::

### Exercise 3: Rank chatbot tasks by risk

Rank these from safest to riskiest, and say why:

1.  Brainstorm ten names for a robotics team.
2.  Summarize a paragraph you pasted in.
3.  List five books with page numbers to cite in a history essay.
4.  Tell you how much of a medicine to give a 6-year-old.

:::hint
Consider two things for each: how likely the chatbot is to invent something, and how bad it is if it does.
:::

:::solution
From safest to riskiest: **1, 2, 3, 4.**

-   **Names** have no right answer, so nothing can be false.
-   **Summarizing your own text** gives the model the facts, so it invents less, but it can still drop a "not" or blur a number. Skim it against the original.
-   **Citations with page numbers** are the most frequently invented output of all, as *Mata v. Avianca* showed. Every one needs checking.
-   **A child's medicine dose** combines a real chance of error with serious harm. Use the package label, a pharmacist, or a doctor, not a chatbot.

Risk is likelihood times harm, and number 4 is the only one where both are high.
:::

### Exercise 4: The plant-watering idea

A classmate wants to train a machine learning model on photos of a houseplant to predict when it needs water, then send a reminder. Run the five questions. Does AI fit?

:::hint
Think about how a gardener actually decides when to water. Is there a simpler signal than a photo?
:::

:::solution
| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Task | Fairly clear: dry or not dry. |
| Data | Needs many labelled photos of the same plant, wet and dry; that's weeks of collecting. |
| Stakes | Low. A wrong guess means a slightly thirsty plant. |
| Errors | Easy to notice and fix. |
| Alternatives | A repeating reminder every few days, or a cheap soil moisture sensor, which measures dryness directly. |

**Verdict:** not a good fit. A photo is an indirect clue about something you can measure directly. It's a fine *learning* project, but as a solution, a sensor or a calendar reminder beats it. "Could AI do this?" and "should AI do this?" are different questions.
:::

### Exercise 5: The essay detector, by the numbers

A teacher runs 300 essays through a detector with the same accuracy OpenAI reported for its 2023 classifier: it flags 26% of AI-written essays and wrongly flags 9% of human-written ones. Suppose 30 of the essays were really AI-written. How many students get flagged, and how many of them did nothing wrong?

:::hint
Work with the two groups separately: 30 AI-written essays and 270 human-written ones. Apply the right percentage to each, then add up the flags.
:::

:::solution
```python run alt="Stacked bar chart of the 32 flagged essays: 8 actually AI-written and 24 written by students themselves." caption="Three out of four flagged students wrote their own essay"
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

ai_essays = 30
human_essays = 300 - ai_essays
caught = round(ai_essays * 0.26)          # AI essays flagged
falsely_flagged = round(human_essays * 0.09)  # honest students flagged

flagged = caught + falsely_flagged
print(f"flagged: {flagged} students")
print(f"actually used AI: {caught}")
print(f"wrote it themselves: {falsely_flagged} ({falsely_flagged / flagged:.0%} of those flagged)")
print(f"AI essays not caught: {ai_essays - caught}")

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 2.6))
plt.barh(["Flagged essays"], [caught], color="#2a78d6", label=f"Used AI ({caught})")
plt.barh(["Flagged essays"], [falsely_flagged], left=[caught], color="#eb6834",
         label=f"Wrote it themselves ({falsely_flagged})")
plt.xlim(0, 35)
plt.xlabel("Students")
plt.legend(frameon=False, loc="upper center", bbox_to_anchor=(0.5, -0.35), ncol=2)
plt.tight_layout()
```

32 students are flagged and 24 of them wrote their own essays, while 22 of the 30 AI essays slip through. The detector is wrong about most of the people it accuses *and* misses most of what it's looking for. This is the rare-disease puzzle from Module 5 again: when the thing you're hunting is a minority, even a modest false-positive rate buries the real cases under false alarms.
:::

### Exercise 6: Score three proposals

For each proposal, give a short answer to each of the five questions and a verdict: **go**, **go with conditions**, or **don't**.

1.  Sort photos of lost-and-found items into categories (jackets, bottles, phones) and post them on the school website.
2.  Predict which ninth graders will drop out, so counselors can reach out early.
3.  A chatbot that answers students' questions about the school handbook.

:::hint
For number 2, a real system already exists: Wisconsin's Dropout Early Warning System. A 2023 investigation by The Markup found that when it predicted a student wouldn't graduate on time, it was wrong nearly three-quarters of the time, and wrong more often for Black and Hispanic students than for white students.
:::

:::solution
**1. Lost-and-found sorter: go with conditions.** The task is clear, the data is easy to collect, mistakes are cheap (a jacket filed under "bags"), and a person posts the photos. Conditions: blur or crop names, ID cards, and phone lock screens before posting, since the items often carry personal information.

**2. Dropout prediction: don't, as proposed.** The task sounds clear but the label (who drops out) depends on support students receive. Historical data reflects existing inequality. The stakes are high: a "high risk" label can change how adults treat a student. The Wisconsin case shows the errors are frequent and fall unevenly. Alternatives exist: counselors already watch attendance, grades, and behavior, which are direct and explainable. If a school insists, conditions would include never showing the score to teachers, testing error rates by group first, and using it only to *offer* help, never to restrict anything.

**3. Handbook chatbot: go with conditions.** The task has right answers (they're in the handbook), but chatbots invent rules, as Air Canada learned in court. Conditions: answers must quote and link the exact handbook section, the bot says "I don't know, ask the office" when the handbook doesn't cover something, and a person reviews a sample of answers each month. Also ask whether a well-organized, searchable handbook page would do the job with no AI at all.
:::

### Challenge: Judge an AI feature you've used

Choose an AI feature you've actually used (autocorrect, a music or video recommendation feed, photo face grouping, a homework helper). Write one paragraph that answers the five questions and ends with a verdict: does AI fit this job, and what one change would make it safer or fairer?

:::solution
A model answer for **photo face grouping** (a phone's photo app that groups pictures by person):

The task is clear: decide whether two faces are the same person. The data is my own photo library, but the faces in it belong to friends and family who never agreed to be grouped. Stakes are low for me (a wrong group is easy to fix), and errors are visible and correctable by hand. The alternative, tagging people manually, works but takes much longer. **Verdict: AI fits**, because errors are cheap, visible, and fixable by the user. One change that would make it better: keep face grouping on the device and off by default, so people's faces aren't processed until the owner chooses it.

Your paragraph should name a real trade-off and a specific change. "It's good" or "it's creepy" without reasons isn't a verdict.
:::
