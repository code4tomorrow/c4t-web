In February 2023, Google posted a short ad for its new chatbot, Bard. Someone asked what to tell a 9-year-old about the James Webb Space Telescope, and Bard answered with three facts. One of them said the telescope took the very first pictures of a planet outside our solar system. It didn't: the first such image came in 2004, from the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope in Chile. Astronomers pointed it out on social media, Reuters reported it, and Alphabet lost roughly $100 billion in market value the day the story spread.

The answer was confident, well written, and wrong. Nothing about its tone gave the mistake away. That's the problem this lesson is about.

## Why chatbots make things up

A large language model is trained to do one thing: given some text, predict what word comes next. It learns this from an enormous amount of writing. When you ask it a question, it writes an answer one word at a time, each time choosing a likely next word.

"Likely" means *likely to appear in text like this*, not *true*. Most of the time those overlap, which is why chatbots are so useful. When they don't overlap, you get a **hallucination**: a fluent answer with invented facts, quotes, or sources. The right answer and the wrong one come out of exactly the same process, in the same confident voice.

Here's a toy version. It isn't how Bard works inside, but it shows the core idea: the toy picks whichever word followed "taken by" most often in its training text.

:::trace Watch the toy model pick the most common word, not the true one
```python
# How often each word followed "taken by" in some made-up training text
counts = {"Webb": 70, "Hubble": 25, "VLT": 5}

best_word = ""
best_count = 0
for word, count in counts.items():
    if count > best_count:
        best_word = word
        best_count = count

print("The first picture of an exoplanet was taken by", best_word)
```
:::

The true answer, VLT, was in the training text, but rarely. In news coverage, the James Webb telescope is mentioned far more often alongside exoplanet pictures, so it wins. Real models are vastly more sophisticated, and they're also trained to be helpful and to admit uncertainty, but they can still produce the popular-sounding answer over the correct one.

Two real cases show what this costs:

-   **Fake court cases (2023).** Two New York lawyers filed a brief in *Mata v. Avianca* that cited earlier court decisions found with ChatGPT. The judge wrote that six of them appeared to be "bogus judicial decisions with bogus quotes and bogus internal citations." The lawyers had even asked ChatGPT whether the cases were real, and it said yes. The judge fined them $5,000.
-   **An invented refund rule (2024).** Air Canada's website chatbot told a passenger, Jake Moffatt, he could claim a bereavement discount after his flight. The airline's actual policy said otherwise. A Canadian tribunal ruled that Air Canada is responsible for everything on its own website, chatbot included, and ordered it to pay.

## Deepfakes: when seeing and hearing aren't enough

A **deepfake** is audio, an image, or video made or altered by AI to show someone saying or doing something they didn't. Three real incidents:

-   **March 2022.** During the Russian invasion, a video of Ukraine's President Zelensky telling soldiers to surrender appeared on a hacked Ukrainian news website. It was crude (the head looked pasted on), and Meta and YouTube removed it the same day.
-   **January 2024.** Before the New Hampshire primary, thousands of voters got a robocall in an AI-cloned voice of President Biden telling them not to vote in the primary. The Federal Communications Commission later fined the political consultant behind it $6 million.
-   **Early 2024.** A finance employee at the engineering firm Arup in Hong Kong joined a video call with what looked like the company's chief financial officer and several colleagues. Every one of them was a deepfake. The employee made 15 transfers totalling about US$25 million.

Notice the trend. The 2022 fake was easy to spot; the 2024 call fooled a trained professional. Advice like "look for weird hands or blinking" ages quickly, because each new generator fixes the last one's tells. Checking *where something came from* lasts far longer than checking *how it looks*.

## How to check: SIFT

The researcher Mike Caulfield's **SIFT** method is four moves you can make in a couple of minutes:

1.  **Stop.** Notice the strong feeling (shock, anger, delight) and don't share yet.
2.  **Investigate the source.** Who posted it? Look them up in a new tab rather than trusting their own bio.
3.  **Find better coverage.** Search for the claim itself. If a president really surrendered, every major news outlet would have it.
4.  **Trace to the original.** Find the earliest version of the image, clip, or quote, and see its full context.

For media, a few tools help with steps 3 and 4:

-   **Reverse image search** (Google Lens, TinEye) finds where else an image appears and often its oldest copy.
-   **Content Credentials** are a signed record some cameras and AI tools attach, saying how an image was made or edited. You can check a file at contentcredentials.org/verify. A credential is useful evidence; a missing one proves nothing, since most images have none.
-   **A second channel.** For a call or voice message asking for money or secrets, hang up and contact the person on a number you already know. That's how the Arup fraud was finally discovered, too late.

```mermaid caption="Checking a claim, image, or clip before you trust or share it"
flowchart TD
    A[Strong reaction to a post] --> B[Stop: don't share yet]
    B --> C[Who posted it? Look them up]
    C --> D[Do trusted outlets report it?]
    D -->|Yes| E[Trace to the original context]
    D -->|No| F[Treat as unconfirmed]
    E --> G{Matches the claim?}
    G -->|Yes| H[Share with the source]
    G -->|No| F
```

## Does AI even fit this problem?

The same skepticism applies to AI proposals. Before asking "which model?", ask whether a model belongs there at all:

1.  **Task.** Is there a clear right answer you could check? "Is this a cat?" has one. "Is this student trustworthy?" doesn't.
2.  **Data.** Is there enough data, does it represent everyone who'll be affected, and was it collected with consent?
3.  **Stakes.** When it's wrong, who is hurt and how badly? A bad song recommendation and a wrong school suspension are not the same.
4.  **Errors.** Will a person catch mistakes before they matter? Can someone affected appeal to a human?
5.  **Alternatives.** Would a simple rule, a form, a checklist, or a person do the job as well, more cheaply, or more fairly?

A real example: in January 2023, OpenAI released a classifier to detect AI-written text. By its own testing it caught 26% of AI-written text and wrongly flagged 9% of human-written text as AI. Run it through the questions. The task is fuzzy (people edit AI drafts and AI edits people's), the stakes are high (an accusation of cheating), errors are hard to catch (how do you prove you wrote something?), and alternatives exist (drafts, in-class writing, conversations about the work). OpenAI withdrew the tool in July 2023, citing its low accuracy.

:::warning
**Three verification mistakes**

1.  **Asking the chatbot if it's right.** It will usually say yes, as it did for the lawyers in *Mata v. Avianca*. Check claims against sources outside the chatbot.
2.  **"I didn't see any glitches, so it's real."** Good fakes don't have visible glitches. Check the source and the original, not the pixels.
3.  **Using AI because it's available.** "We have an AI tool" isn't a reason. Start from the problem and the five questions.
:::

:::quiz
Q: A chatbot gives you a quote from a scientist, with a book title and page number. What should you do before using it?
- [ ] Trust it, because the page number shows it came from a real source
  Specific details are easy to generate. Invented citations often look exactly this precise.
- [ ] Ask the chatbot whether the quote is accurate
  The chatbot's confirmation comes from the same process that may have invented it.
- [x] Find the book or another reliable source that contains the quote
  Tracing a claim to its original is the only reliable check.

Q: Why did the toy model in the trace answer "Webb"?
- [x] "Webb" followed those words most often in its training text
  It chose the most frequent continuation. Frequency in text isn't the same as truth.
- [ ] It looked up the answer and found Webb
  It didn't look anything up. It only compared counts.
- [ ] "Webb" comes first in the dictionary
  The loop compares counts, not alphabetical order.

Q: Your aunt gets a voice message that sounds exactly like your cousin, asking for money for an emergency. What's the best first step?
- [ ] Listen again for robotic sounds
  Cloned voices can sound natural. The absence of glitches proves nothing.
- [x] Call the cousin, or another family member, on a number she already has
  Checking through a second channel you trust defeats almost every voice-clone scam.
- [ ] Send a small amount first to test
  Any payment rewards the scam. Verify before acting.

Q: A school wants AI to decide which students get a scholarship. Which question from the checklist raises the biggest concern?
- [ ] Data: there might not be enough applications
  Possibly, but the deeper issue is what happens to a student when the model is wrong.
- [x] Stakes and errors: a wrong decision changes a student's future, and they need a human to appeal to
  High-stakes decisions about people need human judgement and a real appeal, not just a score.
- [ ] Task: scholarships are hard to type into a computer
  Entering data is easy. The concern is the decision itself.
:::

:::takeaways
- Language models predict likely text, so a fluent, confident answer can be false. Check facts, quotes, and citations against outside sources.
- Deepfakes have moved from crude videos to a convincing multi-person video call. Check where media came from, not just how it looks.
- SIFT: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace to the original.
- For calls asking for money or secrets, verify through a second channel you already trust.
- Before using AI, ask about the task, data, stakes, errors, and alternatives. Sometimes the answer is "don't."
:::
