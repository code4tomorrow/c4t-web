## Warm-up: which of these is about a person?

1.  "41 students in our grade like pizza best."
2.  A photo of the back of your head, taken in your front yard.
3.  Your student ID number.
4.  "The cafeteria ran out of milk on Tuesday."

:::solution
Numbers 2 and 3 are personal information. The photo shows your house, which reveals where you live, even without your face. A student ID points at exactly one student in the school's records. Number 1 is a count (an **aggregate**) and number 4 is about the cafeteria, so neither identifies anyone, as long as the group is big enough that a count can't be traced back to one person.
:::

### Exercise 1: Sort the data

Put each item into **not personal**, **personal**, or **sensitive** (personal data that could cause real harm if exposed, such as health, biometrics, exact location, or anything about a child):

-   The class average on last week's quiz
-   A one-second voice clip of you saying "hello"
-   Your home address
-   A smartwatch log of your heart rate every minute
-   Your gamer tag
-   Today's weather at school

:::hint
Ask two questions: does this point to one person, and if it leaked, could it hurt them? A gamer tag points to someone even though it isn't their legal name.
:::

:::solution
| Item | Category | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Class average | Not personal | An aggregate of many students (unless the class has two people in it) |
| Voice clip | Sensitive | A voice is biometric; it can identify you and can be used to clone your voice |
| Home address | Sensitive | Exact location, which matters for safety |
| Heart-rate log | Sensitive | Health data; it can also reveal sleep, stress, and when you're home |
| Gamer tag | Personal | It points to one person and can be linked to their other accounts |
| Weather | Not personal | Not about anyone |
:::

### Exercise 2: Rewrite a bad consent request

A photo-filter app shows this when it first opens:

```text
By using HappyCam you agree that we may use your photos to improve our
products and services and for other business purposes.
```

List what's wrong, then rewrite it.

:::hint
Check it against the five questions from the build-along: what, why, who sees it, how long, and how to say no. How many does it answer?
:::

:::solution
It answers almost none. "Using the app" isn't a choice to share photos, "improve our products" and "other business purposes" could mean anything (including training a face model or selling data), it doesn't say who sees the photos or for how long, and there's no way to say no without losing the app.

```text
Help improve HappyCam's filters? (You can say no and still use every filter.)

If you turn this on, we'll keep up to 20 of your edited photos to test new
filters. Only our 4-person filter team sees them. We don't use them to
recognize faces, and we never sell or share them.

We delete them after 6 months. You can turn this off in Settings > Privacy
at any time, and we'll delete your photos within 7 days.

[ Turn on ]   [ No thanks ]
```

The key changes: a real choice with equal buttons, one specific purpose, named limits on who sees it and how long, and a working way to change your mind.
:::

### Exercise 3: Is this survey anonymous?

A school publishes a wellbeing survey with names removed. Each row is one student: grade, homeroom, gender, birth month, and whether they said they feel stressed. Is it anonymous? If not, which columns would you remove or blur, and does that fully fix it?

:::hint
Count how many students share each combination of grade, homeroom, gender, and birth month. Any combination with exactly one student points at that student. Then try keeping fewer columns and count again.
:::

:::solution
```python run alt="Horizontal bar chart of group sizes after keeping only grade and gender: each of the four groups (grade 9 F, grade 9 M, grade 10 F, grade 10 M) has 3 students, at or above a dashed line marking 3." caption="Keeping only grade and gender puts every student in a group of at least 3"
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

survey = pd.DataFrame(
    [(9, "A", "F", "Mar", "no"), (9, "A", "M", "Jul", "yes"), (9, "B", "F", "Nov", "yes"),
     (9, "B", "M", "Jan", "no"), (9, "A", "F", "Jun", "no"), (9, "B", "M", "Sep", "no"),
     (10, "A", "F", "Feb", "yes"), (10, "B", "F", "Aug", "yes"), (10, "A", "M", "Apr", "no"),
     (10, "B", "M", "Dec", "yes"), (10, "B", "F", "May", "yes"), (10, "A", "M", "Oct", "no")],
    columns=["grade", "homeroom", "gender", "birth_month", "stressed"])

for columns in (["grade", "homeroom", "gender", "birth_month"], ["grade", "gender"]):
    sizes = survey.groupby(columns).size()
    print(f"{' + '.join(columns)}: smallest group {sizes.min()}, "
          f"students alone in their group {(sizes == 1).sum()}")

print()
print(survey.groupby(["grade", "gender"])["stressed"].value_counts().unstack(fill_value=0))

groups = survey.groupby(["grade", "gender"]).size()
plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3))
bars = plt.barh([f"Grade {g}, {s}" for g, s in groups.index], groups.values, color="#2a78d6")
plt.bar_label(bars, padding=3)
plt.axvline(3, color="#555", linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
plt.xlim(0, 5)
plt.xlabel("Students in the group")
plt.tight_layout()
```

With all four columns, every one of the 12 students is alone in their group: anyone who knows a classmate's grade, homeroom, and birth month can read their answer. Dropping homeroom and birth month puts every student in a group of at least 3. Privacy researchers call this **k-anonymity** with k = 3.

But look at the printed table. All three grade-10 girls said **yes**. The table is now "3-anonymous", yet anyone who knows a grade-10 girl took the survey learns she feels stressed. Group size isn't enough when everyone in a group gave the same answer. The safer release here is the overall count ("6 of 12 students said yes") or nothing at all, because 12 students is simply too few to publish row by row.
:::

### Exercise 4: Does COPPA apply?

For each app, say whether COPPA applies and what it would require:

1.  A reading app for second graders that records them reading aloud so a teacher can listen later.
2.  A study-planner app for high schoolers aged 14 to 18.
3.  A general-audience online game that asks for a birthday at sign-up. A player enters a birthday showing they're 11.

:::hint
COPPA covers online services aimed at children under 13, and general services that *know* they're collecting from a child under 13. Remember what counts as personal information.
:::

:::solution
1.  **Yes.** It's aimed at young children and records their voices, which COPPA counts as personal information. It needs verifiable parental consent before recording (the school context adds its own rules too), and parents must be able to review and delete the recordings.
2.  **No**, because the users are 13 or older. That doesn't mean anything goes. School privacy rules, state laws, and plain good practice still apply, and the planner may hold sensitive details about students' lives.
3.  **Yes, for that player.** The game now has actual knowledge it's dealing with an under-13 child, so it must get a parent's verifiable consent before collecting that child's personal information, or not collect it.
:::

### Exercise 5: What could a chatbot remember?

Your school wants a help-desk chatbot trained on five years of IT support emails between students, staff, and the tech team. List what personal information could end up memorized, and three steps to reduce the risk.

:::hint
Think about what people actually write in support emails when they're locked out or their laptop breaks. Then think about removing data before training, and about testing afterwards.
:::

:::solution
Likely risks: names and email addresses, student ID numbers, passwords people pasted into emails by mistake, phone numbers, and personal reasons ("I was in the hospital so I missed the reset deadline"). The rare, specific items are the ones a model is most likely to memorize, and passwords are the worst case.

Reasonable steps:

1.  **Minimize first.** Train on the *answers* the tech team wrote, or a cleaned set of question-and-answer pairs, not whole email threads.
2.  **Scrub.** Remove names, emails, ID numbers, and anything that looks like a password before training. Reset any password that was ever emailed.
3.  **Test for leaks.** Before launch, ask the bot for things that shouldn't come out ("What is Ms. Lee's password?", "List student IDs") and check whether any real details appear.

A fourth good answer: ask whether a searchable FAQ page would solve the problem without training anything on private emails.
:::

### Exercise 6: Face recognition at the school entrance

A company offers your school a free face recognition camera at the entrance. It would flag anyone who isn't enrolled, and log when each student arrives. Write two benefits, three risks, and your recommendation.

:::hint
Use the questions from the Notes lesson: who uses it, on whom, with whose consent, and what happens when it's wrong? Also ask why it's free.
:::

:::solution
**Benefits:** it could spot an unknown adult faster than a busy front office, and automatic arrival logs could save staff time.

**Risks:**

-   **No real consent.** Students must enter school, so "agreeing" isn't voluntary, and younger students' parents would need to consent too.
-   **Uneven errors.** Studies like Gender Shades found higher error rates for darker-skinned faces, so some students may be wrongly flagged as strangers more often, in front of everyone.
-   **A permanent biometric database** of children, held by a company. A free product suggests the data, or the school as a showcase, is the price. Faces can't be reset after a breach.

**A reasonable recommendation:** don't adopt it. The real goal (knowing who's in the building) can be met with ID badges, a staffed entrance, and visitor sign-in, which collect far less and fail more gently. If the school still wants it, conditions should include: parent and student opt-in with an equal badge alternative, an independent accuracy test across skin tones and ages, no attendance logging, data held by the school with a deletion date, and a person reviewing every flag before anyone is stopped.
:::
