### Exercise 1: Spot the leak

Each team reports a great test score. For each, find the reason the score is too good.

1.  A face-unlock model is trained on 20 photos of each of 50 students. The 1,000 photos are shuffled and split 80/20 into training and test.
2.  A dataset of 5,000 dog photos was collected from the web. Nobody removed duplicates, and many popular photos appear several times.
3.  A model predicts tomorrow's cafeteria attendance. One feature is "number of lunches sold", taken from the same day being predicted.
4.  A student tunes a model by checking its test score after each of 30 changes and keeps the best version.
5.  A chest X-ray model is trained and tested on scans from two hospitals. Very sick patients were mostly scanned at Hospital B, which stamps its name in the corner of every scan.

:::hint
For each, ask: what does the test set share with the training set, or what does the model know that it couldn't know in real use?
:::

:::solution
1.  **The same faces are in both sets.** Each student has about 16 photos in training and 4 in test, so the test asks "do you recognize photos of people you've already seen?" For a model that must work on new people, split by student.
2.  **Duplicates cross the split.** A photo in the test set may have an identical copy in training. Remove duplicates before splitting.
3.  **A feature from the future.** Lunches sold on the day is only known after the day, and it's practically the answer. It wouldn't exist when the prediction is needed.
4.  **The test set became a validation set.** Picking the best of 30 versions by test score selects the version that happened to suit those particular test examples. A fresh test set is needed for an honest number.
5.  **A shortcut.** The model can learn "Hospital B's stamp means sick" instead of reading the lungs. It would score well on scans from these two hospitals and fail at a hospital with a different stamp. Researchers have found real X-ray models picking up which hospital a scan came from, so this is a real risk, not a made-up one.
:::

### Exercise 2: Read the curves

Three models were trained for 50 epochs. Blue is accuracy on the training samples, orange on the test samples.

```python run alt="Three line charts of accuracy over 50 epochs. Model A: both training and test accuracy rise slowly and level off around 0.55 to 0.6. Model B: both rise to about 0.9, staying close together. Model C: training accuracy climbs to 1.0 while test accuracy peaks near 0.8 around epoch 12 and then slowly falls to about 0.7." caption="Which model is underfit, which is overfit, and which is healthy?"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
epochs = np.arange(1, 51)

def curve(start, end, speed):
    return end - (end - start) * np.exp(-epochs / speed)

runs = {
    "Model A": (curve(0.35, 0.58, 8), curve(0.33, 0.55, 8)),
    "Model B": (curve(0.35, 0.93, 6), curve(0.33, 0.89, 7)),
    "Model C": (curve(0.35, 1.00, 5), curve(0.33, 0.84, 5) - 0.004 * np.maximum(epochs - 12, 0)),
}

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11, 3.4), sharey=True)
for ax, (name, (train, test)) in zip(axes, runs.items()):
    ax.plot(epochs, np.clip(train + rng.normal(0, 0.008, 50), 0, 1), color="#2a78d6", linewidth=2, label="training")
    ax.plot(epochs, np.clip(test + rng.normal(0, 0.012, 50), 0, 1), color="#eb6834", linewidth=2, label="test")
    ax.set_title(name, loc="left")
    ax.set_xlabel("Epoch")
    ax.set_ylim(0.2, 1.05)
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
axes[0].set_ylabel("Accuracy")
axes[0].legend(frameon=False, loc="lower right")
fig.tight_layout()
```

1.  Label each model: underfit, overfit, or healthy.
2.  For the overfit model, roughly when should training have stopped?
3.  Suggest one change for the underfit model.

:::hint
Look at two things: how high the training line gets, and the size of the gap between the lines.
:::

:::solution
1.  **Model A is underfit**: even on its own training samples it only reaches about 58%. It can't capture the pattern. **Model B is healthy**: both lines are high and close. **Model C is overfit**: training accuracy hits 100% while test accuracy peaks and then slides down.
2.  Around **epoch 12**, where the orange line peaks. Every epoch after that makes the model better at its training samples and worse at new ones. Stopping at the best validation score is a standard technique called **early stopping**.
3.  For A: give the model more to work with. Better features or examples (classes that actually look different), a more flexible model, or more training if the lines are still rising. Training longer alone won't help much here, since both lines have flattened.
:::

### Exercise 3: Split by person

A club is building a Teachable Machine model that recognizes five sign-language letters. Thirty students each recorded 20 photos of each letter: 3,000 photos. The model must work for students who join next year.

Describe how to split the photos into training, validation, and test sets.

:::hint
Who is the model for? The test set should contain the kind of examples the model will meet in real use.
:::

:::solution
**Split by student, not by photo.** For example: photos from 20 students for training, 5 students for validation, and 5 students for the test set. Each student's photos go entirely into one set.

If you split photos randomly, every student's hands appear in all three sets, and the test score measures "recognizing hands it has already seen". Next year's students are new people, so the test set must be made of people the model has never seen. It's also worth checking that the 5 test students differ in ways that matter: hand size, skin tone, left- and right-handedness, and the rooms they recorded in.
:::

### Exercise 4: A neuron, by hand

A neuron decides whether to bring an umbrella. It has three inputs, three weights, and a bias:

| Input | Weight |
| --- | --- |
| Cloudy now (0 or 1) | 1.0 |
| Forecast chance of rain (0 to 1) | 4.0 |
| Walking more than 10 minutes (0 or 1) | 1.5 |
| Bias | −3.0 |

Total = (each input × its weight, added up) + bias. If the total is above 0, bring the umbrella.

1.  Morning 1: cloudy, 30% rain forecast, long walk. Umbrella or not?
2.  Morning 2: clear sky, 60% rain forecast, short walk. Umbrella or not?
3.  On Morning 2 it poured and you got soaked. During training, which weight should get the biggest nudge, and in which direction?

:::hint
For Morning 2, two of the three inputs are 0. A weight multiplied by an input of 0 had no effect on the decision.
:::

:::solution
```python run
weights = {"cloudy": 1.0, "rain chance": 4.0, "long walk": 1.5}
bias = -3.0

mornings = {
    "Morning 1": {"cloudy": 1, "rain chance": 0.3, "long walk": 1},
    "Morning 2": {"cloudy": 0, "rain chance": 0.6, "long walk": 0},
}
for name, inputs in mornings.items():
    total = sum(inputs[key] * weights[key] for key in weights) + bias
    decision = "umbrella" if total > 0 else "no umbrella"
    print(f"{name}: total = {total:.1f} -> {decision}")
```

1.  **Umbrella.** 1×1.0 + 0.3×4.0 + 1×1.5 − 3.0 = 0.7, which is above 0.
2.  **No umbrella.** 0 + 0.6×4.0 + 0 − 3.0 = −0.6, below 0.
3.  **Increase the rain-chance weight** (and possibly make the bias less negative). Only the rain-chance input was non-zero, so it's the only weight that contributed to the wrong answer. Cloudy and long walk were 0, so changing their weights wouldn't have changed Morning 2's total at all. That's why, in the nudge rule from the trace in Lesson 1 (`w = w - rate * error * km`), the nudge is multiplied by the input: inputs that were zero get no blame.
:::

### Exercise 5: Three learning rates

The same one-weight delivery model from Lesson 1 was trained three times, with learning rates 0.002, 0.02, and 0.15. All three start with the same error. Which run used which rate?

```python run alt="Line chart of average error in minutes over 10 epochs. All three runs start at about 18. Run X climbs steeply and leaves the top of the chart by epoch 3. Run Y falls slowly, reaching about 7 by epoch 10. Run Z drops fast to nearly 0 by epoch 4." caption="Same model, same examples, three different step sizes"
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

examples = [(2, 10), (4, 20), (5, 25)]          # (km, minutes): 5 minutes per km

def train(rate, epochs=10):
    w = 0.0
    history = [sum(minutes for _, minutes in examples) / 3]   # error before any training
    for _ in range(epochs):
        for km, minutes in examples:
            w = w - rate * (w * km - minutes) * km
        history.append(sum(abs(w * km - minutes) for km, minutes in examples) / 3)
    return history

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 3.8))
for name, rate, color, marker in [("Run X", 0.15, "#2a78d6", "o"),
                                  ("Run Y", 0.002, "#eb6834", "^"),
                                  ("Run Z", 0.02, "#1baf7a", "s")]:
    ax.plot(range(0, 11), train(rate), color=color, marker=marker, linewidth=2, label=name)
ax.set_xlabel("Epoch")
ax.set_ylabel("Average error (minutes)")
ax.set_ylim(0, 60)
ax.legend(frameon=False, loc="upper right")
ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
ax.set_title("Three runs, three learning rates", loc="left")
fig.tight_layout()
```

:::hint
A tiny step size makes steady but slow progress. A huge one jumps past the answer, and each overshoot can be bigger than the last.
:::

:::solution
-   **Run Y used 0.002.** The steps are tiny, so error falls steadily but slowly: still about 7 minutes off after 10 epochs.
-   **Run Z used 0.02**, the rate from the Lesson 1 trace. Error drops to nearly zero within four epochs.
-   **Run X used 0.15.** Each nudge overshoots 5 minutes per km by more than the last, so error grows instead of shrinking. That's called **diverging**, and it's why Teachable Machine's tooltip says to be careful with the learning rate.

There's no single "right" learning rate; it depends on the model and the data. But the shapes are worth recognizing: slow and steady means too small, exploding means too big.
:::

### Exercise 6: Predict what Teachable Machine will do

For each experiment with your recycling sorter from Module 3, predict what the **Accuracy per epoch** chart will show, then explain why.

1.  Epochs set to 1.
2.  A fresh copy with only 10 samples per class, and Epochs set to 300.
3.  Learning Rate changed from 0.001 to 1.

:::hint
Use the three ideas from Lesson 1: underfitting, overfitting, and the size of each nudge.
:::

:::solution
1.  **A single point, probably low or middling.** One pass isn't enough nudging for the final layer to settle, so the model is likely underfit. (The pre-trained features are good, so it may do better than you'd guess.)
2.  **Training accuracy hits 100% early and stays there; test accuracy is jumpy.** With 10 samples per class, the 15% test bucket is only one or two samples per class, so each one is worth a huge chunk of the score. With so few examples, overfitting is likely, but the test line is too noisy to show it reliably. The fix is more (and more varied) samples, not more epochs.
3.  **Erratic or stuck accuracy, and loss that jumps around or grows.** Steps 1,000 times larger than the default overshoot, like Run X above. Some runs may still land somewhere reasonable by luck, which is exactly why you shouldn't trust one run. Click **Reset Defaults** afterward.

If you have time, try all three and compare against your predictions in your journal.
:::

### Challenge: An evaluation plan for a bus-stop camera

A school wants a model that looks at a camera photo of the bus stop and says **Bus here** or **No bus**, so students can stay inside until it arrives. Write a one-page evaluation plan:

-   Where the training examples come from, and over what period.
-   How you'll split the data so the test set is honest. Think about time, weather, and near-duplicate frames.
-   What you'll measure. Is accuracy enough, or does one kind of mistake matter more?
-   A result that would make you say "don't use this yet".

:::hint
Which is worse: the model saying "Bus here" when there's no bus, or "No bus" when the bus is there? Who pays for each mistake?
:::

:::solution
An example plan:

-   **Training data:** photos every 30 seconds for four weeks, labeled by checking the bus schedule and the photos themselves. Include rain, snow, morning darkness, and other vehicles (delivery trucks, cars) in the No bus class.
-   **Split by time, not by photo:** weeks 1 and 2 for training, week 3 for validation, week 4 as the test set. Splitting randomly would put frames 30 seconds apart on both sides of the split. Splitting by week also tests whether the model handles weather it didn't train on. If week 4 has no snow, say so in the report rather than claiming it works in snow.
-   **Measure each mistake separately:** "No bus" when the bus is there is the costly mistake, because students miss the bus. Report how often that happens, separately from overall accuracy. A model that's 97% accurate but misses one bus a week isn't good enough.
-   **Don't-use-yet rule:** if the test week shows even one missed bus at a time students were waiting, or if the model confuses delivery trucks with buses more than occasionally, keep a person checking, or add the bus company's GPS feed (a non-AI option that might work better on its own).
:::
