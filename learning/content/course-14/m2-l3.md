title: "Practice: rules or learning, which task, and what a chatbot can't promise"
summary: Sort real systems into rules or learned models, name their task families, reframe one goal four ways, and judge claims people make about chatbots.
---

:::objectives
- Decide quickly whether a system's decisions were written by people or learned from examples.
- Name the task family of everyday AI products from the shape of their answers.
- Turn one real-world goal into classification, regression, clustering, and recommendation versions.
- Judge common claims about chatbots using what you know about how they generate text.
- Argue when a plain rule is a better choice than machine learning.
:::

These exercises are for thinking, not typing. Write your answers in your journal before opening a solution; the point is to find out where your picture of AI is fuzzy, and you can't do that if you peek.

Keep this flowchart open. It's the question order that works for almost any system:

```mermaid caption="Two questions name almost any system: who decided, and what comes back?"
flowchart TD
    A{Did a person write the decision?} -->|yes| B[Rule-based]
    A -->|no, learned from examples| C{What does it give back?}
    C -->|one label| D[Classification]
    C -->|a number| E[Regression]
    C -->|unlabeled groups| F[Clustering]
    C -->|ranked list for one person| G[Recommendation]
    C -->|something new| H[Generation]
```

## Warm-up

For each answer, say whether it's a label, a number, or a ranked list: "Rain" / "73%" / "5 videos, best first" / "Golden retriever" / "$212,000".

:::solution
"Rain" is a label. "73%" is a number (though it could be a confidence attached to a label; Module 5 untangles that). "5 videos, best first" is a ranked list. "Golden retriever" is a label. "$212,000" is a number.
:::

### Exercise 1: Rule or learned?

Decide whether each system's key decision was most likely written by a person or learned from examples. Give a one-line reason.

1.  A microwave's "Popcorn" button runs for 2 minutes 30 seconds.
2.  A phone unlocks when it sees your face.
3.  The library charges 25 cents per day for late books.
4.  A photo app groups your pictures by which person is in them.
5.  A traffic light changes every 45 seconds.
6.  An email service moves messages to the junk folder.

:::hint
Ask: could you write the decision as one or two sentences that cover every case? If yes, someone probably did.
:::

:::solution
1.  **Rule.** A fixed time someone chose.
2.  **Learned.** Nobody can write rules for every face, angle, and lighting condition; face recognition is trained on examples.
3.  **Rule.** A formula: days late × $0.25.
4.  **Learned**, and specifically clustering. Nobody labeled your photos; the app groups faces that look alike, and you add names afterward.
5.  **Rule.** A timer. (Some cities use sensors and smarter timing, but a fixed 45 seconds is a rule.)
6.  **Learned**, usually with some rules added. Spam filters learn from millions of messages people mark as spam, and many also have hand-written rules, like blocking known scam senders. Real systems often mix both.
:::

### Exercise 2: Name the task family

Name the task family for each product.

1.  A weather app shows tomorrow's high: 18°C.
2.  A plant-identifying app says "Monstera deliciosa".
3.  A streaming service's "Because you watched…" row.
4.  An app that turns your sketch into a finished-looking painting.
5.  A bank flags a card payment as "possible fraud".
6.  A school groups 400 students' survey answers into "types" of study habits, without deciding the types in advance.
7.  A map app estimates you'll arrive at 4:52 p.m.
8.  A keyboard suggests the next word as you type.

:::hint
Look only at the output. Is it one label from a list, a number on a scale, a ranked list for you, groups nobody named, or something that didn't exist before?
:::

:::solution
1.  **Regression**: a temperature is a number, and 18.5°C makes sense.
2.  **Classification**: one species from a fixed list.
3.  **Recommendation**: a ranked list chosen for you.
4.  **Generation**: a new image.
5.  **Classification**: fraud or not fraud.
6.  **Clustering**: the groups are found, not given.
7.  **Regression**: an arrival time is a number on a scale (minutes from now).
8.  **Generation**, and also a little like classification: it picks likely next words from a vocabulary. This is the same next-word prediction that sits inside chatbots, just much smaller.
:::

### Exercise 3: One goal, four framings

A school cafeteria throws away too much food. Write one machine learning task for each family that could help: classification, regression, clustering, and recommendation. For each, say what the model's answer would be.

:::hint
Start from the answers the cafeteria staff would find useful. "How much?" is a number. "Which kind of day is this?" is a label. "Which students eat alike?" is groups.
:::

:::solution
One good set (yours can differ):

-   **Regression**: predict how many servings of each dish will be eaten tomorrow. Answer: a number, like 212 servings of pasta.
-   **Classification**: predict whether tomorrow will be a "low", "normal", or "high" attendance day. Answer: one of three labels.
-   **Clustering**: group the weekly menus by how much food was wasted, to discover which kinds of menus waste the most. Answer: groups nobody named in advance.
-   **Recommendation**: suggest to each student the dishes they're most likely to finish. Answer: a ranked list per student.

Notice that the regression version is probably the most useful, and also that a simple rule ("cook what was eaten last Tuesday") might get you most of the way. That's Exercise 6.
:::

### Exercise 4: Number or category?

For each prediction, decide whether it's better treated as classification or regression, and use the halfway test to justify it.

1.  A student's final letter grade (A to F).
2.  The number of minutes a student spends on homework.
3.  A shoe size.
4.  Someone's age, guessed from a photo.
5.  The zip code a package is going to.

:::hint
Halfway between two answers: does it mean something? If yes, it's a scale. If not, the "numbers" are names.
:::

:::solution
1.  **Classification**, though an ordered one. There's no grade halfway between B and C in most schools. (If the school used a 0 to 100 percentage instead, that would be regression.)
2.  **Regression.** Halfway between 30 and 40 minutes is 35 minutes, which is meaningful.
3.  **Either, and it's a real choice.** Shoe sizes come in fixed steps (8, 8.5, 9), which looks like classification, but halfway between 8 and 9 is 8.5, a real size. Treating it as regression and rounding works well.
4.  **Regression.** Halfway between 30 and 40 is 35, a real age.
5.  **Classification.** Zip 94110 and 94112 aren't "two apart" in any useful way. They're names that happen to be written with digits.
:::

### Exercise 5: Judge the chatbot claims

A friend makes each of these claims. Say whether it's right, wrong, or "it depends", and explain using how chatbots work.

1.  "It told me the library closes at 6, so it does."
2.  "It gave me a citation with an author and a page number, so the article exists."
3.  "It can't possibly know about anything that happened this week."
4.  "It's basically searching a giant database of answers."
5.  "It wrote a great first draft of my speech, so I'll edit that."

:::hint
A language model produces text that's likely to come next. Some chatbot apps also give it tools, like web search. Which claims depend on whether a tool was used?
:::

:::solution
1.  **Wrong as reasoning.** It may be right, but a likely-sounding answer isn't a checked one. Opening hours change, and the model may be repeating old text or inventing a plausible time. Check the library's own website.
2.  **Wrong.** Invented citations, with real-looking authors and page numbers, are a well-known failure. A citation exists only once you've found it.
3.  **It depends.** The model's built-in knowledge stops at its training data. If the app searched the web, it can mention this week's news, though it can still misread what it found.
4.  **Wrong.** There's no lookup table of answers. It generates new text each time, which is why the same question can get different answers.
5.  **Right.** Drafting is a strength: the task is "produce plausible, well-organized text", which is exactly what the model is trained to do. Your editing supplies the facts and the voice.
:::

### Exercise 6: Should this use machine learning at all?

For each problem, recommend a rule or machine learning, and give the reason.

1.  Calculating a student's final grade from homework (30%), tests (50%), and a project (20%).
2.  Sorting thousands of photos from a school trip into "has the class banner in it" and "doesn't".
3.  Deciding whether a student can borrow another library book (limit: 5 at a time).
4.  Spotting which of 2,000 written survey answers mention bullying, in any wording.

:::hint
When the rule is known exactly and must be applied the same way every time, a person can write it, and learning adds only risk.
:::

:::solution
1.  **Rule.** The formula is known and must be exactly the same for everyone. A learned model could only add mistakes, and nobody could appeal its decision.
2.  **Machine learning.** "Contains a banner" is easy to recognize and nearly impossible to describe as rules about pixels. A Teachable Machine image model is a reasonable start.
3.  **Rule.** "Borrowed fewer than 5" is one line.
4.  **Machine learning, with people checking.** People describe bullying in endless ways, so word lists miss a lot. A model can flag likely answers, but because the stakes are high, a person should read every flagged answer, and some unflagged ones too.

A pattern to keep: machine learning earns its place when the rule is hard to write down. When the rule is easy and fairness depends on it being exact, use the rule.
:::

### Challenge: Design an AI feature for the school library

Propose one feature for a school library that uses machine learning. Write a half-page covering:

-   The task family, and exactly what the model's answer looks like.
-   The examples it would learn from, and where the known answers (labels) come from.
-   The rule-based version someone could build instead, and why yours is better (or isn't).
-   How you'd check it works on books or students it never saw.
-   One way it could go wrong for a particular student.

:::hint
Recommendation ("books for you") is the obvious choice. A more surprising one: classification of returned books as "needs repair" from a photo of the spine.
:::

:::solution
An example answer, for a "needs repair" classifier:

-   **Task:** classification. Answer: "Needs repair" or "OK" for each returned book, from a photo of its spine and cover.
-   **Examples:** photos of returned books over a term, labeled by the librarian who already decides which go to the repair box. The labels come from that existing human decision.
-   **Rule-based alternative:** "check books older than 10 years". It's easy but wrong in both directions: old books in good shape get pulled, and a new book with a torn spine is missed. The photo model looks at the actual damage.
-   **Checking it:** hold back a set of labeled photos from a different month, never used in training, and compare the model's answers with the librarian's. (Module 4 is about doing this properly.)
-   **Could go wrong:** if almost every training photo was taken under the desk lamp, books photographed in daylight might be misjudged. And if the model is wrong, a student could be blamed for damage they didn't cause, so the librarian should make the final call.
:::
