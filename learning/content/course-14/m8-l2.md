:::objectives
- Write each section of a recommendation memo from a starter template.
- Turn prototype test results into a confusion matrix and accuracy-per-class chart.
- Rank risks by likelihood and harm, and attach a safeguard to each.
- Compare AI against non-AI alternatives on cost, privacy, and errors.
- Write a verdict with conditions, an honest uncertainty statement, and what would change your mind.
:::

Here's the skeleton your memo will grow from. Copy it into a document now. Each milestone below fills in one or two sections, using the lost-and-found example from the last lesson to show what a finished section looks like.

```text
RECOMMENDATION: [Should we use AI to ...?]
Prepared by: [your name]          Date: [date]

0. SUMMARY            (write last: verdict + 3 reasons, under 100 words)
1. THE PROBLEM        Who has it, how it's handled now, what goes wrong
2. THE PROPOSAL       What the AI would do, task type, what decision it feeds
3. DATA AND PRIVACY   Data needed, where it comes from, consent, keeping and deleting
4. EVIDENCE           Prototype test, verified claims, interviews: what you found
5. RISKS              Errors, bias, privacy, misinformation: likelihood, harm, safeguard
6. ALTERNATIVES       Non-AI and partly-AI options compared
7. RECOMMENDATION     Verdict, conditions, what would change my mind, what I don't know
SOURCES               Every source you used, with links
```

Work through the milestones in order. Each ends with a check: if you can't answer it, the section isn't done.

## Milestone 1: The problem and the people

Describe the problem *without mentioning AI*. If you can't, you may be starting from a solution looking for a problem.

```text
1. THE PROBLEM
About 40 items are handed in to the front office each week and go into a bin by
the door. Students rarely check it: in interviews, 3 of 3 students didn't know
where the bin was. Unclaimed items are donated at the end of each term. The office
manager estimates more than half of handed-in items are never claimed.
```

Notice the evidence already in there: a number from the office, and what interviews found. That beats "lots of things get lost."

**Check:** Could someone who has never seen your school understand the problem from this paragraph alone?

## Milestone 2: The proposal, the data, and privacy

Say exactly what the AI would do and what happens with its output. Then summarize your data plan from Module 6 in a few lines.

```text
2. THE PROPOSAL
Office staff photograph each item. An image classifier sorts it into Jacket,
Bottle, Bag, Electronics, or Other. The photo is posted on the matching section of
a "Lost and found" page. Task type: classification.

3. DATA AND PRIVACY
Training data: ~80 photos per class of items from the office bin, taken by staff.
Risk: items carry names, school IDs, and phone lock screens. Photos must be checked
and cropped before posting. Students are 11-14, so no faces or names are posted.
Photos are removed when an item is claimed or donated.
```

**Check:** Does your data section say where data comes from, who agreed, and when it's deleted?

## Milestone 3: Gather the evidence

For the example, a student built a five-class Teachable Machine prototype, then tested it on 60 photos of items it had never seen: 12 per class, half on a plain desk and half on a cluttered one. They tallied each result into a grid. If you're using Colab, paste the cell below and replace the grid with your own counts. If not, your hand tally *is* the evidence; the chart just makes it easier to read.

```python run alt="Left: a 5 by 5 confusion matrix with most counts on the diagonal; the Other row is spread across Jacket, Bottle, Bag, and Electronics. Right: accuracy per class, Bottle 100%, Jacket 92%, Electronics 83%, Bag 75%, Other 50%." caption="The prototype's results on 60 new photos"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

classes = ["Jacket", "Bottle", "Bag", "Electronics", "Other"]
counts = np.array([          # rows: what the item was; columns: what the model said
    [11, 0, 1, 0, 0],
    [0, 12, 0, 0, 0],
    [2, 0, 9, 0, 1],
    [0, 1, 0, 10, 1],
    [1, 2, 2, 1, 6],
])
print(f"{counts.sum()} tests, {np.trace(counts)} right, accuracy {np.trace(counts) / counts.sum():.0%}")

fig, (left, right) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.1, 1]})
left.imshow(counts, cmap="Blues", vmin=0, vmax=12)
for r in range(5):
    for c in range(5):
        left.text(c, r, counts[r, c], ha="center", va="center",
                  color="white" if counts[r, c] > 6 else "#222")
left.set_xticks(range(5), classes, rotation=30)
left.set_yticks(range(5), classes)
left.set_xlabel("Prediction")
left.set_ylabel("Class")
left.set_title("Confusion matrix")

per_class = 100 * np.diag(counts) / counts.sum(axis=1)
bars = right.barh(classes[::-1], per_class[::-1], color="#2a78d6")
right.bar_label(bars, fmt="%.0f%%", padding=3)
right.set_xlim(0, 115)
right.set_xlabel("Accuracy (%)")
right.set_title("Accuracy per class")
plt.tight_layout()
```

Read it the way you learned in Module 5. Bottles and jackets are easy: they look alike within their class. **Other** is a coin flip at 50%, and its errors are scattered across every column. That makes sense: "Other" holds calculators, hats, lunchboxes, and glasses, which share nothing visual. And it's the category whose items are already hardest to find.

The student also split results by condition: 27 of 30 right on a plain desk (90%), 21 of 30 on a cluttered one (70%). Here's how that becomes memo text:

```text
4. EVIDENCE
A 5-class prototype scored 80% on 60 new photos (90% on a plain desk, 70% on a
cluttered one). It was reliable for bottles (12/12) and jackets (11/12), and
unreliable for "Other" items (6/12). A prototype is not the real product, but it
shows where this kind of model struggles: mixed categories and messy backgrounds.
Interviews: the office manager said sorting takes "a few seconds per item"; all 3
students said they'd check a web page, but only if it had photos.
```

That last interview line turns out to matter more than the chart.

**Check:** Does your evidence section include numbers, say what they do and don't prove, and mention at least one surprise?

## Milestone 4: Rank the risks

List every risk you can think of across the course's themes (errors, bias, privacy, misinformation), then score each for **likelihood** and **harm** from 1 to 3:

| # | Risk | Likely | Harm | Safeguard |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A name or face visible on a posted item | 3 | 2 | Staff check and crop every photo before posting |
| 2 | A phone posted with notifications on its lock screen | 2 | 3 | Never photograph the screen side of a device |
| 3 | "Other" items filed in the wrong section | 3 | 1 | Post "Other" items in every section, or skip sorting them |
| 4 | Someone claims an item that isn't theirs | 2 | 2 | Owner must describe a detail not shown in the photo |
| 5 | Electronics posted as "Other" and missed | 1 | 2 | Staff review all electronics by hand |

```python run alt="Risk map with five numbered points on a 3 by 3 grid of likelihood against harm. Risks 1 (likely, moderate) and 2 (possible, serious) are highlighted as act-first; risks 3, 4, and 5 are marked as needing a safeguard." caption="Multiply likelihood by harm; anything scoring 6 or more gets dealt with first"
risks = [(1, 3, 2), (2, 2, 3), (3, 3, 1), (4, 2, 2), (5, 1, 2)]   # (number, likely, harm)

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 4.2))
for number, likely, harm in risks:
    urgent = likely * harm >= 6
    plt.scatter(likely, harm, s=420, color="#eb6834" if urgent else "#2a78d6", zorder=3)
    plt.text(likely, harm, str(number), ha="center", va="center", color="white", fontsize=11, zorder=4)
plt.scatter([], [], s=120, color="#eb6834", label="Act on these first (score 6+)")
plt.scatter([], [], s=120, color="#2a78d6", label="Plan a safeguard")
plt.xticks([1, 2, 3], ["Unlikely", "Possible", "Likely"])
plt.yticks([1, 2, 3], ["Minor", "Moderate", "Serious"])
plt.xlim(0.5, 3.5)
plt.ylim(0.5, 3.5)
plt.xlabel("How likely")
plt.ylabel("How much harm")
plt.title("Risk map for the lost-and-found sorter")
plt.legend(frameon=False, loc="upper center", bbox_to_anchor=(0.5, -0.18), ncol=2)
plt.grid(alpha=0.25)
plt.tight_layout()
```

Look at which risks landed in orange. Both are about **what's in the photos**, and neither is caused by the AI. They'd exist with or without a model. The AI's own errors (risks 3 and 5) are real but low-harm. That's a finding worth stating plainly in your memo.

**Check:** Does every risk have a safeguard, and does every safeguard name who does it?

## Milestone 5: Compare the alternatives

Always compare at least three options, including one with no AI:

| | A. Staff sort by hand | B. AI sorts, staff review | C. AI sorts and posts automatically |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Staff time per week | About 15 minutes | About 10 minutes | About 5 minutes |
| Privacy check before posting | Yes | Yes | No |
| Sorting errors | Very few | Few (staff catch them) | 1 in 5 items, more for "Other" |
| Setup | None | Train and maintain a model | Train and maintain a model |
| Solves the real problem? | Yes: photos go online | Yes | Yes, with privacy risks |

The table makes the uncomfortable point. The real problem was that students never saw the items; **posting photos online** fixes that. Sorting into categories is the small part, and people do it in seconds. Option B saves five minutes a week at the cost of building and maintaining a model. Option C saves more time but skips the privacy check that the risk map says matters most.

**Check:** Is there an option with no AI at all, and did you judge it fairly?

## Milestone 6: Write the verdict

Your verdict section has four parts: the verdict, its conditions, what would change your mind, and what you don't know.

```text
7. RECOMMENDATION
Verdict: DON'T use AI for sorting. DO start posting photos online now.

Post a photo of each handed-in item on a "Lost and found" page, sorted by hand
(Option A). This solves the actual problem, takes about 15 minutes a week, and
keeps a person checking every photo for names, faces, and screens.

Conditions for the page: crop out names and faces; never show device screens;
owners describe a hidden detail to claim; remove photos when items are claimed
or donated.

What would change my mind: if hand-in volume grew past about 150 items a week,
Option B (AI sorts, staff review) could save real time, and the prototype
suggests it would work well for everything except "Other".

What I don't know: my prototype was trained and tested by one student on items
from one school. A real system would need testing on a full term's items.
```

The example's first guess was **Go with conditions**. The evidence moved it to **Don't**, not because the model was bad (80%, and 100% on bottles), but because the interviews and the alternatives table showed the AI wasn't solving the expensive part of the problem. That's what good evidence is supposed to do.

## Assemble the memo

Put the sections together, add a **Sources** list with links (the Teachable Machine FAQ, any articles or studies you used, and "interviews with three students and the office manager, October 2026"), then write the **Summary** last:

```text
0. SUMMARY
Verdict: Don't use AI to sort lost property; start posting photos online by hand.
- The real problem is visibility: students never see handed-in items.
- A prototype sorted 80% of items correctly, but sorting by hand takes only
  about 15 minutes a week, so AI saves little.
- The biggest risks (names, faces, phone screens in photos) need a person
  checking every photo whichever option is chosen.
```

A reader who stops after the summary should still know your verdict and why.

:::takeaways
- Describe the problem without mentioning AI. Then describe exactly what the AI would do and what its output decides.
- Evidence means numbers with limits: say what your prototype shows and what it can't prove.
- Score risks by likelihood times harm, and give each one a safeguard and an owner.
- Always compare against a no-AI option. Sometimes it wins.
- A verdict includes conditions, what would change your mind, and what you don't know.
:::
