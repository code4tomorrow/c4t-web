Hold a banana up to a model trained only on Thumbs up, Thumbs down, and Nothing. There's a fair chance the Preview panel shows something like **Thumbs up 96%**. The model has never seen a banana. It isn't 96% sure the banana is a thumb in any sense a person would mean. So what is that number?

This lesson answers that question, then follows it to a harder one. When a model is wrong, who is it wrong about? In 2018, two researchers showed that face-analysis products from three large companies were almost perfect for one group of people and wrong about a third of the time for another. Each product's headline accuracy looked fine.

## A confidence score is a split, not a promise

The bars under **Output** in Teachable Machine always add up to 100%. That's by design: the last step of the model takes its raw scores and squeezes them into shares of 100, one share per class. The model *must* hand out all 100 points, even when the picture matches none of its classes well.

So **Thumbs up 96%** means: "of the answers I'm allowed to give, this one fits best, by a lot." It does not mean "there's a 96% chance this is a thumbs-up." Those two statements sound alike and are very different.

Here's the precise version. A model is **well calibrated** if, out of all the times it says 80%, it's right about 80% of the time. Many models aren't. They tend to be **overconfident**, especially on inputs unlike their training examples: a banana, a new background, a face from a group that was rare in the training data. Confidence is most trustworthy on inputs that look like what the model trained on, which is exactly when you need it least.

## Thresholds turn a score into a decision

Most real systems don't show you bars. They make a decision. A spam filter gives every email a **spam score** from 0 to 1, and a rule decides what happens: *if the score is at least 0.5, send it to the spam folder.* That cutoff is the **threshold**.

The chart below shows the scores a filter gave 1,000 real emails and 1,000 spam emails (made-up data, shaped like a real filter's).

```python run alt="Two overlapping histograms of spam scores. Real emails cluster near 0.2, spam near 0.8, and they overlap between about 0.35 and 0.65. A dashed line marks a threshold at 0.5." caption="Spam scores for 1,000 real emails and 1,000 spam emails"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
real_mail = rng.beta(2, 6, 1000)   # scores the filter gave real emails
spam = rng.beta(6, 2, 1000)        # scores it gave spam

bins = np.linspace(0, 1, 26)
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
plt.hist(real_mail, bins=bins, color="#2a78d6", alpha=0.7, label="Real email")
plt.hist(spam, bins=bins, color="#eb6834", alpha=0.7, label="Spam")
plt.axvline(0.5, color="#333", linestyle="--", linewidth=1.5)
plt.text(0.52, 100, "threshold 0.5:\nat or above goes\nto the spam folder", color="#333")
plt.xlabel("Spam score the filter gave the email")
plt.ylabel("Number of emails")
plt.title("The two piles overlap, so no threshold is perfect")
plt.legend(frameon=False, loc="upper left")
plt.tight_layout()
```

Look at the middle, where blue and orange overlap. Some real emails (a newsletter, a message with lots of links) score as high as some spam. Wherever you draw the line, a few emails land on the wrong side. The only question is *which* wrong side you'd rather have.

Now slide the threshold from 0 to 1 and count the mistakes at every position:

```python run alt="Line chart. As the threshold rises from 0 to 1, real email wrongly sent to spam falls from 1,000 to 0, while spam left in the inbox rises from 0 to 1,000. The lines cross near 0.5 at about 57 mistakes each." caption="Every threshold is a trade between the two kinds of mistake"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
real_mail = rng.beta(2, 6, 1000)
spam = rng.beta(6, 2, 1000)

thresholds = np.linspace(0, 1, 101)
false_pos = [(real_mail >= t).sum() for t in thresholds]  # real mail sent to spam
false_neg = [(spam < t).sum() for t in thresholds]        # spam left in the inbox

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
plt.plot(thresholds, false_pos, color="#2a78d6", linewidth=2, label="Real email sent to spam (false positive)")
plt.plot(thresholds, false_neg, color="#eb6834", linewidth=2, label="Spam left in inbox (false negative)")
plt.axvline(0.5, color="#888", linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
plt.xlabel("Threshold: send to spam if the score is at least this")
plt.ylabel("Mistakes (out of 1,000 of each)")
plt.title("Raising the threshold trades one mistake for the other")
plt.legend(frameon=False, loc="upper center")
plt.grid(alpha=0.25)
plt.tight_layout()

for t in [0.3, 0.5, 0.7]:
    print(f"threshold {t}: {(real_mail >= t).sum()} real emails lost, {(spam < t).sum()} spam let through")
```

The blue line falls as the threshold rises: a strict filter rarely buries a real email. The orange line climbs: the same strict filter lets more spam through. At 0.3 you lose about 300 real emails to stop nearly all the spam. At 0.7 you lose almost none but let about a third of the spam in. The lines can't both reach zero, because the piles overlap. **Changing the threshold never removes mistakes. It chooses which ones you get.**

## Four outcomes, and why the names matter

Any yes/no decision has four possible results. Call the thing you're looking for "positive" (spam, disease, a match):

| | Model says **yes** | Model says **no** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Really yes** | True positive: caught it | False negative: missed it |
| **Really no** | False positive: false alarm | True negative: correctly left alone |

This grid is called a **confusion matrix**. Teachable Machine can draw one for you under **Advanced › Under the hood**, where it's labelled with the true **Class** on one axis and the **Prediction** on the other. Next lesson you'll build one from your own tests.

Which mistake is worse depends entirely on the setting:

-   **Spam filter.** A false positive hides a real email, maybe a job offer. A false negative means you delete one more junk message. Most people prefer a high threshold that lets some spam through.
-   **Medical screening.** A false negative sends a sick person home thinking they're fine. A false positive means a worrying phone call and a second test. Screening tests use a low threshold on purpose, and they produce many false alarms as a result: in US studies, roughly half to 60% of women who get a mammogram every year for ten years get at least one false-positive callback. Doctors accept that cost because a follow-up test sorts out most false alarms, while a missed cancer can't be undone.
-   **A smoke alarm** is the everyday version. It shrieks at burnt toast because a missed fire is far worse than an annoying beep.

## The number that hid the problem: Gender Shades

In 2018, Joy Buolamwini (MIT Media Lab) and Timnit Gebru (Microsoft Research) tested three commercial products that guess a person's gender from a photo, made by Microsoft, IBM, and Face++. Popular face datasets at the time were 79.6% and 86.2% lighter-skinned, so they built a balanced test set: 1,270 official photos of members of parliament from Rwanda, Senegal, South Africa, Iceland, Finland, and Sweden. A dermatologist labelled each person's skin type.

Overall, the products were right on 87.9% to 93.7% of faces. Then the researchers split the results into four groups:

```python run alt="Grouped bar chart of error rates. For darker-skinned women, error rates are 20.8% (Microsoft), 34.5% (Face++), and 34.7% (IBM). For lighter-skinned men they are 0.0%, 0.8%, and 0.3%. Darker men and lighter women fall in between." caption="Error rates from Table 4 of the Gender Shades paper (Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018)"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

groups = ["Darker\nwomen", "Darker\nmen", "Lighter\nwomen", "Lighter\nmen"]
error_rates = {   # % of faces classified wrongly
    "Microsoft": [20.8, 6.0, 1.7, 0.0],
    "Face++":    [34.5, 0.7, 9.8, 0.8],
    "IBM":       [34.7, 12.0, 7.1, 0.3],
}
colors = ["#2a78d6", "#eb6834", "#1baf7a"]

x = np.arange(len(groups))
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
for i, (company, rates) in enumerate(error_rates.items()):
    bars = plt.bar(x + (i - 1) * 0.26, rates, 0.24, color=colors[i], label=company)
    plt.bar_label(bars, fmt="%.1f", fontsize=8, padding=2)
plt.xticks(x, groups)
plt.ylim(0, 40)
plt.ylabel("Error rate (%)")
plt.title("Who the face classifiers got wrong")
plt.legend(frameon=False)
plt.grid(axis="y", alpha=0.25)
plt.tight_layout()
```

Read the chart left to right. Every product did worst on darker-skinned women: one in five wrong for Microsoft, about one in three for Face++ and IBM. Every product did best, or nearly best, on lighter-skinned men, where IBM's error rate was 0.3% and Microsoft's was zero. An average of 90% hid a group getting a third of its answers wrong.

The study made news, and it had an effect: a follow-up audit by Deborah Raji and Buolamwini found the companies had narrowed the gaps in later versions. The lesson for you is the method. **Never accept a single accuracy number. Ask "accurate for whom?" and split the results by the groups who will use the system.**

:::warning
**Three mistakes people make with scores**

1.  **Reading confidence as probability of being right.** "It said 98%, so it's almost certainly correct." Only if the model is well calibrated *and* the input looks like its training data. Test it; don't assume.
2.  **Reporting one accuracy number.** 94% accuracy can mean 99% for most users and 60% for a group nobody checked. Always split by group.
3.  **Deleting a sensitive column and calling the data fair.** Removing "race" or "gender" doesn't remove the pattern if other columns (a ZIP code, a first name, a school) carry the same information. Check the results by group, not the inputs.
:::

:::quiz
Q: A model trained on Rock, Paper, and Scissors shows **Paper 97%** when you hold up a sheet of homework. What's the best explanation?
- [ ] The homework really is 97% likely to be a hand making "paper"
  The model can't consider "homework" at all. 97% is its share of the vote, not a probability of being right.
- [x] The bars must add up to 100% across the classes it knows, so a flat white shape gets nearly all of it
  With no class for "something else", every input is forced into one of the three, often with high confidence.
- [ ] The model is broken and needs retraining from scratch
  It's working as designed. A "Nothing" or "Other" class, plus testing on unfamiliar objects, is the fix.

Q: A clinic uses a screening test for a serious disease that is easy to treat if caught early. A follow-up test is cheap and accurate. Where should the screening threshold go?
- [x] Low, accepting more false positives so that very few sick people are missed
  A false alarm costs a follow-up test. A miss costs a person their early treatment, so misses are the worse mistake here.
- [ ] High, so that almost nobody gets a false alarm
  That would send more sick people home. In screening, false negatives are usually the costlier error.
- [ ] Exactly 0.5, because that's the fair middle
  0.5 has no special meaning. The right threshold depends on the cost of each kind of mistake.

Q: On the spam chart, you move the threshold from 0.5 to 0.7. What happens?
- [ ] Both kinds of mistake go down
  The lines move in opposite directions. Lowering one raises the other.
- [x] Fewer real emails go to spam, but more spam reaches the inbox
  A stricter cutoff flags fewer emails of every kind, so fewer false positives and more false negatives.
- [ ] More real emails go to spam
  That happens when you lower the threshold, not raise it.

Q: A company says its face-unlock feature is "95% accurate." What's the most useful question to ask next?
- [ ] Was it trained for more epochs?
  Training length doesn't tell you who the 5% of errors fall on.
- [ ] Is 95% higher than last year?
  An improving average can still hide a group that fails badly.
- [x] What is the accuracy for each group of users, such as skin tone, age, and gender?
  Gender Shades showed that a strong average can hide error rates over 30% for one group.
:::

:::takeaways
- Confidence bars add up to 100% across the classes a model knows, so an unfamiliar input still gets a confident-looking answer.
- A threshold turns a score into a decision. Moving it trades false positives for false negatives; it never removes both.
- Pick the threshold by asking which mistake is worse and for whom: a hidden email, a missed illness, a false alarm.
- A single accuracy number can hide large gaps. Split results by group before trusting a system.
- Gender Shades (2018) found error rates up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women and under 1% for lighter-skinned men in the same products.
:::
