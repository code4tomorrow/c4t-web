- Choose a problem that is real, specific, and small enough to research well.
- Map the people a system would affect, including people who are never asked.
- Plan the evidence you'll gather: a prototype test, checked sources, or interviews.
- Fill in a one-page plan for your recommendation.
:::

Since 2012, a computer system in Wisconsin has scored every sixth through ninth grader in the state on how likely they are to graduate from high school on time. In 2023, the news site The Markup reported that when the system flagged a student as high risk, it was wrong nearly three-quarters of the time, and wrong more often for Black and Hispanic students than for white students. Somewhere, early on, someone wrote a recommendation saying this was a good idea.

Your capstone is to write that kind of recommendation, but well. You'll take a real proposal to use AI, investigate it with everything from this course (confidence, errors, bias, privacy, consent, misinformation, alternatives), and recommend what should happen. "Don't use AI here" is an allowed answer. So is "yes", if your evidence supports it.

## What you'll produce

| Part | What it is | Built in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recommendation memo | 800 to 1,500 words, using the template in the next lesson | Lesson 2 |
| Evidence | At least one of: a Teachable Machine prototype with a stress test, a verification log of claims about the system, or interviews with people affected | Lesson 2 |
| Presentation | 3 minutes, with up to 5 slides or a one-page handout | Lesson 3 |

Every memo ends with one of four verdicts:

-   **Go**: use AI as proposed.
-   **Go with conditions**: use it only if specific safeguards are in place, and name them.
-   **Not yet**: it might work, but something must be tested or fixed first.
-   **Don't**: AI is the wrong tool, and here's what to do instead.

## Five proposals to choose from

Each is a real kind of system that schools and organizations are offered or already use. They're listed from most straightforward to hardest to judge.

| Level | Proposal | What makes it interesting | Evidence you could gather |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starter | **Lost-and-found sorter.** Photograph items handed in, sort them into categories, and post them online. | Low stakes, but items carry names, ID cards, and phone screens. | Teachable Machine image model of 3 or 4 item types, stress-tested on new items |
| Starter | **Cafeteria waste camera.** Photograph trays at the return window to estimate how much food is thrown away. | Useful data, but who appears in the photos? | Image model with "mostly eaten / half / mostly left" classes |
| Intermediate | **Classroom noise monitor.** An always-on microphone warns when the room is too loud. | Privacy of constant listening, and a sound model that has to tell "talking" from "chairs scraping". | Teachable Machine Audio Project (it needs at least 20 seconds of Background Noise) |
| Intermediate | **Handbook chatbot.** Students ask questions about school rules and get instant answers. | Hallucinated rules, as in the Air Canada case; students under 13 using it. | A verification log of a chatbot's answers to 15 real handbook questions (if you're old enough to use one), or a review of how a similar bot behaved |
| Advanced | **Face recognition at the entrance**, or **dropout prediction** to assign counselors. | High stakes, real documented harms, consent problems, uneven errors. | Published research (Gender Shades, The Markup's investigation), interviews, and a careful alternatives analysis. No prototype on real faces. |

**Or bring your own.** A problem from your school, team, club, family business, library, or town is often the best choice, because you can talk to the people it affects. A good own-problem passes three tests: someone has actually proposed (or could reasonably propose) AI for it; you can find or create evidence about it; and it's narrow enough to cover in 1,500 words. "AI in healthcare" fails the third test. "An app that reads my grandmother's pill labels aloud" passes all three.

:::tip
Pick the problem you'd most like to argue about at dinner. You'll spend a few weeks with it, and caring about the answer shows in the writing.
:::

## Map everyone it affects

Most bad AI decisions forget somebody. List three groups:

1.  **Users**: people who operate the system (the front office, the teacher, the student asking the chatbot).
2.  **Subjects**: people the system makes decisions *about* (the student flagged as high risk, the face at the door).
3.  **Bystanders**: people affected without being asked (visitors walking past a camera, a classmate whose voice the noise monitor records).

Subjects and bystanders are the ones whose voices go missing. In the Wisconsin case, the subjects were sixth to ninth graders, and students The Markup interviewed were surprised to learn the system existed at all.

## Talk to two or three of them

Nothing beats asking. A ten-minute conversation with someone affected often reveals the thing no article mentions. Useful questions:

-   How is this handled now, and what's frustrating about it?
-   If a system got it wrong about you, what would happen next? Who would you tell?
-   What would you want to know before agreeing to this?
-   Would you rather have this, or [the simple alternative]?

Ask permission before taking notes, and don't record names in your memo unless people agree. Your own data plan from Module 6 applies to your research too.

## Plan your evidence

Your memo needs at least one kind of evidence beyond your opinion:

-   **A prototype test.** Build a small Teachable Machine version and stress-test it like the rock-paper-scissors model in Module 5: conditions, groups, a log, a confusion matrix, and accuracy by condition. A prototype can't prove a real product works, but it can reveal *how* this kind of system fails.
-   **A verification log.** List the claims made for the system (a vendor's "99% accurate", a news story's numbers) and check each against original sources, as in Module 7.
-   **Interviews** with the people you mapped above, summarized without names.

Strong memos usually combine two of these.

## Fill in your plan

Copy this into a document and fill it in. It should fit on one page.

```text
CAPSTONE PLAN

Project:          ______________________________________________
The problem:      Who has it? How do they handle it today? What goes wrong?
The AI proposal:  What would it do? What task type (classify, predict a number,
                  group, generate)? What decision does its output feed into?
People affected:  Users:       ___________
                  Subjects:    ___________
                  Bystanders:  ___________
Evidence:         [ ] Prototype test   [ ] Verification log   [ ] Interviews
                  Details: ___________________________________________
The five questions I must answer:
                  Task / Data / Stakes / Errors / Alternatives
Risks I already suspect:
                  ______________________________________________
My first guess at the verdict (allowed to change!):
                  Go / Go with conditions / Not yet / Don't
Timeline:         Plan by ____  Evidence by ____  Draft by ____  Present on ____
```

Writing down your first guess matters. At the end, compare it with your final verdict. If the evidence never moved you at all, ask yourself whether you really looked.

## A filled-in example

The next lesson follows this example through every milestone:

```text
CAPSTONE PLAN

Project:          Lost-and-found sorter for Lincoln Middle School
The problem:      About 40 items are handed in each week and piled in a bin. Students
                  rarely check it. At the end of each term, unclaimed items are donated.
The AI proposal:  Office staff photograph each item; a model sorts it (jacket, bottle,
                  bag, electronics, other) and posts it to a "Lost and found" web page.
                  Task type: classification. Output feeds into: which page it's posted on.
People affected:  Users:       2 front-office staff
                  Subjects:    students who lost things (about 500 students, ages 11-14)
                  Bystanders:  anyone whose name, face, or photos appear on an item
Evidence:         [x] Prototype test   [ ] Verification log   [x] Interviews
                  Details: TM image model, 5 classes, 40 test items in 3 lighting
                  conditions; interview the office manager and 3 students
The five questions: to answer in the memo
Risks I already suspect:
                  Names on water bottles posted publicly. Phones posted showing lock
                  screens. "Other" items never found.
My first guess:   Go with conditions
Timeline:         Plan by Oct 3  Evidence by Oct 17  Draft by Oct 24  Present Oct 31
```

The plan already does something good: it spotted that the riskiest part isn't the AI at all. It's what's *in* the photos.

:::takeaways
- The capstone is a recommendation, not a sales pitch. "Don't" and "Not yet" are real answers.
- Choose a problem that is real, specific, and small enough to research properly.
- Map users, subjects, and bystanders; subjects and bystanders are the ones most often forgotten.
- Plan at least one kind of evidence beyond opinion: a prototype test, a verification log, or interviews.
- Write down your first-guess verdict so you can see whether the evidence changed your mind.
:::
