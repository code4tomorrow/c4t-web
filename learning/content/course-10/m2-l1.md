A team wires a red LED straight from an Uno's 5V pin to GND. It flashes very bright, then fades, and the plastic smells warm. Their code was fine; in fact there was no code at all. The problem is arithmetic, and by the end of these notes you'll be able to do that arithmetic before you plug anything in.

## Voltage pushes, current flows, resistance resists

Here's the intuition. Picture water in pipes.

-   **Voltage** is like the pressure difference between two points in the pipe. It is the *push*. It is measured in **volts (V)**, and it always describes two points: "5 volts" on an Arduino means 5 volts higher than GND.
-   **Current** is how much charge flows past a point each second, like liters of water per second. It is measured in **amperes (A)**. Arduino circuits usually use **milliamps (mA)**: 1 A = 1000 mA.
-   **Resistance** is how hard a part makes it for current to get through, like a narrow section of pipe. It is measured in **ohms (Ω)**. 1 kΩ (kilohm) = 1000 Ω.

Current only flows around a complete loop: out of the supply, through the parts, and back to the supply. Break the loop anywhere (a loose wire, a switch that's open) and current stops everywhere in that loop at once.

The water picture breaks down eventually (electrons don't leak out of an open wire end), but it gets the main relationships right.

## Ohm's law makes it precise

For a resistor, the three quantities are tied together by **Ohm's law**:

**V = I × R**, or rearranged, **I = V / R**

Double the voltage and the current doubles. Double the resistance and the current halves. Let's check a typical Arduino number: 5 volts across a 220 Ω resistor.

```python run title="ohms_law.py"
supply_volts = 5.0
resistor_ohms = 220

current_amps = supply_volts / resistor_ohms
print(f"Current: {current_amps:.4f} A")
print(f"       = {current_amps * 1000:.1f} mA")
```

About 23 mA. Now let's see the pattern for a few resistors at once. Each line is one resistor; the steeper the line, the more current per volt.

```python run alt="Line chart of current against voltage for 220, 470 and 1000 ohm resistors. All three are straight lines from zero; the 220 ohm line is steepest, reaching about 23 mA at 5 V, while 1000 ohms reaches only 5 mA." caption="Ohm's law gives a straight line for every resistor. Its slope is 1/R."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

volts = [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
for ohms, color in [(220, "#2a78d6"), (470, "#eb6834"), (1000, "#1baf7a")]:
    milliamps = [v / ohms * 1000 for v in volts]
    plt.plot(volts, milliamps, color=color, linewidth=2, marker="o", label=f"{ohms} Ω")

plt.xlabel("Voltage across the resistor (V)")
plt.ylabel("Current (mA)")
plt.title("Same voltage, more resistance, less current")
plt.legend()
plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
```

Notice that every line passes through zero: no voltage, no current. And at 5 V, the 1 kΩ resistor allows only 5 mA, less than a quarter of what 220 Ω allows.

## Power: where the heat comes from

Every resistor turns electrical energy into heat. The rate is **power**, measured in **watts (W)**:

**P = V × I**

The small resistors in starter kits are usually rated for **¼ W (0.25 W)**. Push more power than that through one and it gets hot enough to discolor, smell, or burn out. Let's check a few resistors connected straight across 5 V:

```python run title="power_check.py"
RATING_WATTS = 0.25

def power_watts(volts, ohms):
    current = volts / ohms
    return volts * current

for ohms in [1000, 220, 47, 10]:
    watts = power_watts(5.0, ohms)
    verdict = "fine" if watts <= RATING_WATTS else "TOO HOT for a 1/4 W resistor"
    print(f"{ohms:>5} ohms at 5 V: {watts:.3f} W  {verdict}")
```

The pattern is worth remembering: **smaller resistance means more current and more heat**. Halving the resistance at the same voltage doubles the power.

## Short circuits: when resistance goes to almost zero

A **short circuit** is a path from the supply back to itself with almost no resistance, like a wire from 5V straight to GND. Ohm's law says what happens as R shrinks. Step through this and watch the current:

:::trace Watch the current explode as resistance shrinks
```python
supply = 5.0
for ohms in [1000, 100, 10, 1, 0.1]:
    milliamps = round(supply / ohms * 1000, 1)
    print(ohms, "ohms ->", milliamps, "mA")
```
:::

At 0.1 Ω the formula says 50,000 mA, or 50 amps. A real wire and battery can't quite deliver that, because the battery has a little resistance of its own, but they can deliver *several amps*. That heats the wire, the battery, and anything else in the loop. A USB port or board may shut off or be damaged. With a battery there is no computer to cut the power, so the wire just keeps heating up.

:::note
Why can you touch the 5 V pins safely, but not a wall outlet? Dry skin has a high resistance, tens of thousands of ohms or more, so 5 V pushes only a tiny current through you. Mains voltage (120 or 230 V) is 24 to 46 times higher, and a few tens of milliamps through the chest can disrupt the heart. That is why this course never uses mains power.
:::

## Why an LED needs a resistor

An LED is not a resistor, and it doesn't follow Ohm's law. Below a certain voltage, called its **forward voltage** (about 2 V for a red LED, about 3 V for blue or white), almost no current flows. Just above it, the current rises extremely fast. The chart uses an idealized model of a red LED to show the shape:

```python run alt="Curve of LED current against voltage. Current stays near zero until about 1.8 volts, then rises steeply, crossing 20 mA at 2.0 volts and passing 80 mA by about 2.1 volts." caption="An idealized red LED. A tenth of a volt more takes it from a safe 20 mA to over 80 mA."
import math
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def led_milliamps(volts):
    # Idealized diode curve, scaled so 2.0 V gives 20 mA.
    return 20 * math.exp((volts - 2.0) / 0.067)

volts = [v / 1000 for v in range(1500, 2111, 10)]
plt.plot(volts, [led_milliamps(v) for v in volts], color="#e34948", linewidth=2)
plt.axhline(20, color="#52514e", linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
plt.text(1.52, 23, "20 mA: typical safe maximum", color="#52514e")
plt.xlabel("Voltage across the LED (V)")
plt.ylabel("Current (mA)")
plt.title("An LED's current shoots up past its forward voltage")
plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
```

So if you connect an LED straight to 5 V, nothing limits the current except the LED's own tiny internal resistance and whatever the supply can deliver. That's the flash-then-fade in the opening story: far too much current, too much heat, a damaged LED, and a stressed pin.

A **series resistor** fixes it. The LED takes its forward voltage, and the resistor takes whatever is left over. Since the resistor follows Ohm's law, it sets the current:

**I = (V_supply − V_forward) / R**

The resistor also makes the current *stable*. Real LEDs vary a little from one to the next, so look what happens if the forward voltage is a bit off:

```python run title="led_current.py"
supply = 5.0
resistor = 220

for forward in [1.9, 2.0, 2.1]:
    milliamps = (supply - forward) / resistor * 1000
    print(f"Forward voltage {forward} V -> {milliamps:.1f} mA")
```

A 0.2 V spread in the LED changes the current by less than 1 mA. Without the resistor, in the model above, the same 0.2 V spread would change the current by a factor of about 20.

## Batteries: voltage and capacity

A battery has two numbers worth knowing:

-   **Voltage**: an AA cell is about 1.5 V, so four in series give about 6 V. A 9 V battery is 9 V. A lithium cell (LiPo or Li-ion) is about 3.7 V.
-   **Capacity**, in **milliamp-hours (mAh)**: roughly how long it can supply a current. A 2000 mAh battery could supply about 200 mA for 10 hours, or 20 mA for 100 hours. Real batteries deliver less at high currents, so treat it as an estimate.

Lithium cells pack a lot of energy into a small space. Shorted or punctured, they can overheat and catch fire, which is why they're only charged with a proper charger and never used if swollen.

:::warning
**Three classic mistakes.**
Mixing up milliamps and amps: 20 mA is 0.02 A, not 20 A. Put 20 into I = V/R as if it were amps and your resistor comes out 1000 times too small.
Thinking the resistor "uses up" current: in a single loop, the same current flows through every part. The resistor drops *voltage*, and that sets the current for the whole loop.
Worrying about which side the resistor goes on: before or after the LED gives exactly the same current, because both are in the same loop.
:::

:::quiz
Q: A 1 kΩ resistor is connected across a 9 V battery. How much current flows?
- [ ] 9 A
  That forgets the "k": 1 kΩ is 1000 Ω, not 1 Ω.
- [x] 9 mA
  I = V / R = 9 / 1000 = 0.009 A, which is 9 mA.
- [ ] 111 mA
  That's R / V upside down. Current is voltage divided by resistance.

Q: Someone puts a 10 Ω resistor across the Uno's 5 V and GND pins. What happens to the resistor?
- [ ] Nothing; 10 Ω is a normal value
  10 Ω is a real value, but across 5 V it carries 500 mA.
- [x] It gets very hot, because it's asked to handle 2.5 W
  P = V × I = 5 × 0.5 = 2.5 W, ten times the rating of a ¼ W resistor.
- [ ] It limits the current to a safe 20 mA
  Ohm's law gives 5 / 10 = 0.5 A, which is 500 mA.

Q: Why can't you control an LED's current just by choosing the right supply voltage?
- [ ] LEDs only work at exactly 5 V
  LEDs work over a range; the problem is how sensitive their current is to voltage.
- [x] A tiny change in voltage causes a huge change in current
  Past the forward voltage, current climbs so steeply that small differences in the LED or supply swing the current wildly. A resistor smooths that out.
- [ ] LEDs follow Ohm's law, so a resistor isn't needed
  LEDs don't follow Ohm's law, which is exactly why they need one.

Q: An LED circuit is 5V → LED → 220 Ω → GND. Your friend says it's broken because the resistor is "after" the LED. Are they right?
- [ ] Yes, the LED gets the full current before the resistor can limit it
  Current doesn't arrive at the LED first. The same current flows everywhere in the loop at the same moment.
- [x] No, the current is the same in either order
  Both parts are in one series loop, so the resistor limits the current wherever it sits.
:::

:::takeaways
- Voltage is the push between two points (V), current is the flow (A or mA), resistance opposes the flow (Ω).
- Ohm's law: I = V / R. More resistance at the same voltage means less current.
- Power is P = V × I. Kit resistors are usually rated ¼ W; less resistance across the same voltage means more heat.
- A short circuit is a near-zero resistance path, so the current becomes huge and things heat up fast.
- An LED's current shoots up past its forward voltage, so it always needs a series resistor: I = (V_supply − V_forward) / R.
- Never use mains power. Low-voltage USB and small batteries are the only power sources in this course.
:::
