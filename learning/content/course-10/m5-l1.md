An Arduino Uno pin is happy supplying about 20 mA. The datasheet for its chip lists 40 mA as the absolute limit, the point where damage starts. Adafruit measured the small yellow "TT" gear motor that ships in most robot kits: 155 mA spinning freely at 4.5 V, and **1.2 A** when the wheel is held still. A stalled motor wants sixty times what the pin is comfortable giving.

So the pin can't be the power source. It can still be the *boss*, telling a motor driver when to switch a big current from a battery.

## Servo or DC motor: pick the right actuator

Robots mostly use two kinds of motor, and they are controlled in completely different ways.

| | DC gear motor | Hobby servo |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it does | Spins continuously. More voltage, more speed. | Turns to an angle (0 to 180°) and holds it. |
| How you control it | A driver chip: two direction pins plus a PWM speed pin | One signal wire that gets a pulse every 20 ms |
| Typical robot job | Driving the wheels | Pointing a sensor, steering, a gripper |
| In Wokwi | No DC motor part, so LEDs stand in for the driver inputs | Yes, `wokwi-servo` |

Wokwi's parts list has a servo and stepper motors, but no plain DC motor and no L298N driver board. So the build in this module puts LEDs exactly where the driver's input wires would go. The code is the code a real robot runs; only the last few centimeters of wire change.

## Why a pin can't run a motor

Current is the first problem, and you've seen the numbers. There are two more.

**Motors kick back.** A motor is a coil, and a coil resists sudden changes in current. Switch it off and the voltage spikes far above the supply, straight into whatever just switched off.

**Motors sag the supply.** Starting a motor pulls a surge. If the Arduino shares that battery, its voltage dips and the board resets. A robot that "reboots every time it starts driving" is almost always this.

## The H-bridge: four switches that pick a direction

To reverse a DC motor you swap which terminal gets positive. An **H-bridge** does that with four electronic switches arranged like the letter H, with the motor as the crossbar.

```mermaid caption="Close S1 and S4 and current flows A to B through the motor. Close S3 and S2 and it flows B to A, so the motor reverses."
flowchart TB
    P["Motor supply +"] --> S1["S1"]
    P --> S3["S3"]
    S1 --> A(("A"))
    S3 --> B(("B"))
    A --- M["Motor"]
    M --- B
    A --> S2["S2"]
    B --> S4["S4"]
    S2 --> G["Ground"]
    S4 --> G
```

Closing S1 and S2 together would short the supply to ground (*shoot-through*). Driver chips such as the L298N handle the switches for you, so you only set three pins per motor:

| ENA (enable) | IN1 | IN2 | What the motor does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HIGH or PWM | HIGH | LOW | Forward |
| HIGH or PWM | LOW | HIGH | Reverse |
| HIGH or PWM | same as IN2 | same as IN1 | Brakes hard (both terminals tied together) |
| LOW | anything | anything | Coasts to a stop (motor disconnected) |

"Forward" just means one direction; if your wheel spins the wrong way, swap the two motor wires or swap HIGH and LOW in code.

## Give the motors their own battery, and share the ground

The safe layout has two power sources. The Arduino runs from USB (or its own regulator). The motors run from a battery pack wired to the driver's motor-supply terminal. The two systems share one thing: **ground**.

```mermaid caption="Two supplies, one ground. The Arduino only sends signals to the driver; the battery does the heavy lifting."
flowchart LR
    USB["USB or 5 V"] --> UNO["Arduino Uno"]
    BAT["Battery pack, 4 x AA"] --> DRV["Motor driver"]
    UNO -- "ENA, IN1, IN2 signals" --> DRV
    DRV --> MOT["DC motors"]
    UNO -. "GND" .- DRV
    BAT -. "GND" .- DRV
```

Why the shared ground? A signal of "5 V" means 5 V *compared to something*. Without a common ground the driver has no reference, and the motor twitches randomly or does nothing.

## Flyback: where the current goes when you switch off

Think of current in a motor coil as water in a long, heavy pipe. Slam a valve shut and the water hammers against it. A **flyback diode** is a bypass pipe: when the switch opens, the current loops back through the diode and dies away harmlessly.

In a one-transistor motor circuit you add the diode yourself, across the motor, stripe toward the positive supply. Most L298N modules sold for robots already include these diodes; check your board's description.

## PWM: speed from a switch that flips 490 times a second

An Arduino pin is either 0 V or 5 V, nothing between. **Pulse width modulation** fakes the in-between: switch fully on and off very fast, and vary the fraction of time spent on, the **duty cycle**.

`analogWrite(pin, value)` takes 0 (always off) to 255 (always on). On an Uno it works on pins 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, and 11, at about 490 Hz (980 Hz on pins 5 and 6). The motor's coil and weight smooth the pulses, so it responds to the *average*.

```python run alt="Three square waves at duty cycles of 25, 50 and 75 percent, each with a dashed line showing the average voltage of 1.5, 3.0 and 4.5 volts" caption="PWM on a 6 V motor supply: the pulse width changes, the height does not"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

period_ms = 1000 / 490        # one PWM cycle on most Uno pins
t = np.linspace(0, 3 * period_ms, 1500)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(3, 1, figsize=(7, 5), sharex=True)
for ax, value in zip(axes, [64, 128, 191]):
    duty = value / 255
    volts = np.where((t % period_ms) < duty * period_ms, 6.0, 0.0)
    ax.plot(t, volts, color="#7892ee")
    ax.axhline(6.0 * duty, color="#e8590c", linestyle="--", label=f"average {6.0 * duty:.1f} V")
    ax.set_ylabel(f"value {value}")
    ax.set_ylim(-0.5, 6.9)
    ax.legend(loc="upper right", fontsize=8)
axes[-1].set_xlabel("time (ms)")
fig.suptitle("analogWrite() on a 6 V motor supply")
plt.tight_layout()
```

The pulses all reach 6 V. Only their width changes, and with it the average.

The chart hides a catch: a motor needs a minimum voltage to overcome friction. Here's a rough model of a TT motor on an L298N (which loses about 2 V inside the chip), with every assumption written down so you can change it:

```python run alt="Line chart of modeled wheel speed against analogWrite value: flat at zero up to about 64, then rising in a straight line to about 135 rpm at 255" caption="A modeled motor: the bottom quarter of the range does nothing"
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

supply = 6.0        # volts from the battery pack
driver_loss = 2.0   # an L298N-style driver wastes about this much
start_volts = 1.0   # below this, friction wins and the wheel stays still
rpm_per_volt = 45   # rough slope for a small TT gear motor

values = list(range(0, 256))
speeds = []
for value in values:
    motor_volts = value / 255 * (supply - driver_loss)
    speeds.append(max(0, rpm_per_volt * (motor_volts - start_volts)))

first_moving = next(v for v, s in zip(values, speeds) if s > 0)
print("Lowest analogWrite value that turns the wheel:", first_moving)
print("Speed at 128:", round(speeds[128]), "rpm   Speed at 255:", round(speeds[255]), "rpm")

plt.plot(values, speeds, color="#7892ee")
plt.axvspan(0, first_moving, color="#e8590c", alpha=0.15, label="dead zone")
plt.xlabel("analogWrite value")
plt.ylabel("wheel speed (rpm, modeled)")
plt.legend()
```

Everything below 64 is a **dead zone**: the motor hums but the wheel doesn't move. Your motor's numbers will differ, but every DC motor has one.

Robot code usually describes speed as one signed number, −255 to 255, and a small function turns it into the three driver pins. Step through it:

:::trace Turn one signed speed into IN1, IN2 and a PWM value
```python
def drive(speed):
    speed = max(-255, min(255, speed))
    if speed > 0:
        in1, in2 = 1, 0
    elif speed < 0:
        in1, in2 = 0, 1
    else:
        in1, in2 = 0, 0
    return in1, in2, abs(speed)

for command in [200, -90, 0, 400]:
    print(command, "->", drive(command))
```
:::

Watch the last call: 400 is clamped to 255 first, so a bad calculation elsewhere can't ask the hardware for something impossible.

## Servos: a motor with its own feedback loop

A hobby servo hides a DC motor, a gearbox, a potentiometer on the output shaft, and a control chip. You send a pulse every 20 ms whose width (roughly 1,000 to 2,000 µs) names an angle. The chip compares it with the potentiometer and drives the motor until they match: feedback control, the big idea of the next module. The `Servo` library makes the pulses:

```arduino
#include <Servo.h>

Servo arm;

void setup() {
  arm.attach(9);        // signal wire on pin 9
}

void loop() {
  for (int angle = 0; angle <= 180; angle += 1) {
    arm.write(angle);   // 0 to 180 degrees
    delay(15);          // let the horn catch up
  }
  for (int angle = 180; angle >= 0; angle -= 1) {
    arm.write(angle);
    delay(15);
  }
}
```

Each direction takes 181 × 15 ms, about 2.7 seconds, and during that time the Arduino does nothing else. Remember that; it becomes a real problem once a robot must watch a sensor while it moves.

:::warning
**Four classic motor mistakes.**
1. **No shared ground.** The driver ignores the signals or the motor jitters. Wire Arduino GND to driver GND.
2. **ENA jumper left on.** On an L298N board the jumper ties ENA to 5 V, so the motor runs only at full speed. Pull it and wire ENA to a PWM pin.
3. **Servo plus PWM on pin 9 or 10.** On an Uno the `Servo` library disables `analogWrite()` on pins 9 and 10, even with no servo there. Put motor speed pins on 3, 5, 6, or 11.
4. **Motors on the Arduino's 5 V pin.** The surge resets the board.
:::

:::quiz
Q: An L298N has ENA = 255, IN1 = HIGH, IN2 = HIGH. What does the motor do?
- [ ] Spins forward at full speed
  Forward needs IN1 and IN2 to be different. Here they match.
- [x] Brakes to a quick stop
  Both motor terminals are tied to the same side of the bridge, so the motor's own spinning is shorted out and it stops fast.
- [ ] Shorts the battery
  That would happen with a hand-built H-bridge closing S1 and S2, but the driver chip's logic prevents it.
- [ ] Coasts to a stop
  Coasting is what ENA = LOW does: the motor is disconnected and friction slows it.

Q: Your code calls `analogWrite(5, 40)` and the wheel doesn't move, but `analogWrite(5, 150)` works. What's the most likely explanation?
- [ ] Pin 5 doesn't support PWM
  Pin 5 is a PWM pin on the Uno (at about 980 Hz). And 150 works on it.
- [x] 40 is inside the motor's dead zone
  About 16% duty gives an average too low to beat friction. The motor needs a minimum value before it turns.
- [ ] `analogWrite` needs `pinMode(5, OUTPUT)` first
  The Arduino reference says you don't need `pinMode()` before `analogWrite()`. And 150 works without it.

Q: A robot's Arduino resets every time both wheels start at once. Which change fixes the cause rather than the symptom?
- [ ] Add `delay(500)` at the start of `loop()`
  The motors still pull the same surge; you've only slowed the program.
- [ ] Use a smaller value in `analogWrite`
  Lower speed may reduce the surge a little, but the Arduino still shares a sagging supply.
- [x] Power the motors from their own battery and connect the grounds
  The surge now sags only the motor battery. The Arduino's supply stays steady.

:::

:::takeaways
- A pin supplies milliamps; a stalled motor wants over an amp. A driver switches battery current on the pin's command.
- On an L298N, IN1/IN2 pick the direction and ENA sets the speed.
- Motors get their own battery, everything shares one ground, and flyback diodes give switched-off current a safe path.
- `analogWrite(pin, 0–255)` sets the duty cycle. Expect a dead zone at low values.
- Servos take an angle with `write(0–180)`. The library costs you PWM on pins 9 and 10.
:::
