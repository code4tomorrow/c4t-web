A greenhouse controller prints `light = 512`. Is the greenhouse bright or dark? You can't say yet. That number went through three steps: light changed a sensor's resistance, a small circuit turned that resistance into a voltage, and the Uno turned the voltage into a number. To interpret a reading, you have to run those steps backwards. These notes go through each step.

## Digital inputs see only two answers

`digitalRead` asks a yes-or-no question: is this pin's voltage high or low? On a 5 V board like the Uno, Arduino's reference says a pin reads **HIGH** above **3.0 V** and **LOW** below **1.5 V**. Between those, the answer isn't guaranteed: the same pin might give either value.

Here's a voltage rising slowly from 0 to 5 V, and what `digitalRead` would make of it:

```python run alt="A straight line rising from 0 to 5 volts over 10 seconds, across three shaded bands: LOW below 1.5 volts, an uncertain band from 1.5 to 3.0 volts, and HIGH above 3.0 volts." caption="On a 5 V Uno, only voltages in the top and bottom bands have a guaranteed digital meaning."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

seconds = [t / 10 for t in range(101)]
volts = [t / 2 for t in seconds]

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3.8))
plt.axhspan(0, 1.5, color="#2a78d6", alpha=0.12)
plt.axhspan(1.5, 3.0, color="#eda100", alpha=0.15)
plt.axhspan(3.0, 5.2, color="#1baf7a", alpha=0.12)
plt.plot(seconds, volts, color="#0b0b0b", linewidth=2)
plt.text(0.2, 0.6, "reads LOW", color="#0b0b0b")
plt.text(0.2, 2.1, "not guaranteed", color="#0b0b0b")
plt.text(0.2, 4.2, "reads HIGH", color="#0b0b0b")
plt.xlabel("Time (s)")
plt.ylabel("Pin voltage (V)")
plt.ylim(0, 5.2)
plt.title("How digitalRead interprets a rising voltage")
plt.tight_layout()
```

That's why digital sensors are built to snap cleanly between near 0 V and near 5 V: buttons, door switches, tilt switches, and the "DO" (digital output) pin on many sensor modules. It's also why a floating pin is a problem: it wanders around the middle band.

## analogRead turns a voltage into a number

The pins A0 to A5 connect to an **analog-to-digital converter (ADC)**. The Uno's ADC has 10 bits, so it splits the range from 0 to 5 V into 1024 levels, numbered 0 to 1023. Each step is about 5 / 1024 ≈ **4.9 millivolts**.

To go from a reading back to volts, Arduino's own examples multiply by 5.0 / 1023.0:

**volts = reading × 5.0 / 1023.0**

```python run title="adc_to_volts.py"
for reading in [0, 1, 205, 512, 614, 1023]:
    volts = reading * 5.0 / 1023.0
    print(f"reading {reading:>4} -> {volts:.3f} V")
```

Some code, including Wokwi's sensor examples, divides by 1024 instead. The two answers differ by at most about 5 mV, less than one step, so either is fine for sensors.

The step size sets the finest change you can see. Zoom in on a tiny slice of the input range and the "smooth" analog reading turns out to be a staircase:

```python run alt="A staircase plot of ADC reading against input voltage from 2.400 to 2.430 volts. The reading jumps up by one count roughly every 4.9 millivolts, staying flat in between." caption="Zoomed in, every analog reading is a staircase. Changes smaller than about 4.9 mV are invisible to the Uno."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

volts = [2.400 + i * 0.0001 for i in range(301)]
readings = [int(v / 5.0 * 1023) for v in volts]

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3.5))
plt.plot(volts, readings, color="#2a78d6", linewidth=2, drawstyle="steps-post")
plt.xlabel("Input voltage (V)")
plt.ylabel("analogRead value")
plt.title("A 10-bit ADC, zoomed in to 30 millivolts")
plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
```

## Voltage dividers turn resistance into voltage

Many sensors don't produce a voltage at all. A **photoresistor** (also called an LDR, light-dependent resistor) just changes its resistance: high in the dark, low in bright light. A **thermistor** changes resistance with temperature. The ADC measures voltage, so we need a way to turn resistance into voltage.

The answer is a **voltage divider**: two resistances in series between 5 V and GND, with the output taken from the point between them.

```mermaid caption="A voltage divider. The output is a fraction of 5 V set by the two resistances."
flowchart TD
    V[5 V] --> R1["R_top (fixed 10 kΩ)"]
    R1 --> M((V_out to A0))
    M --> R2["R_bottom (photoresistor)"]
    R2 --> G[GND]
```

The same current flows through both, so each takes a share of the 5 V in proportion to its resistance:

**V_out = 5 V × R_bottom / (R_top + R_bottom)**

When the photoresistor sits on the bottom, bright light lowers its resistance, so V_out and the reading **fall**. Wokwi's photoresistor module acts like this circuit with a 10 kΩ resistor on top: its documented readings and its lux conversion code both work out to that arrangement. Let's model the photoresistor (Wokwi's defaults: 50 kΩ at 10 lux, with a "gamma" of 0.7 describing how fast resistance falls with light) and check against the values in Wokwi's documentation: about 853 at 10 lux, 511 at 100 lux, and 170 at 1000 lux.

```python run alt="Line chart on a logarithmic light axis from 1 to 10,000 lux. The analogRead value falls from about 980 at 1 lux, through about 852 at 10 lux, 511 at 100 lux and 170 at 1000 lux, to about 40 at 10,000 lux." caption="The divider model reproduces Wokwi's documented readings. Brighter light gives a lower number."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

R_TOP = 10_000      # fixed resistor, ohms
RL10 = 50_000       # photoresistor resistance at 10 lux
GAMMA = 0.7

def reading_at(lux):
    r_ldr = RL10 * (10 / lux) ** GAMMA
    volts = 5.0 * r_ldr / (R_TOP + r_ldr)
    return round(volts / 5.0 * 1023)

for lux in [10, 100, 1000]:
    print(f"{lux:>5} lux -> analogRead about {reading_at(lux)}")

lux_values = [10 ** (i / 20) for i in range(81)]
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3.8))
plt.plot(lux_values, [reading_at(x) for x in lux_values], color="#2a78d6", linewidth=2)
plt.xscale("log")
plt.xlabel("Light (lux, log scale)")
plt.ylabel("analogRead value")
plt.title("Photoresistor in a divider: more light, lower reading")
plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
```

The model matches Wokwi's numbers to within one count. So in Wokwi, `light = 512` means about 100 lux: a dim room, far darker than a sunny greenhouse. With a real kit you build the same divider from a bare photoresistor and a 10 kΩ resistor. Swap the two parts, and the reading rises with light instead.

## Reading temperature

Two temperature sensors are common in starter kits.

**An NTC thermistor** is a resistor whose resistance falls as it warms (NTC means "negative temperature coefficient"). In a divider with a 10 kΩ resistor, its reading converts to temperature with the Beta equation, the formula Wokwi's NTC sensor documentation uses (Beta is 3950 for that part, and 298.15 is 25 °C in kelvin).

**The TMP36** is a chip that outputs a voltage directly: 0.5 V at 0 °C, rising 10 mV for every degree. So °C = (volts − 0.5) × 100.

```python run title="temperatures.py"
import math

BETA = 3950

def ntc_celsius(reading):
    return 1 / (math.log(1 / (1023.0 / reading - 1)) / BETA + 1.0 / 298.15) - 273.15

def tmp36_celsius(reading):
    volts = reading * 5.0 / 1023.0
    return (volts - 0.5) * 100

print("NTC module:")
for reading in [684, 569, 512, 456]:
    print(f"  reading {reading} -> {ntc_celsius(reading):5.1f} C")
print("TMP36:")
for reading in [102, 153, 164]:
    print(f"  reading {reading} -> {tmp36_celsius(reading):5.1f} C")
```

Notice that the NTC reading falls as it gets warmer, while the TMP36 reading rises. You can't guess a sensor's direction; you read its documentation, or you test it.

Here's how common sensors produce their signals:

| Sensor | Signal | How the Uno reads it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Button, tilt switch, door switch | Digital: near 0 V or near 5 V | `digitalRead` |
| Photoresistor, thermistor | Resistance, turned into a voltage by a divider | `analogRead` |
| Potentiometer, TMP36, soil moisture probe | A voltage between 0 and 5 V | `analogRead` |
| Ultrasonic distance sensor (HC-SR04) | A digital pulse whose length depends on distance | Timing the pulse, covered with robots later |

Soil moisture probes deserve a warning: some give higher readings when wet and others when dry. Record a reading in dry soil and one in wet soil before writing any thresholds.

## Smoothing noisy readings

Real analog readings jitter. Electrical noise, a flickering light, or a loose wire can make a steady sensor read 510, 514, 507, 523, 511. A **moving average** keeps the last few readings and reports their average, so one odd value gets diluted. Step through this one with a window of 3, and watch what happens to the spike of 900:

:::trace Watch a moving average swallow a spike
```python
readings = [500, 520, 480, 900, 510, 505]
window = []
for r in readings:
    window.append(r)
    if len(window) > 3:
        window.pop(0)
    average = sum(window) // len(window)
    print(r, "->", average)
```
:::

The spike raises the average for three readings instead of appearing once at full size. Here's the effect on a longer, noisy signal where the light really does change halfway through:

```python run alt="Line chart of 120 readings. Raw readings jitter by about plus or minus 15 around 400, then jump to around 600 at reading 60. A 10-reading moving average follows the same steps with far less jitter, but takes about 10 readings to climb from 400 to 600." caption="A 10-reading moving average removes most of the jitter, but reacts to the real change about 10 readings late."
import random
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

random.seed(4)
true_level = [400] * 60 + [600] * 60
raw = [level + random.randint(-15, 15) for level in true_level]

WINDOW = 10
smoothed = []
for i in range(len(raw)):
    recent = raw[max(0, i - WINDOW + 1): i + 1]
    smoothed.append(sum(recent) / len(recent))

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3.8))
plt.plot(raw, color="#eb6834", linewidth=1, label="raw analogRead")
plt.plot(smoothed, color="#2a78d6", linewidth=2, label="10-reading average")
plt.xlabel("Reading number")
plt.ylabel("Value")
plt.title("Smoothing trades jitter for delay")
plt.legend()
plt.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
```

That delay is the price of smoothing. A bigger window gives a calmer line but reacts more slowly. For a greenhouse, a few seconds of delay is fine; for a robot about to hit a wall, it might not be.

:::warning
**Classic analog mistakes.**
Integer math: `float volts = reading * 5 / 1023;` computes with whole numbers *before* storing, so a reading of 600 gives exactly 2.00 V instead of 2.93 V. Write `5.0` and `1023.0` to force decimal math.
Reading a pin with nothing attached: an unconnected analog pin floats and returns drifting nonsense, often influenced by the pin next to it. If a reading wanders on its own, check the wire first.
Mixing up `A0` and `0`: `analogRead(A0)` reads analog pin A0. Digital pin 0 is the serial receive pin.
:::

:::quiz
Q: A sensor's digital output pin sits at 2.2 V. What does `digitalRead` return on a 5 V Uno?
- [ ] HIGH, because 2.2 V is more than zero
  HIGH is only guaranteed above 3.0 V.
- [ ] LOW, because 2.2 V is under half of 5 V
  LOW is only guaranteed below 1.5 V.
- [x] It isn't guaranteed; it could read either way
  2.2 V is in the band between 1.5 V and 3.0 V, where the result can be either.

Q: `analogRead(A0)` returns 614. About what voltage is on A0?
- [ ] 6.14 V
  The Uno's analog pins only measure 0 to 5 V.
- [x] About 3.0 V
  614 × 5.0 / 1023 ≈ 3.0 V.
- [ ] About 1.2 V
  That would be a reading near 245.

Q: A sketch has `float volts = reading * 5 / 1023;` and `reading` is 600. What does `Serial.println(volts)` show?
- [ ] 2.93
  That's the right answer, but this line computes with whole numbers before storing the result.
- [x] 2.00
  `600 * 5 / 1023` is whole-number math: 3000 / 1023 = 2, then it's stored as 2.00.
- [ ] A compile error
  Assigning an int to a float is allowed; it just loses the decimals that were already thrown away.

Q: You change a moving average from 5 readings to 50. What happens?
- [ ] The readings become more accurate and respond faster
  You can't get both. A bigger window is smoother but slower.
- [x] The output is smoother but reacts more slowly to real changes
  Each new reading is only a fiftieth of the average, so a real change takes about 50 readings to show fully.
- [ ] Nothing changes, because the average of noise is the same
  More readings dilute each random wiggle further, so the output does get calmer.
:::

:::takeaways
- On a 5 V Uno, `digitalRead` gives HIGH above 3.0 V and LOW below 1.5 V; anything between is not guaranteed.
- `analogRead` turns 0 to 5 V into 0 to 1023, about 4.9 mV per step. Volts = reading × 5.0 / 1023.0.
- A voltage divider turns a sensor's resistance into a voltage: V_out = 5 V × R_bottom / (R_top + R_bottom).
- Sensors differ in direction: check whether yours reads higher or lower as light, heat, or moisture rises.
- A moving average reduces jitter, at the cost of a delay that grows with the window size.
:::
