A student builds two LED circuits side by side. Every wire matches the diagram, both resistors are correct, and one LED stays dark. Swapping the LED doesn't help. The fault is invisible from above: both of that LED's legs are pushed into the same row of five holes. Under the plastic, that row is a single metal strip, so current runs along the strip and skips the LED entirely.

A breadboard only makes sense once you know where its hidden metal strips are.

## What's under the holes

A solderless breadboard is a plastic block with rows of holes, and under the holes are springy metal clips that grip a wire or a component leg. The clips are joined in a fixed pattern:

-   **Terminal strips.** Each numbered row is split into two groups of five holes: **a–e** on one side and **f–j** on the other. The five holes in a group are connected to each other.
-   **The center gap.** The channel down the middle separates the a–e group from the f–j group, so hole 10e is **not** connected to hole 10f.
-   **Power rails.** The long lines of holes along the edges, marked **+** (often red) and **−** (often blue), are connected along their whole length. On many full-size boards each rail is split in the middle; a break in the printed red or blue line marks the split.

![The back of a breadboard with its cover removed, showing the metal strips that join each row, with one strip pulled out](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/95/Metal_contacts_within_a_breadboard.jpg "Photo: Zeroping, CC BY 4.0")

Here is the same pattern as a map. Every `●─●─●─●─●` group is one connected strip:

```text
          a   b   c   d   e       f   g   h   i   j
row 1     ●───●───●───●───●       ●───●───●───●───●
row 2     ●───●───●───●───●       ●───●───●───●───●
row 3     ●───●───●───●───●       ●───●───●───●───●
                          ↑ center gap ↑
          (1a to 1e are joined; 1e and 1f are not)

+ rail    ●───●───●───●───●───●───●───●───●───●───●   joined along the length
− rail    ●───●───●───●───●───●───●───●───●───●───●
```

## Think in nodes, not holes

A **node** is a set of points that are all connected together, so they are all at the same voltage. On a breadboard, each five-hole group is one node, and each rail is one node (or two, if it's split).

A component does useful work only when its legs sit in **two different nodes**. A resistor with both legs in row 12, a–e side, is "shorted out": current goes along the metal strip and ignores it. That is exactly what happened to the dark LED in the opening story.

Here is a correct layout for an LED circuit, drawn as nodes. Each box is one node on the breadboard; each arrow is a part or a wire that bridges two nodes.

```mermaid caption="An LED circuit as nodes: every part bridges two different nodes"
flowchart LR
    P[Uno pin 9] -->|jumper wire| A["Row 10 (a–e)"]
    A -->|220 Ω resistor| B["Row 14 (a–e)"]
    B -->|"LED (long leg in row 14)"| C["Row 15 (a–e)"]
    C -->|jumper wire| N["− rail"]
    N -->|jumper wire| G[Uno GND]
```

To check any breadboard, trace the loop node by node: from the pin, through each part, back to GND. If you ever pass through a part whose two legs are in the same node, or you reach a node with nothing leading out, you've found the fault.

## LEDs and resistors

**LEDs have polarity.** Current only flows from the **anode** (+) to the **cathode** (−). On a real LED the anode is the **longer leg**, and the rim of the plastic has a **flat edge** on the cathode side. Put one in backwards and it simply stays dark. With a resistor in the loop on a 5 V circuit that normally doesn't harm it, so flipping a dark LED is a reasonable first test. In Wokwi the LED's pins are named **A** and **C**.

**Resistors have no polarity**; either way round works. Their value is printed as colored bands. On the common four-band resistor, the first two bands are digits, the third is a multiplier (how many zeros to add), and the fourth (gold or silver, set slightly apart) is the tolerance.

| Color | Digit | Color | Digit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| black | 0 | green | 5 |
| brown | 1 | blue | 6 |
| red | 2 | violet | 7 |
| orange | 3 | grey | 8 |
| yellow | 4 | white | 9 |

So red-red-brown means 2, 2, then one zero: 220 Ω. Gold tolerance means the real value is within ±5%. Here is the rule as code:

```python run title="color_code.py"
DIGITS = ["black", "brown", "red", "orange", "yellow",
          "green", "blue", "violet", "grey", "white"]
TOLERANCE = {"gold": 5, "silver": 10, "brown": 1}

def decode(bands):
    first, second, multiplier, tolerance = bands
    value = (DIGITS.index(first) * 10 + DIGITS.index(second)) * 10 ** DIGITS.index(multiplier)
    return value, TOLERANCE[tolerance]

for bands in [["red", "red", "brown", "gold"],
              ["brown", "black", "orange", "gold"],
              ["yellow", "violet", "red", "gold"]]:
    ohms, plus_minus = decode(bands)
    print(f"{'-'.join(bands):<28} {ohms:>6} ohms  +/-{plus_minus}%")
```

Notice that brown-black-orange is 10,000 Ω (10 kΩ): the third band is orange, meaning three zeros, not the digit 3. Precision resistors use five bands (three digits, then multiplier and tolerance), so count the bands before decoding. When colors are hard to tell apart, measure with a multimeter instead of guessing.

## Pushbuttons and the pull-up resistor

A small tactile pushbutton has **four legs**, but only **two connections**. The legs come in pairs that are always joined inside; pressing the button joins one pair to the other. In Wokwi, legs `1.l` and `1.r` are one pair and `2.l` and `2.r` are the other. Set the button's `xray` attribute to `"1"` in `diagram.json` and Wokwi draws the internal wiring for you.

On a breadboard, place the button **across the center gap**, so each pair lands in its own row. If you rotate it 90°, the always-joined legs end up on opposite sides, and the button looks permanently pressed.

There is a second trap. A pin set to plain `INPUT` with only a button attached is **floating** when the button is open: it isn't connected to 5V or GND, so it picks up electrical noise and reads random HIGHs and LOWs. The fix is a **pull-up resistor** that gently holds the pin at 5 V until the button connects it to GND. The Uno has one built into every pin, about 20 kΩ, switched on with `pinMode(pin, INPUT_PULLUP)`.

```mermaid caption="INPUT_PULLUP: the pin reads HIGH until the button connects it to GND"
flowchart LR
    V[5 V inside the chip] -->|"built-in 20 kΩ"| P[Pin 2]
    P -->|button, when pressed| G[GND]
    P -.->|digitalRead| R["HIGH when open, LOW when pressed"]
```

This flips the logic: **pressed reads LOW**. It also explains why the button isn't a short circuit. When pressed, the 20 kΩ resistor sits between 5 V and GND, so only 5 / 20,000 = 0.25 mA flows.

Buttons have one more quirk: when the metal contacts close, they **bounce** apart and together several times within about a millisecond before settling. Your code can see one press as several. Wokwi simulates this bounce by default, and the practice lesson at the end of this module shows how to handle it.

## Potentiometers and buzzers

A **potentiometer** ("pot") is a resistor with a sliding contact, the **wiper**, controlled by a knob. Connect its two outer legs to 5V and GND, and the middle leg outputs any voltage between 0 and 5 V depending on where the knob is turned. Read it with `analogRead`, which gives 0 at one end, 1023 at the other, and about 512 in the middle. In Wokwi the pot's pins are labeled **VCC**, **SIG** (the wiper), and **GND**.

A **buzzer** makes sound. An *active* buzzer beeps on its own when given power. A *passive* buzzer needs a changing signal, which `tone(pin, frequency)` produces, so it can play different notes. Both connect between a pin and GND.

## Reading a schematic

A **schematic** shows *which things are connected*, not where they sit. Lines are wires; wherever lines meet with a dot, they form one node. These are the symbols you'll see in this course:

| Part | Symbol |
| --- | --- |
| Resistor | A zigzag line (US style) or a small rectangle (European style) |
| LED | A triangle pointing in the direction of current, with a bar on the cathode side and two small arrows pointing away |
| Pushbutton | A line with a gap, and a small bar above the gap that bridges it when pressed |
| Potentiometer | A resistor symbol with an arrow pointing at its middle (the wiper) |
| Battery | Pairs of long and short parallel lines; the long line is + |
| Ground | Three horizontal lines getting shorter, like an upside-down triangle |

Turning a schematic into a breadboard layout is a matching job. Give each node in the schematic its own breadboard row (or a rail, for 5V and GND), then place each part so its legs land in the two rows it connects. The node diagram earlier in this lesson is that translation, written out.

:::warning
**Three layout faults that look fine from above.**
Both legs of a part in one five-hole group: the part is bypassed. Move one leg to another row.
A circuit that uses both halves of a split power rail: the far half has no power. Bridge the gap with a short jumper, or keep everything on one half.
A pushbutton turned 90°: it reads as always pressed. Rotate it so it straddles the center gap.
:::

:::quiz
Q: An LED's legs are in holes 12a and 12e, and a resistor connects row 12 to pin 9. What happens?
- [ ] The LED lights normally
  12a and 12e are in the same five-hole group, so both LED legs are on one node.
- [x] The LED never lights, because both legs are on the same node
  With no voltage between its legs, no current flows through the LED.
- [ ] The LED lights, but dimly
  It's not about brightness; the LED is completely bypassed.

Q: Is hole 20e connected to hole 20f?
- [ ] Yes, they're in the same numbered row
  Same row number, but on opposite sides of the center gap.
- [x] No, the center gap separates them
  a–e and f–j are separate groups, which is why a button or chip can straddle the gap.

Q: A button is wired between pin 2 and GND, with `pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP)`. What does `digitalRead(2)` return while the button is held down?
- [ ] HIGH, because pressing means "on"
  With a pull-up, pressing connects the pin to ground.
- [x] LOW
  The pressed button pulls the pin to GND; when released, the internal resistor pulls it back to HIGH.
- [ ] A random value
  Random values happen with a floating `INPUT`. The pull-up prevents that.

Q: A pot's outer legs go to 5V and GND, and its middle leg goes to A0. The knob is turned a quarter of the way from the GND end. About what does `analogRead(A0)` return?
- [ ] 1023
  That's the reading with the wiper all the way at the 5V end.
- [x] About 256
  A quarter of the way gives about a quarter of 5 V, and a quarter of 1023 is about 256.
- [ ] 0
  That's the reading at the GND end.
:::

:::takeaways
- Each five-hole group (a–e or f–j in one row) is one node; the center gap separates the two sides; rails run lengthwise and may be split.
- Every part must bridge two different nodes. Two legs in one group means the part is bypassed.
- LED: long leg (anode) toward the supply, flat edge (cathode) toward GND. Resistors: read digit, digit, multiplier, tolerance.
- A button straddles the center gap. With `INPUT_PULLUP`, it reads LOW when pressed and HIGH when released.
- A schematic shows connections, not positions: give each node its own breadboard row.
:::
