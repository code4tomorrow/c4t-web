A computer can add up a million numbers in under a second. Ask it to make you a sandwich, though, and it's stuck. It has no idea what "spread" means, which side of the bread is up, or when the sandwich is finished. Someone has to spell out every step, in the right order, with nothing left to guess.

That someone is a programmer, and spelling out steps is the skill this course teaches. You won't memorize a programming language here. You'll learn how to think about problems so that, whichever language you pick later, you already know what to tell it.

You need two kinds of tools: paper ones for thinking, and Scratch for testing whether your thinking works.

## Your paper toolkit

About half of this course happens away from the screen. Programmers do this too: they sketch a plan, walk through it by hand, and only then type it in. Collect these before Module 2:

-   **A notebook** (or a stack of paper in a folder). This becomes your algorithm notebook. The third lesson of this module shows how to lay out a page.
-   **A pencil with an eraser.** Pencil, not pen, because your first try at any algorithm will have a mistake in it. That's normal, and finding it is part of the work.
-   **Grid paper**, or plain paper with a grid you draw with a ruler. Module 3 has a robot that moves square by square.
-   **A deck of playing cards**, or ten scraps of paper numbered 1 to 10. Later modules use them for searching and sorting.
-   **Sticky notes or small paper strips.** Writing one step per strip lets you shuffle steps around until the order is right.
-   **A helper (optional).** A friend, sibling, or parent who will pretend to be a robot and follow your instructions exactly, even when that's silly.

None of this needs to be bought. Scrap paper and a pencil cover every activity.

## Your computer toolkit

-   **A computer.** A Windows laptop or desktop, a Mac, or a Chromebook. A tablet can build Scratch projects too, but dragging blocks with a finger is slow. A phone can only play projects, not build them.
-   **A current web browser.** Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or Safari. Internet Explorer can't run Scratch 3.
-   **An internet connection**, or the free offline Scratch app (below).
-   **A mouse (nice to have).** Blocks are much easier to drag with a mouse than with a trackpad.

## Open the Scratch editor

Scratch is a free, block-based coding tool made at MIT. Instead of typing code, you snap colored blocks together, so you can test an idea in seconds without worrying about spelling or punctuation.

The quickest way in is the online editor. You don't need an account to use it.

:::tabs
@tab Windows
1.  Open **Chrome**, **Edge**, or **Firefox** from the Start menu.
2.  Click the address bar at the top, type `scratch.mit.edu/projects/editor`, and press **Enter**.
3.  Wait a few seconds for the editor to load.
4.  Press **Ctrl + D** to bookmark the page so you can get back to it in one click.

**Prefer an app that works offline?** Go to `scratch.mit.edu/download`, choose Windows, and get the Scratch app from the Microsoft Store. Then open **Scratch 3** from the Start menu.
@tab macOS
1.  Open **Safari** or **Chrome** (both are fine).
2.  Click the address bar, type `scratch.mit.edu/projects/editor`, and press **Return**.
3.  Wait a few seconds for the editor to load.
4.  Press **Cmd + D** to bookmark it.

**Prefer an app that works offline?** Go to `scratch.mit.edu/download`, choose macOS, and either get it from the Mac App Store or download it directly. With the direct download, open the `.dmg` file and drag **Scratch 3** into your **Applications** folder.
@tab Chromebook
1.  Open **Chrome**.
2.  Click the address bar, type `scratch.mit.edu/projects/editor`, and press **Enter**.
3.  Wait a few seconds for the editor to load.
4.  Press **Ctrl + D** to bookmark it.

**Prefer an app?** Many Chromebooks can install Scratch from the Google Play Store; `scratch.mit.edu/download` links to it. School Chromebooks sometimes block Play Store apps, and that's fine: the browser editor does everything this course needs.
:::

### What you should see

When the editor has loaded, you'll see three main areas:

```text
Left:    the block palette (colored circles: Motion, Looks, Sound, Events,
         Control, Sensing, Operators, Variables, My Blocks) and the blocks in each
Middle:  the code area, an empty space where you build scripts
Right:   the stage with an orange cat, a green flag and a red stop sign above it,
         and the list of sprites underneath
```

If you see the cat, Scratch is working.

## A ten-second test

Find the blue **Motion** blocks on the left. Click once, right in the palette, on this block (you don't need to drag it anywhere):

```scratch
move (10) steps
```

The cat on the stage should shift a little to the right. Click it five more times and the cat keeps going. Clicking a block in the palette runs it once, which is a handy way to check what a block does before you use it in a script.

## Account or no account?

Both choices work for every lesson in this course.

| | No account | With an account |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Build and run projects | Yes | Yes |
| Where your work is saved | A file on your computer, which you save yourself | Online, in **My Stuff**, from any computer |
| Sharing projects with others | No | Yes, if you choose to |
| Needs a grown-up's help | No | Yes |

To make one, click **Join Scratch** at the top of `scratch.mit.edu`. Scratch asks for a username, a password, your country, and your birth month and year. If you're young, it asks for a parent's or guardian's email address, and in some places a grown-up has to approve the account before it works. Do this together with that grown-up.

:::warning
**Keep your username anonymous.** Scratch's own advice is "Don't use your real name." Leave out your school and your age too. "OrbitFox27" is a good username. "MayaLopez2014" tells strangers your name and roughly how old you are.
:::

:::tip
Not sure yet? Start without an account. Everything you save as a file can be uploaded to an account later.
:::

## When Scratch won't cooperate

-   **The page stays blank, or says your browser isn't supported.** Update your browser, or try a different one. On an old computer, Chrome or Firefox usually works best.
-   **The site is blocked on a school computer.** Ask your teacher whether `scratch.mit.edu` can be unblocked, or use a home computer. Every paper activity still works in the meantime.
-   **Blocks won't drag on a tablet.** Press and hold a block for a moment before you move your finger, or plug in a mouse.
-   **Everything is slow.** Close other tabs and apps. Scratch runs inside your browser, so a browser with 30 tabs open has less room for it.
-   **The editor is in the wrong language.** Click the globe icon at the top-left of the editor and pick your language.
-   **There's no sound.** Check your computer's volume and make sure the browser tab isn't muted. Sound is a small part of this course, so don't let it hold you up.
