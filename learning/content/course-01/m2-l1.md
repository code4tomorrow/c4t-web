:::objectives
- Describe a problem by its goal, inputs, constraints, and output.
- Break a big problem into smaller pieces you can solve one at a time.
- Know when a piece is small enough to stop splitting.
- Spot a piece that repeats, and solve it once.
- Rewrite a fuzzy instruction so that anyone would follow it the same way.
:::

Saturday, 9:55 a.m., at the Riverside Community Center. The coding club runs from 10:00 to 11:00. There are 6 working computers. There are 12 kids at the door, and more are walking up the path. The volunteer in charge, Ms. Okafor, got exactly one instruction from her manager: "Make it fair."

What should she actually do?

"Make it fair" is a wish, not a plan. Nobody can follow it step by step, because it doesn't say what to do first, or what "fair" means, or how anyone will know it worked. Turning a wish like that into steps is the first job of every programmer, and it's what this module is about.

## Start by asking what the problem actually is

Before solving anything, pin the problem down with four questions:

| Question | What it means | At the community center |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Goal** | What does "done" look like? | Every kid gets the same amount of computer time during the hour. |
| **Inputs** | What information do you start with? | 12 kids, 6 computers, 60 minutes. |
| **Constraints** | Which rules can't be broken? | One kid per computer at a time. The club ends at 11:00. Nobody waits the whole hour. |
| **Output** | What do you hand back at the end? | A schedule: who uses a computer, and when. |

Each row does a different job. The **goal** tells you when to stop. The **inputs** are the facts you'll work with, and they change: next Saturday there might be 9 kids, or 20. A good plan works for any inputs, not just today's. The **constraints** rule out answers that look fine but aren't allowed. "Give every kid the whole hour" is fair, but it needs 12 computers, and there are 6. The **output** is the thing you'll be able to point at and check.

Writing these down takes two minutes, and it catches a surprising number of mistakes before they happen.

## Decomposition: cut the problem into pieces

Nobody cleans a disaster of a bedroom by "cleaning the room". You do the clothes, then the books, then whatever is under the bed. Each of those is small enough to start right away.

Programmers call this **decomposition**: splitting a problem into smaller sub-problems, each simple enough to solve on its own, so that solving all of them solves the whole thing. If a piece is still too big, you split it again.

Here is Ms. Okafor's problem, decomposed:

```mermaid caption="Splitting 'make it fair' until every piece is something you know how to do"
flowchart TD
    A[Run a fair computer hour] --> B[Work out the turns]
    A --> C[Decide who goes when]
    A --> D[Run the turns]
    B --> B1[How many rounds?]
    B --> B2[How long is each round?]
    C --> C1[List kids in sign-in order]
    C --> C2[Split the list into rounds]
    D --> D1[Start a timer]
    D --> D2[Swap kids when it rings]
```

Look at the bottom row. Every box is something Ms. Okafor could do right now, without asking anyone a question:

-   **How many rounds?** 12 kids ÷ 6 computers = 2 rounds.
-   **How long is each round?** 60 minutes ÷ 2 rounds = 30 minutes.
-   **List kids in sign-in order.** Use the sign-in sheet at the door.
-   **Split the list into rounds.** The first 6 names go in round 1, the next 6 in round 2.

That's the rule for when to stop splitting: **stop when a piece is obvious.** If you'd need to ask "but how?", it needs splitting again.

Decomposing also means you can check each piece by itself. If the schedule ends up wrong, you don't have to stare at the whole plan. You can check "how many rounds?" on its own, then "how long?", and find the piece with the mistake.

## Some pieces repeat, so solve them once

Look at "Run the turns". In round 1, she starts a 30-minute timer and the first 6 kids log on. When it rings, they log off. In round 2, she starts a 30-minute timer and the next 6 kids log on. When it rings, they log off.

That's the same piece twice. The only thing that changes is *which* kids are sitting down. So she can write it once:

```text
Run one round:
  1. Call the next 6 names on the list.
  2. Start a 30-minute timer.
  3. When the timer rings, those kids log off.
```

...and then say "run one round, twice." If 18 kids show up, it's "three times", and the piece itself doesn't change at all.

Spotting a repeated piece is one of the most useful habits in programming. You solve it once, test it once, and reuse it. Notice the part that changes, though (here, *which* names). A reusable piece always has a blank in it that gets filled in differently each time. Module 4 shows how computers repeat a piece like this with loops.

## Turn each piece into instructions anyone can follow

Once you have pieces, each one needs instructions so clear that two different people would do the same thing. Here's the test: **hand the instruction to two people. If they could do different things, it isn't precise yet.**

| Fuzzy | Precise |
| --- | --- |
| Give everyone a turn. | The first 6 names on the sign-in sheet use computers from 10:00 to 10:30. |
| Swap when it's time. | At 10:30, those 6 kids log off, and the next 6 names log on until 11:00. |
| Be fair to latecomers. | Anyone who arrives after 10:00 is added to the bottom of the list. |

The fuzzy versions aren't wrong exactly; they just leave the important decisions to whoever happens to be reading. "Swap when it's time" could mean every 10 minutes, or whenever someone gets bored. The precise version can only mean one thing.

## Mistakes to watch for

:::warning
**A piece that's really the whole problem again.** A plan with the step "make the schedule fair" hasn't decomposed anything; that's the original problem wearing a new label. Keep splitting until each piece is something you could do this minute.
:::

:::warning
**Solving only today's numbers.** "Split the kids into 2 rounds" works for 12 kids and 6 computers, and breaks when 18 kids turn up. Write the piece as a rule that uses the inputs: rounds = kids ÷ computers (rounded up when it doesn't come out even, which the next lesson handles). Then it works on any Saturday.
:::

:::warning
**Forgetting a constraint.** It's easy to make a schedule that's fair and accidentally runs until 11:30. After you finish, read your plan against every constraint on your list, one at a time.
:::

:::quiz
Q: You're planning a class pizza order so that everyone gets 2 slices. Which of these is an **input**?
- [x] The number of people in the class
  Right. It's a fact you start with, and it changes from class to class.
- [ ] Everyone gets 2 slices
  That's a rule the plan has to follow, so it's a constraint (or part of the goal), not an input.
- [ ] The number of pizzas to order
  That's what the plan produces at the end, so it's the output.

Q: Which of these pieces still needs to be split further?
- [ ] Count the kids on the sign-in sheet
  You could do this right now, so it's small enough.
- [x] Make sure the hour goes well
  You'd have to ask "but how?" That's the sign of a piece that's still too big.
- [ ] Divide the number of kids by the number of computers
  This is a single, exact calculation. Nothing left to split.

Q: Next Saturday, 18 kids come, with the same 6 computers and 60 minutes. Using the rules from this lesson, how long is each turn?
- [ ] 10 minutes
  That's 60 ÷ 6, dividing by computers instead of rounds. Work out the rounds first.
- [x] 20 minutes
  18 ÷ 6 = 3 rounds, and 60 ÷ 3 = 20 minutes each.
- [ ] 30 minutes
  That's last Saturday's answer. A rule that only works for one set of numbers isn't a rule yet.

Q: Which instruction would two different volunteers carry out in exactly the same way?
- [ ] Let kids swap when they're done
  "Done" means different things to different kids, so the swaps would happen at different times.
- [ ] Give the younger kids a bit longer
  "Younger" and "a bit" both leave the details to whoever is reading.
- [x] At 10:30, the 6 kids on computers log off and the next 6 names on the list log on
  Every part is exact: when, who, and what happens.
:::

:::takeaways
- Pin a problem down with four questions: goal, inputs, constraints, and output.
- Decomposition splits a big problem into smaller pieces. Stop splitting when a piece is obvious.
- Check each piece by itself; a mistake is much easier to find in a small piece.
- When a piece repeats, solve it once and notice the blank that changes each time.
- An instruction is precise when two different people would carry it out the same way.
:::
