:::objectives
- Plan a program as pieces on paper before building anything.
- Use Scratch's `ask` block to collect inputs and variables to remember them.
- Build and test one piece at a time, so every bug has a small place to hide.
- Check a finished program against answers you worked out by hand.
:::

Ms. Okafor from the community center doesn't want to do division at the door every Saturday. She wants a helper: type in the morning's numbers, and it tells her how many rounds to run and how long each turn is. You're going to build it in Scratch, and you're going to build it the way the last lesson described, one piece at a time.

## What we're building

When it's finished, a conversation with the helper looks like this:

```text
Cat:  How many kids came today?             You type: 9
Cat:  How many computers work?              You type: 6
Cat:  How many minutes is the club open?    You type: 60
Cat:  2 rounds.
Cat:  Each turn is 30 minutes.
```

Inside, it's four pieces, and you'll test each one before starting the next:

```mermaid caption="The helper's four pieces, built and tested left to right"
flowchart LR
    A["1. Ask for the inputs"] --> B["2. Work out the rounds"]
    B --> C["3. Work out the turn length"]
    C --> D["4. Announce the answer"]
```

## Step 1: Plan it on paper

Open your notebook to a fresh page and fill it in before you touch Scratch:

```text
Problem:  Tell the volunteer how many rounds to run and how long each turn is.
Inputs:   kids, computers, minutes
Output:   rounds, turn length
Steps:    1. Ask how many kids. Remember the answer.
          2. Ask how many computers. Remember the answer.
          3. Ask how many minutes. Remember the answer.
          4. rounds = kids ÷ computers, rounded UP to a whole number.
          5. turn = minutes ÷ rounds.
          6. Say the rounds, then say the turn length.
```

Now work two examples by hand. These are your tests, so write the answers down.

-   12 kids, 6 computers, 60 minutes: 12 ÷ 6 = 2 rounds, and 60 ÷ 2 = 30 minutes.
-   9 kids, 6 computers, 60 minutes: 9 ÷ 6 = 1.5. You can't run one and a half rounds, so round up to 2. Each turn is 30 minutes, and 3 computers sit empty in round 2.

Why round **up**? If you round 1.5 down to 1, only 6 kids get a turn and 3 kids sit out the whole hour. That breaks the constraint "nobody waits the whole hour".

## Step 2: Make the variables

A **variable** is a labeled box where a program keeps a value it needs to remember. The helper needs five boxes.

1.  Click the orange **Variables** circle.
2.  Click **Make a Variable**, type `kids`, and click **OK**.
3.  Do the same for `computers`, `minutes`, `rounds`, and `turn`.

Each new variable appears in the palette with a checkbox beside it. Checked variables also show up on the stage as small boxes called **monitors**, which display the current value. Leave them checked; they're how you'll test each piece. (Scratch starts you with one called `my variable`. You can ignore it.)

## Step 3: Piece 1, ask for the inputs

The `ask` block and the `answer` block are in the light-blue **Sensing** category. The `set` block is in **Variables**; use its dropdown to choose which box to fill.

```scratch
when green flag clicked
ask [How many kids came today?] and wait
set [kids v] to (answer)
ask [How many computers work?] and wait
set [computers v] to (answer)
ask [How many minutes is the club open?] and wait
set [minutes v] to (answer)
```

`ask` shows a question and waits until someone types a reply at the bottom of the stage and presses **Enter**. Whatever they typed goes into `answer`. The `set` block right after it copies `answer` into a variable, because the next `ask` will overwrite `answer` with a new reply.

**Test it.** Click the green flag and type 12, 6, and 60. Look at the monitors on the stage:

```text
kids  12      computers  6      minutes  60
```

If a monitor shows the wrong number, the mistake is somewhere in these seven blocks and nowhere else. That's the reason for testing a piece before building the next one.

Notice something about this piece: it's the same two blocks, `ask` then `set`, three times. Only the question and the box change. That's the "repeated piece with a blank" idea from the Notes lesson, showing up in real code.

## Step 4: Piece 2, work out the rounds

This block has other blocks nested inside it. Build it from the outside in:

1.  Drag another `set` block from Variables under the last block, and choose `rounds` in its dropdown.
2.  From the green **Operators** category, drag `(abs v) of ()` into the slot where the `set` block's number is. Click its dropdown and choose **ceiling**.
3.  Drag the `() / ()` block into the slot of `ceiling of`.
4.  From Variables, drag `kids` into the left side of the division and `computers` into the right.

```scratch
set [rounds v] to ([ceiling v] of ((kids) / (computers)))
```

**Ceiling** rounds up to the next whole number: 1.5 becomes 2, 2.1 becomes 3, and 2 stays 2. It's exactly step 4 of the plan.

**Test it** with all three of these. Watch the `rounds` monitor each time:

```text
12 kids, 6 computers  ->  rounds 2
 9 kids, 6 computers  ->  rounds 2
 6 kids, 6 computers  ->  rounds 1
```

:::warning
**Floor instead of ceiling.** The same dropdown has **floor**, which rounds *down*. With floor, 9 kids gives 1 round, and 3 kids never get a computer. Scratch shows no error message, because nothing is broken as far as Scratch knows. The only way to catch this bug is to compare the monitor with the answer you worked out by hand in Step 1.
:::

## Step 5: Piece 3, work out the turn length

```scratch
set [turn v] to ((minutes) / (rounds))
```

**Test it.** 12, 6, 60 should give `turn` 30. Then try 18 kids, 6 computers, 60 minutes: that's 3 rounds, so `turn` should be 20.

## Step 6: Piece 4, announce the answer

The `join` block (in Operators) glues two pieces of text together, so the cat can say a number and a word in one bubble. To join three things, put a `join` inside another `join`.

```scratch
say (join (rounds) [ rounds.]) for (2) seconds
say (join [Each turn is ] (join (turn) [ minutes.])) for (3) seconds
```

Look closely at `[ rounds.]` and `[ minutes.]`. Each starts with a space. Without it, the cat says "2rounds." because `join` glues text together exactly as it is, adding nothing.

## The finished program

```scratch
when green flag clicked
ask [How many kids came today?] and wait
set [kids v] to (answer)
ask [How many computers work?] and wait
set [computers v] to (answer)
ask [How many minutes is the club open?] and wait
set [minutes v] to (answer)
set [rounds v] to ([ceiling v] of ((kids) / (computers)))
set [turn v] to ((minutes) / (rounds))
say (join (rounds) [ rounds.]) for (2) seconds
say (join [Each turn is ] (join (turn) [ minutes.])) for (3) seconds
```

Save it as `m2-fair-share-helper.sb3`.

Here are the same steps in a text language. Each line of Python lines up with a piece of your script. `input()` is Python's version of the `ask` block, and `int()` turns the typed text into a whole number, which Scratch does for you automatically. Step through it and watch `rounds` and `turn` appear only once the earlier lines have run:

:::trace The helper with 9 kids, 6 computers, and 60 minutes
```python input="9|6|60"
import math
kids = int(input("How many kids came today? "))
computers = int(input("How many computers work? "))
minutes = int(input("How many minutes is the club open? "))
rounds = math.ceil(kids / computers)
turn = minutes / rounds
print(rounds, "rounds.")
print("Each turn is", turn, "minutes.")
```
:::

Python prints `30.0` where Scratch says `30`. Both mean exactly thirty; Python just shows the decimal point after a division.

## Test it like you mean it

Run the finished helper with every row of this table, and compare what the cat says with the expected column:

| Kids | Computers | Minutes | Expected rounds | Expected turn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 | 6 | 60 | 2 | 30 |
| 9 | 6 | 60 | 2 | 30 |
| 6 | 6 | 60 | 1 | 60 |
| 18 | 6 | 60 | 3 | 20 |
| 7 | 6 | 45 | 2 | 22.5 |
| 0 | 6 | 60 | ? | ? |

Predict the last row before you run it. With 0 kids, `rounds` is 0, and the helper then divides 60 by 0. Scratch's answer is `Infinity`, so the cat cheerfully announces "Each turn is Infinity minutes." Nothing crashed, but the answer is nonsense. Inputs like 0 that sit at the very edge of what makes sense are called **edge cases**, and Module 5 shows how to catch them before they reach the user.

:::quiz
Q: Why does the helper use **ceiling** rather than **floor** to work out the rounds?
- [ ] Ceiling is faster
  Both take no noticeable time. The difference is the answer they give, not speed.
- [x] Rounding down would leave some kids without a turn
  With 9 kids and 6 computers, floor gives 1 round, so 3 kids sit out the whole hour. Ceiling gives 2 rounds, so everyone gets a turn.
- [ ] Scratch can't divide without it
  Scratch divides fine; 9 ÷ 6 gives 1.5. Ceiling only decides what to do with the .5.

Q: Why did you test piece 1 before building piece 2?
- [x] If something's wrong, you know it's in those seven blocks
  Testing each piece as you go keeps the search for a bug small. Testing only at the end means a wrong answer could be coming from anywhere.
- [ ] Scratch won't run a script with unfinished pieces
  Scratch runs whatever blocks are snapped together. That's exactly what lets you test half a program.
- [ ] To save the variables to your computer
  Running a script doesn't save anything. Saving is File › Save to your computer.

Q: You type `[rounds.]` with no space at the start. What does the cat say for 12 kids and 6 computers?
- [ ] 2 rounds.
  That needs a space somewhere, and `join` never adds one by itself.
- [x] 2rounds.
  `join` sticks the two pieces together exactly as they are, with nothing in between.
- [ ] An error message
  Missing spaces never cause an error; the text just looks wrong.
:::

:::takeaways
- Plan the pieces on paper first, and work out test answers by hand before you build.
- `ask` waits for typed input and puts it in `answer`; copy it into a variable straight away.
- Build and test one piece at a time. A bug found right after you add a piece must be in that piece.
- Some bugs make no error message at all. Comparing output with a hand-worked answer is how you catch them.
- Unusual inputs like 0 can produce nonsense answers, even when the program runs without errors.
:::
