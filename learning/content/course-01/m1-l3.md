Picture this: you spend 40 minutes getting a robot to drive around a room, you close the browser tab, and the robot is gone. Without a Scratch account, that's exactly what happens to work you haven't saved. The editor doesn't keep it for you. Two minutes of good habits now saves you from ever doing that work twice.

## Save a project to your computer

This works with or without an account, and in the Scratch app too.

1.  Click **File** in the menu bar at the top of the editor.
2.  Choose **Save to your computer**.
3.  Your browser downloads a file ending in `.sb3`. That one file holds everything: your sprites, scripts, sounds, and backdrops.

Some browsers ask you for a file name and a place to save it. Others save it straight to your Downloads folder with a name like `Scratch Project.sb3`. Here's where to find it:

:::tabs
@tab Windows
Press **Windows key + E** to open File Explorer, then click **Downloads** on the left. Chrome and Edge also show recent downloads when you click the download arrow near the top-right of the browser.
@tab macOS
Open **Finder** and press **Option + Cmd + L** to jump to your Downloads folder. In Safari, the download arrow in the top-right of the toolbar lists recent downloads.
@tab Chromebook
Open the **Files** app from the launcher (or press **Alt + Shift + M**), then click **Downloads** under **My files**.
:::

## Load it back

1.  Open the Scratch editor.
2.  Click **File**, then **Load from your computer**.
3.  Pick your `.sb3` file.

:::warning
**Loading replaces whatever is in the editor right now.** If you have unsaved work open, save it first, then load the other project.
:::

## If you have an account

When you're signed in, the online editor saves to your account. Type a name for the project in the title box at the top of the editor, then use **File** › **Save now** before you close the tab. Your projects are listed in **My Stuff**, the folder icon in the menu bar.

Even with an account, save a copy to your computer now and then. A file on your own computer is a backup that doesn't depend on being online.

## Name files so you can find them later

By the end of the course you'll have a dozen or more projects. "Scratch Project (14).sb3" won't help you find the one you want. Make a folder called `Computational Thinking` in your Documents folder, and name every file the same way:

```text
m1-first-walk.sb3
m2-fair-share-helper.sb3
m3-delivery-robot.sb3
m3-delivery-robot-v2.sb3
m4-square-flower.sb3
```

The module number goes first so the files sort in order. Next comes a few words about what the project does. When you're about to try something risky, save a `-v2` copy first. If the experiment goes badly, the version that worked is still there.

## Set up your algorithm notebook

Your notebook is where you plan before you build and where you write down what really happened. Use this layout whenever a lesson gives you a problem:

```text
Date:      ______
Problem:   What am I trying to do, in one sentence?
Inputs:    What information do I start with?
Output:    What should be true, or shown, when it's finished?
Steps:     1. ...
           2. ...
           3. ...
Test:      What I expected       |  What actually happened
           ....................  |  ....................
Fix:       What I changed, and why
```

Two habits make it far more useful. First, when a step turns out to be wrong, cross it out with one line instead of erasing it. Later you can see what you changed, which is exactly what you need when the same kind of bug shows up again. Second, always fill in **Test** before you look at the answer. Writing "I expected 3, I got 4" is the beginning of nearly every fix.

## How the lessons in this course work

Modules 2 to 7 each have three lessons:

-   **Notes** teach one big idea with everyday examples, diagrams, and a short video.
-   **Build-along** uses that idea to make one thing in Scratch, a step at a time. Each step adds a few blocks and tells you what you should see when you run them.
-   **Practice** gives you six problems that get harder as you go, then a challenge. Each has a folded **hint** and a folded **solution**. Give each problem at least five minutes of real effort before you open the hint.

Module 8 is a project of your own design.

Along the way you'll meet three special kinds of block in the lessons:

-   **Quizzes.** Pick an answer and you'll see why it's right or wrong. Every wrong option has an explanation, so a wrong pick still teaches you something.
-   **Step-through animations.** These show an algorithm written in Python, a text language. Use the step buttons to move forward and back one line at a time, and watch the variables change. You only need to read them, never write them.
-   **Scratch blocks.** They look like the real blocks in the editor, and the color tells you which category to find them in. Motion is blue, Looks is purple, Events is yellow, Control is orange.

## When you're stuck

Everyone gets stuck, including people who have programmed for years. The difference is that experienced programmers have a routine instead of staring at the screen hoping. Here's one:

```mermaid caption="A routine for getting unstuck: it loops until you find the step that goes wrong"
flowchart TD
    A[Stuck] --> B["Say it: I expected ___ but got ___"]
    B --> C[Be the computer: follow your steps by hand]
    C --> D{Found the step that goes wrong?}
    D -->|yes| E[Change one thing, then test again]
    D -->|no| F[Shrink it: test one small piece on its own]
    F --> C
    E --> G{Fixed?}
    G -->|yes| H[Write what you learned in your notebook]
    G -->|no| I[Ask for help, and bring your notes]
```

1.  **Say exactly what's wrong.** "It doesn't work" can't be fixed. "The robot should stop at the desk, but it drives through the wall" can.
2.  **Be the computer.** Put your finger on the first step and do only what it says. On paper, track where everything is after each step. Usually the bug shows up as the moment where what the step *says* differs from what you *meant*.
3.  **Shrink the problem.** Pull a few blocks off and run just the first part. If that part works, add the next part back. You've cornered the bug when the program breaks right after you add a piece.
4.  **Change one thing at a time.** If you change three blocks at once and it starts working, you won't know which change fixed it.
5.  **Ask for help, and bring your notes.** Show your script, what you expected, what happened, and what you already tried. A good question often solves itself while you're writing it down.

Taking a five-minute break counts too. Coming back with fresh eyes finds bugs surprisingly often.

## Safety nets in Scratch

-   **Undo.** Right-click an empty part of the code area and choose **Undo**, or press **Ctrl + Z** (**Cmd + Z** on a Mac).
-   **Restore.** Deleted a whole sprite by accident? **Edit** › **Restore Sprite** brings it back.
-   **Clean up Blocks.** Right-click the code area and choose **Clean up Blocks** to line up scattered scripts neatly.
-   **Add Comment.** Right-click a block and choose **Add Comment** to stick a note on it, such as "this part resets the robot". Notes like that help the next person who reads your project, and that next person is usually you.
