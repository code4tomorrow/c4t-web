:::objectives
- Turn a short list of plain-English steps into a Scratch script.
- Predict what a script will do, then run it and compare.
- See that a computer follows steps in exactly the order you give them.
- Step through the same algorithm written in a text language.
:::

Here are four steps. Read them and picture what happens before you build anything:

1.  Stand in the middle of the stage.
2.  Say "Watch me walk." for 2 seconds.
3.  Walk 150 steps to the right.
4.  Say "Made it!" for 2 seconds.

A list of steps that solves a problem is called an **algorithm**. You already follow algorithms all the time: a recipe, the directions to a friend's house, the rules printed inside a board game box. The difference in this course is who follows them. You'll write steps for a computer, which does exactly what you say and nothing you didn't say.

## Write your prediction first

Open your notebook and write one sentence: where will the cat be when the script finishes, and what will it have said, in what order?

This feels like a waste of time when the answer seems obvious. It isn't. A prediction is how you notice a surprise. If you don't decide what *should* happen, you can't tell when something goes wrong.

## Build the script, one block per step

Open the Scratch editor. Each block below matches one step of the algorithm, plus one block at the top that says *when* to start.

1.  Click the yellow **Events** circle. Drag `when green flag clicked` into the code area in the middle.
2.  Click **Motion**. Drag `go to x: () y: ()` and snap it under the first block. You'll see a gray shadow where it will land; let go and it clicks into place. Click each number and change both to `0`.
3.  Click the purple **Looks** circle. Drag `say [Hello!] for (2) seconds` under that. Click the word *Hello!* and type `Watch me walk.`
4.  Back in **Motion**, drag `move (10) steps` under that and change `10` to `150`.
5.  From **Looks**, add another `say` block and change its text to `Made it!`

Your script should look like this:

```scratch
when green flag clicked
go to x: (0) y: (0)
say [Watch me walk.] for (2) seconds
move (150) steps
say [Made it!] for (2) seconds
```

Step 1 of the plain-English version, "stand in the middle", became `go to x: (0) y: (0)`. The middle of the Scratch stage is the spot where x is 0 and y is 0. Module 3 explains these numbers properly; for now, think of them as the address of the center.

## Run it and compare

Click the green flag above the stage. Here is what happens:

```text
1. The cat jumps to the middle of the stage.
2. A speech bubble says "Watch me walk." for 2 seconds.
3. The cat jumps 150 steps to the right.
4. A new bubble says "Made it!" for 2 seconds, then disappears.
```

Look at line 3 again. The algorithm said *walk*, but the cat didn't walk. It vanished from one spot and appeared at the next, because `move` changes the cat's position all at once. You meant "walk", you wrote "move 150", and the computer did exactly what you wrote. That gap between what you meant and what you said is the main thing this course will train you to spot.

Now check your work against this list. It's your proof that everything is set up:

-   The cat ends up to the right of the center.
-   The two bubbles appear in the right order, one after the other.
-   Click the flag again. The same thing happens, from the same starting spot.

If all three are true, your computer and Scratch are ready for the rest of the course.

## Order is part of the algorithm

:::try
Pull the `move (150) steps` block out and snap it back in **above** the first `say` block. (Dragging a block also drags everything under it, so you may need to move the bottom blocks back afterwards.) Predict what will change, then click the flag.
:::

Now the cat jumps right first and *then* says "Watch me walk.", which makes no sense: it has already gone. The blocks are the same five blocks. Only the order changed, and the program changed with it. Computers don't rearrange steps into the order you probably meant.

Try one more experiment. Right-click the `go to x: (0) y: (0)` block (on a Mac trackpad, Control-click) and choose **Delete Block**. Only that block disappears, and the blocks below it close the gap. Click the flag three times. The cat moves further right every time, until it bumps the edge of the stage.

The computer remembers where the cat is between runs. Without the `go to` block, each run starts from wherever the last one ended. That remembered information is called **state**, and Module 3 is all about keeping track of it. Put the `go to` block back (or press **Ctrl + Z**, **Cmd + Z** on a Mac, to undo the delete) before you continue.

## The same steps in a text language

Scratch is one way to write an algorithm. Most programmers type theirs in a text language such as Python. You don't need to learn Python for this course. These step-through animations let you watch a computer follow steps one line at a time, which is hard to see in Scratch because it runs so fast.

Press the step button and watch `x`, the cat's position, in the variables panel:

:::trace The cat's walk, one line at a time
```python
x = 0
print("Watch me walk.")
x = x + 150
print("Made it!")
print("I am at x =", x)
```
:::

Notice that `x` stays at 0 while the first message prints. It only becomes 150 when line 3 runs. The computer does one step, finishes it, then moves to the next, in order, every time.

## If it doesn't work

:::warning
**Nothing happens when you click the flag.** The top block must be the curved-top `when green flag clicked` from Events, and every block must be snapped on. If there's a gap between two blocks, the ones below the gap never run. Drag the lower block up until it clicks into place.
:::

-   **The script disappeared.** Scripts belong to whichever sprite is selected. Look at the sprite list under the stage: the cat should be highlighted. If you had the stage (on the far right) selected, your blocks were added to the stage instead. Click the cat to find its scripts.
-   **The cat moves the wrong way or the wrong distance.** Check that `150` is in the `move` block, not in one of the `go to` boxes, and that there's no minus sign in front of it.
-   **A bubble shows the wrong words.** Click the white text inside the `say` block and retype it. Spelling inside the bubble doesn't break anything, but it's worth matching the plan.
