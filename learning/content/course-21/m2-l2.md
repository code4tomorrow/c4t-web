:::objectives
- Train a BPE tokenizer by repeatedly merging the most frequent pair of adjacent symbols.
- Use the learned merges to split words the tokenizer has never seen.
- Build a vocabulary with special tokens, encode a batch of sentences into padded ids with an attention mask, and decode them back.
- Count which words appear near which, weight the counts with PPMI, and compress them into dense vectors with SVD.
- Plot the vectors and use cosine similarity to find each word's nearest neighbours.
:::

You'll build the two halves of the pipeline from the last lesson, starting from nothing but 18 sentences: a subword tokenizer like the ones GPT models use, and a set of word vectors learned only from which words appear together. Here's where it ends up. The tokenizer, after 40 merges, splits a word it never saw in training:

```text
loaned    ['loan', 'ed</w>']
riverbank ['riv', 'er', 'bank</w>']
```

And the word vectors, squeezed into two dimensions, put money words in one group, river words in another, and *bank* between them, because it was used both ways.

Make a new Colab notebook called `nlp-m2-l2-tokenizer.ipynb` and add each step as a new cell. Everything here uses only Python, NumPy, and matplotlib.

## Step 1: A tiny corpus and a cleaning function

Half of these sentences use *bank* for money, half for a river's edge. That's on purpose: it's the ambiguity from the start of the module.

```python run
import re
import unicodedata
from collections import Counter

import numpy as np

corpus = [
    "She deposited her paycheck at the bank on Friday.",
    "The bank approved the loan after checking her account.",
    "He opened a savings account at the bank downtown.",
    "The loan has a low interest rate.",
    "Transfer money from your savings account to repay the loan.",
    "The bank charges a fee for every money transfer.",
    "Interest is added to the savings account each month.",
    "She asked the bank for a bigger loan.",
    "Deposit the check, and the money appears in your account.",
    "They walked along the river bank at sunset.",
    "The boat drifted down the river past the muddy bank.",
    "Fish swim in the cold river water.",
    "We sat on the grassy bank and watched the water.",
    "The river flooded the bank after the storm.",
    "Kids were fishing from the bank of the river.",
    "The water rose until the river covered the bank.",
    "A heron waited on the bank for fish.",
    "Rowing a boat on the river is calm in the morning.",
]

def tokenize(text):
    text = unicodedata.normalize("NFKD", text)
    text = "".join(ch for ch in text if not unicodedata.combining(ch))
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.casefold())

word_counts = Counter(word for sentence in corpus for word in tokenize(sentence))
print(len(word_counts), "different words,", sum(word_counts.values()), "words in total")
print(word_counts.most_common(6))
```

`tokenize` does the normalization from the last lesson (strip accents, case-fold) and keeps runs of letters and digits, which drops punctuation. Real BPE tokenizers keep punctuation and spaces; dropping them keeps this build small.

## Step 2: Train BPE by merging the most frequent pair

BPE starts with every word spelled as single characters, plus an end-of-word marker `</w>` so the tokenizer can tell a piece at the end of a word from the same letters in the middle. Then it repeats one move: count every pair of neighbouring symbols across the corpus, weighted by how often each word appears, and merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol.

```python run
def count_pairs(splits):
    pairs = Counter()
    for word, pieces in splits.items():
        for left, right in zip(pieces, pieces[1:]):
            pairs[left, right] += word_counts[word]
    return pairs

def merge_pieces(pieces, pair):
    out, i = [], 0
    while i < len(pieces):
        if i < len(pieces) - 1 and (pieces[i], pieces[i + 1]) == pair:
            out.append(pieces[i] + pieces[i + 1])
            i += 2
        else:
            out.append(pieces[i])
            i += 1
    return out

splits = {word: list(word) + ["</w>"] for word in word_counts}
print("Before:", splits["deposited"])

merges = []
for step in range(40):
    pairs = count_pairs(splits)
    best, freq = pairs.most_common(1)[0]
    merges.append(best)
    splits = {word: merge_pieces(pieces, best) for word, pieces in splits.items()}
    if step < 10:
        print(f"merge {step + 1:2}: {best[0]!r} + {best[1]!r}  (seen {freq} times)")

print("After: ", splits["deposited"], splits["savings"], splits["river"])
```

Read the merges in order. The first is `h` + `e`, because *the* appears 29 times and *he*, *her*, *checking* add more. Next `he` + `</w>` forms a word ending, then `t` + `he</w>` builds the whole word *the*. Frequent words become single tokens quickly; *bank* gets there by merge 11 (scroll the full list by changing `step < 10` to `step < 40`). Rare words stay in pieces, but the pieces they share, like `ed</w>` and `ing`, get merged because they add up across many words.

:::note
`pairs.most_common(1)` breaks ties by whichever pair was counted first. Real tokenizers use a fixed rule for ties too; without one, two training runs on the same text could give different vocabularies.
:::

## Step 3: Split words the tokenizer has never seen

To tokenize a new word, start from characters again and replay the merges in the order they were learned:

```python run
def encode_word(word):
    pieces = list(word) + ["</w>"]
    for pair in merges:
        pieces = merge_pieces(pieces, pair)
    return pieces

for word in ["loaned", "banking", "riverbank", "deposits", "zebra"]:
    print(f"{word:10}", encode_word(word))
```

None of these words is in the corpus. *loaned* comes out as `loan` + `ed</w>`, two pieces that carry meaning. *riverbank* shows why the end-of-word marker matters: the tokenizer learned `river</w>`, a *complete* word, so *river* in the middle of a compound only gets as far as `riv` + `er`. And *zebra* falls back to single letters, which is how BPE guarantees it can spell anything, as long as every character was seen in training.

## Step 4: A vocabulary of ids, with padding

A model needs integers. The vocabulary is two special tokens, then every starting character, then one new token per merge:

```python run
alphabet = sorted({ch for word in word_counts for ch in word}) + ["</w>"]
tokens = ["[PAD]", "[UNK]"] + alphabet + ["".join(pair) for pair in merges]
token_to_id = {token: i for i, token in enumerate(tokens)}
print("Vocabulary size:", len(tokens))

def encode(text):
    pieces = [piece for word in tokenize(text) for piece in encode_word(word)]
    return [token_to_id.get(piece, token_to_id["[UNK]"]) for piece in pieces]

def decode(ids):
    return "".join(tokens[i] for i in ids if i > 1).replace("</w>", " ").strip()

batch = ["The bank loaned money.", "A zebra swam in the river."]
encoded = [encode(text) for text in batch]
longest = max(len(ids) for ids in encoded)
input_ids = [ids + [0] * (longest - len(ids)) for ids in encoded]
attention_mask = [[1] * len(ids) + [0] * (longest - len(ids)) for ids in encoded]

for ids, mask in zip(input_ids, attention_mask):
    print(ids)
    print(mask)
print([decode(ids) for ids in encoded])
```

Two things to notice. The letter `z` never appeared in training, so it isn't in the alphabet and becomes `[UNK]` (id 1), and the decoded sentence silently loses it. GPT-2 avoids this by starting from the 256 possible *bytes* instead of characters, so every string is covered. Second, the shorter sentence is padded with `[PAD]` (id 0) to the length of the longer one, so the batch is a rectangle, and `attention_mask` marks which positions are real. Hugging Face tokenizers return the same two lists, under the same names.

## Step 5: Count which words appear near which

Now the second half: vectors that capture meaning. The plan is the distributional hypothesis made literal. For each word, count how often every other word appears within two positions of it. Function words like *the* appear next to everything and say nothing about meaning, so they're dropped first.

```python run
STOP = {"the", "a", "an", "and", "at", "of", "to", "in", "on", "is", "it", "her", "he",
        "she", "we", "they", "your", "for", "from", "after", "every", "has", "past",
        "down", "along", "were", "until", "each"}

sentences = [[w for w in tokenize(s) if w not in STOP] for s in corpus]
vocab = sorted({w for s in sentences for w in s})
index = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}

window = 2
cooc = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(vocab)))
for s in sentences:
    for i, word in enumerate(s):
        for j in range(max(0, i - window), min(len(s), i + window + 1)):
            if i != j:
                cooc[index[word], index[s[j]]] += 1

print(cooc.shape)
row = cooc[index["bank"]]
print("bank's neighbours:", [(vocab[j], int(row[j])) for j in row.argsort()[::-1][:6]])
```

Each row of `cooc` is a word's context profile. *bank*'s row mixes *river* (5 times), *water*, and *grassy* with *loan* and *friday*. That row already *is* a vector for *bank*, 50 numbers long, but it's noisy and mostly zeros.

## Step 6: Reweight with PPMI and compress with SVD

Raw counts favour frequent words. **Pointwise mutual information** asks a better question: does *river* appear near *bank* more often than you'd expect if words were scattered at random? PMI = log(P(word, context) / (P(word) × P(context))). Negative values are unreliable with this little data, so **PPMI** (positive PMI) clips them to zero.

Then **singular value decomposition** rewrites the matrix as a sum of patterns ordered from strongest to weakest. Keeping only the first few gives each word a short dense vector that captures its main patterns of use. This is the idea behind latent semantic analysis, and GloVe vectors are a close relative.

```python run
total = cooc.sum()
expected = cooc.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) * cooc.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True) / total
with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
    ppmi = np.maximum(np.log(cooc / expected), 0)

U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(ppmi)
vectors = U * S              # one row per word, strongest pattern first
coords = vectors[:, :2]      # keep two dimensions so we can plot them
print("Strongest singular values:", S[:4].round(2))
```

Now plot two groups of words, plus *bank*:

```python run alt="Scatter plot of words in two learned dimensions. Money words (account, interest, loan, transfer, fee) cluster low on the chart, river words (river, boat, water, muddy, fishing) cluster high on the right, and bank sits between the two groups." caption="Word vectors learned from 18 sentences, reduced to two dimensions"
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

groups = [
    (["account", "interest", "loan", "transfer", "fee"], "#2a78d6", "o", "money sentences"),
    (["river", "boat", "water", "muddy", "fishing"], "#eb6834", "s", "river sentences"),
    (["bank"], "#333333", "D", "bank"),
]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 5))
for words, color, marker, label in groups:
    points = np.array([coords[index[w]] for w in words])
    ax.scatter(points[:, 0], points[:, 1], s=60, color=color, marker=marker, label=label)
    for word, (x, y) in zip(words, points):
        ax.annotate(word, (x, y), xytext=(5, 4), textcoords="offset points", fontsize=9)

ax.set_xlabel("dimension 1")
ax.set_ylabel("dimension 2")
ax.set_title("PPMI + SVD word vectors")
ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
ax.legend(loc="best")
```

Nothing told the program which sentences were about money. The two groups come purely from which words shared neighbours. *bank* sits between them, because its context row is a mix of both. It's closer to the river side, since more of its 12 uses in this corpus are river uses. That's the limit of one vector per word, drawn as a picture.

## Step 7: Nearest neighbours by cosine similarity

Two dimensions are for plotting. For finding similar words, keep more: here, ten. **Cosine similarity** compares directions, ignoring length: divide each vector by its length, then take dot products.

```python run
def neighbours(word, k=4, dims=10):
    v = vectors[:, :dims]
    v = v / np.linalg.norm(v, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    sims = v @ v[index[word]]
    best = [j for j in sims.argsort()[::-1] if vocab[j] != word][:k]
    return [(vocab[j], round(float(sims[j]), 2)) for j in best]

for word in ["savings", "river", "bank"]:
    print(f"{word:8}", neighbours(word))
```

*savings* lands next to *account* and *loan*. *bank*'s neighbours come from both senses at once. With 18 sentences the lists are noisy; the same recipe on millions of sentences gives the familiar results where *coffee* sits near *tea*. Module 3 uses cosine similarity again, on whole documents.

## The finished program

Here are both halves in one file, the version to keep. It needs `corpus` from Step 1 and `STOP` from Step 5; paste those two lists above it if you run it on its own.

```python run title="textprep.py"
import re
import unicodedata
from collections import Counter

import numpy as np


def tokenize(text):
    text = unicodedata.normalize("NFKD", text)
    text = "".join(ch for ch in text if not unicodedata.combining(ch))
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.casefold())


def merge_pieces(pieces, pair):
    out, i = [], 0
    while i < len(pieces):
        if i < len(pieces) - 1 and (pieces[i], pieces[i + 1]) == pair:
            out.append(pieces[i] + pieces[i + 1])
            i += 2
        else:
            out.append(pieces[i])
            i += 1
    return out


def train_bpe(texts, num_merges):
    counts = Counter(w for t in texts for w in tokenize(t))
    splits = {w: list(w) + ["</w>"] for w in counts}
    merges = []
    for _ in range(num_merges):
        pairs = Counter()
        for w, pieces in splits.items():
            for pair in zip(pieces, pieces[1:]):
                pairs[pair] += counts[w]
        if not pairs:
            break
        best = pairs.most_common(1)[0][0]
        merges.append(best)
        splits = {w: merge_pieces(p, best) for w, p in splits.items()}
    alphabet = sorted({ch for w in counts for ch in w}) + ["</w>"]
    tokens = ["[PAD]", "[UNK]"] + alphabet + ["".join(m) for m in merges]
    return merges, {t: i for i, t in enumerate(tokens)}


def bpe_encode(text, merges, token_to_id):
    ids = []
    for word in tokenize(text):
        pieces = list(word) + ["</w>"]
        for pair in merges:
            pieces = merge_pieces(pieces, pair)
        ids += [token_to_id.get(p, token_to_id["[UNK]"]) for p in pieces]
    return ids


def word_vectors(texts, stop, window=2, dims=10):
    sents = [[w for w in tokenize(t) if w not in stop] for t in texts]
    vocab = sorted({w for s in sents for w in s})
    index = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
    cooc = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(vocab)))
    for s in sents:
        for i, w in enumerate(s):
            for j in range(max(0, i - window), min(len(s), i + window + 1)):
                if i != j:
                    cooc[index[w], index[s[j]]] += 1
    expected = cooc.sum(1, keepdims=True) * cooc.sum(0, keepdims=True) / cooc.sum()
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
        ppmi = np.maximum(np.log(cooc / expected), 0)
    U, S, _ = np.linalg.svd(ppmi)
    vecs = (U * S)[:, :dims]
    return vocab, index, vecs / np.linalg.norm(vecs, axis=1, keepdims=True)


merges, token_to_id = train_bpe(corpus, num_merges=40)
print(len(token_to_id), "tokens;", bpe_encode("The bank loaned money.", merges, token_to_id))

vocab, index, vecs = word_vectors(corpus, STOP)
sims = vecs @ vecs[index["account"]]
print("closest to account:", [vocab[j] for j in sims.argsort()[::-1][1:4]])
```

:::quiz
Q: Why does the tokenizer add `</w>` to the end of every word before training?
- [ ] To make every word the same length
  Words still have different lengths; one marker is added to each.
- [x] So a piece at the end of a word is a different token from the same letters elsewhere
  That's why *riverbank* can't use the learned `river</w>` token in the middle of the word.
- [ ] Because the regex needs it to find word boundaries
  The words were already separated by `tokenize`; the marker is for BPE.

Q: The batch's second sentence decodes as "a ebra swam in the river". Why is the *z* gone?
- [ ] `decode` drops the first letter of long words
  `decode` only skips ids 0 and 1, which are `[PAD]` and `[UNK]`.
- [x] *z* wasn't in the training alphabet, so it was encoded as `[UNK]`, which `decode` skips
  A byte-level base alphabet, as in GPT-2, would have covered it.
- [ ] Padding overwrote it
  Padding is added at the end of the shorter sequence, never inside it.

Q: Why use PPMI instead of raw co-occurrence counts before SVD?
- [ ] PPMI makes the matrix smaller
  The matrix keeps the same shape; only the values change.
- [x] It scores how much more often two words co-occur than chance would predict, so frequent words don't dominate
  A word that appears everywhere gets large raw counts with everything, but a low PMI.
- [ ] SVD only works on values between 0 and 1
  SVD works on any real matrix.
:::

:::takeaways
- BPE training is one loop: count adjacent pairs (weighted by word frequency), merge the most frequent, repeat. The merge list *is* the tokenizer.
- Encoding replays the merges in order, so unseen words split into known pieces; characters never seen in training become `[UNK]` unless the base alphabet is bytes.
- Batches are padded to a rectangle, and an attention mask marks the real positions.
- Co-occurrence counts, reweighted with PPMI and compressed with SVD, give dense word vectors in which words used alike end up close.
- One vector per word blends senses: *bank* lands between money and river.
:::

## Ideas to extend it

- Train with 10, 40, and 100 merges and print how many tokens `encode` produces for the whole corpus each time. More merges means fewer, longer tokens.
- Start from bytes, as GPT-2 does: spell each word as its UTF-8 bytes with `list(word.encode("utf-8"))`, and give each merge a new integer id instead of joining strings. Then any text works, *zebra* and emoji included. This is the version Karpathy builds in the video.
- Change `window` to 1 and to 5, and watch how the neighbour lists change. Small windows tend to group words that play the same grammatical role; large windows group words by topic.
- Add six sentences about cafés and drinks, and check that a third group appears with more dimensions.
