Here is a comparison that could end up in a real report. On the diabetes data, with "progression above 200" as the positive class, a logistic regression and a boosted model are each trained on 75% of the patients and scored with average precision on the other 25%. That one split is repeated with 30 different random seeds.

```python run alt="Strip chart of 30 dots showing logistic regression's AP minus boosting's AP on 30 random splits; most dots are between 0.03 and 0.12, three dots fall below zero, the lowest near -0.07" caption="Each dot is one possible single-split experiment."
import sklearn
from threadpoolctl import threadpool_limits
threadpool_limits(limits=1, user_api="openmp")

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import average_precision_score
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

data = load_diabetes(as_frame=True, scaled=False)
X, y = data.data, (data.target > 200).astype(int)
gaps = []
for seed in range(30):
    X_a, X_b, y_a, y_b = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.25, stratify=y, random_state=seed)
    logistic = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression()).fit(X_a, y_a)
    boosting = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(learning_rate=0.05, max_leaf_nodes=8,
                                              random_state=0).fit(X_a, y_a)
    gaps.append(average_precision_score(y_b, logistic.predict_proba(X_b)[:, 1])
                - average_precision_score(y_b, boosting.predict_proba(X_b)[:, 1]))
gaps = np.array(gaps)
print(f"logistic wins on {np.sum(gaps > 0)} of 30 splits; gap ranges from {gaps.min():.3f} to {gaps.max():.3f}")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 1.8))
ax.scatter(gaps, np.random.default_rng(0).uniform(-0.3, 0.3, 30), s=40, color="#2a78d6")
ax.axvline(0, color="#52514e", lw=1)
ax.set_yticks([])
ax.set_ylim(-1, 1)
ax.set_xlabel("AP(logistic) minus AP(boosting) on one split")
```

Logistic regression wins most of the time, by anything from almost nothing to 0.12. On three of the splits, boosting wins. If your project happened to use one of those three seeds, your report would have recommended the wrong model, and nothing in that report would have told you so. The capstone is about making comparisons that can't be fooled this easily.

## The brief

You will deliver a **reproducible model-comparison report**: a Colab notebook that runs top to bottom with fixed seeds, plus a one-to-two-page write-up. Together they must show:

1.  **A decision and a metric.** Who uses the model, what they do with its output, and which metric matches that use.
2.  **Honest validation.** A sealed test set opened once, repeated cross-validation on the rest, and group or time splits where the data needs them.
3.  **Bounded tuning.** At most three candidate model families plus a baseline, each with a fixed tuning budget, tuned *inside* the cross-validation.
4.  **Imbalance handling.** A justified choice between plain training, class weights, and threshold tuning.
5.  **Leakage checks.** A shuffled-label test, a single-feature scan, and a duplicate or group check.
6.  **Calibration.** A reliability diagram and Brier score for the finalists, and recalibration if the probabilities will be used.
7.  **Interpretation.** Permutation importance and at least one partial dependence or ICE plot, with a sentence on what they mean.
8.  **Error analysis.** Errors by at least two slices, with intervals and a per-slice baseline.
9.  **A recommendation and its limits.** Which model, at which threshold, with what uncertainty, and what the evidence can't tell you.

## Write the plan before you look

Every choice you make after seeing results is a chance to fool yourself: switching to the metric your favourite model wins on, dropping the fold that went badly, tuning "just a bit more". The fix is old and simple. Write the plan first, commit it to the top of your notebook, and treat any later change as something you must report.

Copy this template into a text cell and fill it in:

```text
PROJECT PLAN (written before any model is trained)

Decision:        who acts on the output, and what they do
Positive class:  exact definition, e.g. target > 200
Primary metric:  one metric, and why it matches the decision
Also reported:   ROC AUC, Brier score, cost at the chosen threshold
Cost of errors:  a missed positive costs __, a false alarm costs __
Candidates:      baseline + up to 3 model families
Tuning budget:   grid or number of random configs per family
Validation:      test share __% sealed; outer CV __ folds x __ repeats;
                 inner CV __ folds; group/time split needed? why?
Imbalance plan:  plain / class_weight / threshold, and how you'll choose
Leakage checks:  shuffled labels, single-feature scan, duplicates/groups
Slices:          at least two, with why they matter to the users
Decision rule:   "Prefer the simpler model unless the other wins by
                 more than __ AP on average AND in most paired folds"
Seeds:           one global seed, used everywhere
```

The last field matters most. A **decision rule** written in advance stops you from calling a 0.004 gap a win.

## How you'll decide between two models

Repeated cross-validation gives each candidate a score on the same folds, so you compare them fold by fold. A natural idea is a paired t-test on those differences, but it is too confident: the folds share most of their training rows, so their scores are correlated and the test treats them as more independent than they are. Nadeau and Bengio's **corrected resampled t-test** inflates the variance by a term that depends on the share of data in each test fold. You will implement it in six lines in the next lesson. The video explains why the usual tests break here and which ones are used instead.

:::video hh3CR64otvE
What to watch for: the resampled paired t-test and why its assumptions fail when training sets overlap, which is exactly the problem the corrected version fixes.

## Budget your compute

A plan should fit in your time. Count the fits: each outer fold runs every candidate's search, and each search fits every configuration on every inner fold, plus one refit.

```python run
import time

outer_folds, repeats, inner_folds = 5, 2, 3
grid_sizes = {"baseline": 1, "logistic": 4, "boosting": 4}
fits = {name: outer_folds * repeats * (size * inner_folds + 1) if size > 1
        else outer_folds * repeats for name, size in grid_sizes.items()}
print("fits per candidate:", fits, "| total:", sum(fits.values()))

start = time.perf_counter()
HistGradientBoostingClassifier(max_iter=100, random_state=0).fit(X, y)
one_fit = time.perf_counter() - start
print(f"one boosting fit takes about {one_fit:.2f} s, so its searches need about "
      f"{fits['boosting'] * one_fit:.0f} s")
```

About 270 fits is comfortable on a dataset this size. If your own dataset has 100,000 rows, the same plan might take an hour; shrink the grid, use fewer repeats, or switch to `HalvingRandomSearchCV` from Module 4 before you start, not halfway through.

:::quiz
Q: Why must the decision rule be written before you see any cross-validation results?
- [ ] Because scikit-learn needs it as a parameter
  It's a rule for you, not for the library.
- [x] Because once you've seen the numbers, it's easy to pick a rule that favours the model you already prefer
  Fixing the rule in advance turns "I think it's better" into a test the result can actually fail.
- [ ] Because the test set needs to know which model will win
  The test set is opened once at the end and never informs the rule.

Q: Your plan has 3 candidates, each with a 6-configuration grid, 3 inner folds, and 5 × 3 outer folds. Roughly how many fits is that?
- [ ] About 90
  That counts outer folds times configurations but forgets the inner folds and refits.
- [x] About 850
  Each candidate needs 15 × (6 × 3 + 1) = 285 fits, and three candidates make 855.
- [ ] About 18
  That's one candidate's inner search for a single outer fold, without the refit.
:::

:::takeaways
- A single split can crown the wrong model; repeated, paired cross-validation is the minimum for a comparison.
- Write the decision, metric, validation scheme, budget, and decision rule before training anything.
- Count your fits and time one of them before committing to a plan.
- Use a corrected test for paired fold scores; plain paired t-tests are overconfident when training sets overlap.
:::
