- Decide which preview readings to trust, and predict bars from raw scores.

The middle exercises build a quick recycling sorter. Before you start, gather about a dozen clean, empty household items: cans, plastic bottles, a cardboard box, paper, and non-recyclable things like a chip bag, a candy wrapper, or a used paper towel. Recycling rules differ from town to town, so decide which items count as **Recycling** using your local program's list, and write that rule in your log. Wash anything that held food.

## Warm-up: which readings would you trust?

Three moments from a rock, paper, scissors preview. For each, would you act on the answer?

1. **Rock 51%, Paper 47%**, steady for two seconds.
2. **Paper 99%**, steady, for a flat hand at your desk.
3. Flickering between **Scissors 95%** and **Paper 80%** several times a second.

:::solution
1. **No.** A near tie means the model can't separate the two classes for this input. Treat it as "unsure."
2. **Yes, probably.** It's steady, it's high, and it's the familiar situation the model was trained in.
3. **No.** Any single frame looks confident, but the answer changes from frame to frame. The flicker is the real signal: the input sits on the border between two classes.
:::

### Exercise 1: Predict the bars

A model's raw scores for one frame are **Rock 3.0, Paper 3.0, Scissors 0.0, Nothing 0.0**. Estimate the four percentages before you look.

:::hint
Equal scores get equal shares. A gap of 3 is big after softmax: each point of gap multiplies the share by about 2.7.
:::

:::solution
```python run
import math
scores = {"Rock": 3.0, "Paper": 3.0, "Scissors": 0.0, "Nothing": 0.0}
total = sum(math.exp(s) for s in scores.values())
for name, s in scores.items():
    print(f"{name:9}{math.exp(s) / total:.0%}")
```

Rock and Paper each get about 48%, and the other two about 2% each. In the preview this looks like a two-way tie, which is exactly what it is: the model sees a hand that fits Rock and Paper equally and is sure it's neither of the others.
:::

### Exercise 2: Build a sorter and hold items out

1. **Before recording anything,** split your items. For each class, put one or two items in a bag marked *TEST*. These are your unseen objects; they never go in front of the camera during recording.
2. Start an image project with classes `Recycling`, `Trash`, and `Nothing`.
3. Record about 60 samples per class using the remaining items. Hold each item at different angles, crushed and uncrushed if it's a can, on the table and in your hand. **Nothing** is the empty table, and your empty hand.
4. Train with the defaults.
5. Test each *training* item three times, then each *TEST* item three times. Write the top class and confidence each time.

Predict before testing: will the TEST items score lower, the same, or higher?

:::hint
The model has seen every angle of the training cans. Has it seen *this* label, this color, this shape?
:::

:::solution
Expected result: training items come back right almost every time, usually above 95%. TEST items are less reliable. Something close in look to a training item (another aluminum can) often still works, at lower confidence. Something with a new look is where it fails: a glass jar when you trained on no glass, or a shiny chip bag that gets called Recycling because shiny and crinkly looked like a crushed can.

That's the model telling you what it actually learned: shine, color, and shape, not what the item is made of. Your log should report the TEST-item accuracy as the sorter's score, not the training-item one.
:::

### Exercise 3: Change the surface

Test your TEST items again, this time on a different surface: the floor, a patterned cloth, or a colored sheet of paper instead of the table you trained on.

:::hint
In Module 2 you saw that a model learns whatever differs between classes, including backgrounds. What's the background in most of your sorter's samples?
:::

:::solution
Expected result: accuracy drops again, and **Nothing** starts showing up for real items (or real items get pulled toward whichever class you held up most often near that color). Most of every sample is the table, so a new surface changes most of the picture. The fix is the same as in Module 2: record every class on two or three surfaces. Then retest on the TEST items, not the training ones.
:::

### Exercise 4: Things with no class

Hold up things that are neither recycling nor trash: your phone, a shoe, a stuffed toy, your face. Write the bars for each.

:::hint
How many choices does the model have, and must it use one of them?
:::

:::solution
Expected result: each one gets a confident answer, often 90% or more, in Recycling, Trash, or Nothing. A phone, with its glass and metal, frequently comes out as Recycling. The model must split 100% across three classes and has no "that's not waste" option.

Two practical fixes: make **Nothing** cover more ("not an item to sort": empty table, hands, phones, people passing), and, in an application, don't act on any reading you can't trust. The next exercise picks that line.
:::

### Exercise 5: Choose a confidence threshold

A sorter could act only when the top class is at or above a **threshold**, and otherwise ask a person. Here are 30 unseen test results, each the top confidence and whether the answer was right. For thresholds of 50, 70, 80, 90, and 95, how many items would the sorter act on, how many of those would be wrong, and how many go to a person? Which threshold would you pick for a school cafeteria?

```text
right: 99 98 97 99 95 92 88 99 97 84 96 99 91 86 98 99 93 81 76 94 89
wrong: 99 93 87 78 71 64 58 55 52
```

:::hint
For each threshold, count the values at or above it in each row. Items below the threshold aren't wrong; they're handed off.
:::

:::solution
```python run alt="Line chart of how many of the 30 items a sorter acts on and how many of those are wrong, for thresholds from 50 to 95 percent. As the threshold rises, items acted on fall from 30 to 12, and wrong actions fall from 9 to 1." caption="Raising the threshold trades wrong answers for hand-offs."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

right = [99, 98, 97, 99, 95, 92, 88, 99, 97, 84, 96, 99, 91, 86, 98, 99, 93, 81, 76, 94, 89]
wrong = [99, 93, 87, 78, 71, 64, 58, 55, 52]
thresholds = [50, 70, 80, 90, 95]
acted, acted_wrong = [], []
print("threshold  acts on  wrong  to a person")
for t in thresholds:
    ok = sum(c >= t for c in right)
    bad = sum(c >= t for c in wrong)
    acted.append(ok + bad)
    acted_wrong.append(bad)
    print(f"{t:>8}%  {ok + bad:>7}  {bad:>5}  {30 - ok - bad:>11}")

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3.2))
plt.plot(thresholds, acted, marker="o", color="#1967D2", label="Items acted on")
plt.plot(thresholds, acted_wrong, marker="o", color="#E67701", label="Wrong actions")
plt.xlabel("Threshold (%)")
plt.ylabel("Items (of 30)")
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
```

As the threshold rises, wrong actions fall, but more items go to a person. At 50% the sorter acts on everything and gets 9 wrong. At 90% it acts on 17 items with 2 wrong and hands off 13. At 95% only 1 of its 12 actions is wrong, but 18 of 30 items need a person, which defeats the point of a sorter.

For a cafeteria, 80% or 90% is a reasonable choice, and whichever you pick, you'd say so in your log: *"acts at 90%; on our unseen test, 2 of its 17 actions were wrong, and it handed off 13 of 30 items."* Notice that no threshold removes every error: one wrong answer was at 99%.
:::

### Exercise 6: Write the sorter's test plan

Write an unseen test plan for your sorter that someone else could follow: which items, how many photos or tries per class, which surfaces and lighting, and who handles the items. Keep it to one screen.

:::hint
Reuse the structure of the rock, paper, scissors test plan: what changes in real use, at least 10 per class, truth written down first, and nothing from the test ever trained on.
:::

:::solution
```text
UNSEEN TEST PLAN   recycling-sorter
Rule:      "Recycling" = items on our town's curbside list
Items:     held out before recording, kept in the TEST bag
           Recycling: 2 cans (new brands), 1 glass jar, 1 yogurt cup,
                      1 cereal box, 1 sheet of office paper
           Trash:     1 chip bag, 1 candy wrapper, 1 paper towel,
                      1 plastic utensil, 1 foam cup
Per class: 12 tries (each item 2 to 3 times, different angles)
Conditions:
           half on the training table, half on the cafeteria tray
           half under the room lights, half by the window
           4 tries per class done by a friend, who agreed
Nothing:   12 tries: empty tray, hands, phone, someone walking past
Record:    item, true class, top class, confidence, surface, light
Never:     put a TEST item in front of the camera while recording
```
:::

### Challenge: Fool it on purpose

Find something that makes your sorter say **Recycling** at 95% or more when no recyclable is there. Try a photo of a can shown on your phone screen, a drawing of a bottle, or a piece of foil crumpled into a ball.

:::hint
The Teachable Machine FAQ suggests exactly this: try tricking the model with a photo or a drawing of an object. Ask what the model can and can't sense through a webcam.
:::

:::solution
Expected result: a photo of a can on a phone screen often scores very high for Recycling, because to a webcam it looks almost exactly like a can. Crumpled foil can pass as a crushed can too. Drawings are less reliable: a detailed color drawing may work; a stick-figure sketch usually doesn't.

The lesson for real use: an image model judges **how things look from one angle**, not what they're made of or whether they're real. A real sorter would need either more information (a scale, a metal sensor) or a person checking the cases where looks can lie. Write your fooling examples in your log; they're excellent unseen test items for the next version.
:::
