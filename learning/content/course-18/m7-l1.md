A CR2032 coin cell holds roughly 220 mAh. Run a gesture remote at a steady 6 mA and the cell is flat in about a day and a half. Keep the same model and the same chip, but let it sleep between predictions so it averages 50 µA, and the same cell lasts around six months.

Nothing about the model changed. The difference is how much of each second the chip spends awake. This lesson does that arithmetic properly, then turns to the other reason TinyML runs on the device: the raw data never has to leave it. That protects more than you might expect, but less than people usually claim.

## Battery life is capacity divided by average current

A battery's capacity is a charge, measured in **milliamp-hours** (mAh): 220 mAh can supply 220 mA for one hour, 1 mA for 220 hours, or any other split. So:

`battery life (hours) = capacity (mAh) ÷ average current (mA)`

The hard part is the average. A TinyML device moves between states (asleep, sampling, running the model, using the radio), each with its own current and duration. The average is each state's current weighted by the share of time spent in it. Step through one 2-second cycle of a sensor that wakes every 2 seconds:

:::trace Average current over one wake cycle
```python
states = [("sleep", 0.003, 1900), ("sense + infer", 5.0, 95), ("radio", 6.4, 5)]   # name, mA, ms
charge = 0       # mA x ms
period = 0       # ms
for name, ma, ms in states:
    charge = charge + ma * ms
    period = period + ms
avg_ma = charge / period
print(f"average {avg_ma:.3f} mA")
print(f"220 mAh lasts {220 / avg_ma / 24:.0f} days")
```
:::

The chip sleeps 95% of the time, yet the 95 ms awake contributes almost all of the charge: 5 mA × 95 ms is 475, while sleep adds under 6. That's the first rule of power design: **active time dominates until you make it tiny**.

Where do these numbers come from? The nRF52840's product brief lists 6.4 mA for the radio transmitting at 0 dBm and 3.16 µA asleep in its "System ON" mode, with all RAM kept and a timer running to wake it (both measured with its DC/DC regulator at 3 V). The 5 mA for "sense + infer" is a planning figure for the CPU and sensor together; you replace it with a measurement.

## Duty cycling, and the floor that sleep current sets

The **duty cycle** is the fraction of time a device is awake. Average current is then:

`average = duty × active current + (1 − duty) × sleep current`

Lower the duty cycle and battery life climbs, but only until the sleep term takes over. Plot it:

```python run alt="Log-log chart of battery life in days against duty cycle from 0.01% to 100%, for a 220 mAh coin cell with sleep currents of 3 µA and 100 µA. Both lines rise as duty cycle falls. The 100 µA line flattens near 90 days; the 3 µA line keeps rising to over 2,000 days at the lowest duty cycles." caption="Below a few percent duty cycle, sleep current decides the battery life"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

CAPACITY_MAH = 220
ACTIVE_MA = 5.0
duty = np.logspace(-4, 0, 200)          # 0.01% to 100% awake

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
for sleep_ua, color in [(3, "#1baf7a"), (100, "#eb6834")]:
    avg_ma = duty * ACTIVE_MA + (1 - duty) * sleep_ua / 1000
    plt.loglog(duty * 100, CAPACITY_MAH / avg_ma / 24, color=color, linewidth=2,
               label=f"sleep current {sleep_ua} µA")
plt.axhline(365, color="#999", linestyle=":")
plt.text(0.012, 400, "1 year", color="#666")
plt.xlabel("Duty cycle (% of time awake)")
plt.ylabel("Coin-cell life (days)")
plt.legend(frameon=False)
plt.grid(alpha=0.25, which="both")
plt.tight_layout()

for d in [1.0, 0.05, 0.001]:
    for s in [3, 100]:
        avg = d * ACTIVE_MA + (1 - d) * s / 1000
        print(f"awake {d:7.1%}, sleep {s:3} µA -> {CAPACITY_MAH / avg / 24:7.0f} days")
```

At 100% awake, sleep current is irrelevant: about 2 days either way. At 0.1% awake, the 3 µA design lasts years and the 100 µA design stalls at a few months. Once a device is mostly asleep, cutting sleep current is worth more than any speed-up to the model.

That's why development boards are poor guides to battery life. A board like the Nano 33 BLE Sense keeps a power LED, a voltage regulator, and several sensors powered even when the chip sleeps, so it draws far more than the chip's 3 µA. Battery products use custom boards that switch peripherals off. Always measure the current of the hardware you'll actually ship.

```mermaid caption="A duty-cycled TinyML device spends most of its life in the first state"
stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Sleep
    Sleep --> Sense: timer or sensor interrupt
    Sense --> Infer: window full
    Infer --> Sleep: nothing interesting
    Infer --> Radio: event detected
    Radio --> Sleep: message sent
```

The interrupt arrow is the second big lever. Many accelerometers and some microphones can watch for motion or sound on their own, drawing microamps, and wake the main chip only when something happens. The model then runs only on the moments that might matter.

## The radio is the expensive part

Pete Warden, who helped create TensorFlow Lite Micro at Google, put the rule simply: energy grows with how far you send the bits. Moving data a few millimeters to the CPU is cheap; moving it meters over a radio is expensive. Compare how much each design would have to transmit per day:

```python run
SECONDS_PER_DAY = 86_400
designs = {
    "stream raw accelerometer (62.5 Hz, 3 axes, 2 bytes)": 62.5 * 3 * 2 * SECONDS_PER_DAY,
    "stream raw audio (16 kHz, 2 bytes)": 16_000 * 2 * SECONDS_PER_DAY,
    "send each gesture label (200/day, 20 bytes)": 200 * 20,
    "send one daily summary (64 bytes)": 64,
}
for name, nbytes in designs.items():
    print(f"{name:52} {nbytes / 1e6:12.6f} MB/day")
```

Streaming raw accelerometer data means 32 MB a day; raw audio, 2.8 GB. Sending labels is a few kilobytes, thousands of times less. Streaming audio also means the radio transmits all day, and at 6.4 mA the radio alone would flatten a coin cell in about a day and a half. For a battery-powered microphone, local inference isn't an optimization. It's the only design that works.

## What on-device inference protects

When the model runs on the device, the raw audio, images, and motion traces can be processed in RAM and thrown away. Nothing raw is transmitted, so there's nothing to intercept on the way, nothing piling up on a server to be breached, and no company holding hours of recordings from someone's home. Collecting only the data you need for a purpose has a name, **data minimization**, and privacy laws such as the EU's GDPR require it. On-device inference makes it the default.

## What still leaks

"The data never leaves the device" is a slogan, not a guarantee. Four leaks remain:

1.  **The outputs themselves.** A label is still information. A kitchen sound classifier that reports only "activity" with a timestamp still reveals when people wake, eat, and sleep, and when the house is empty.
2.  **Metadata.** Even with no content, the timing, size, and frequency of messages, plus the device's radio identity, show that something happened and roughly where.
3.  **The device.** Anyone holding the board may be able to read its flash through the debug port, unless the chip's readback protection is turned on. That exposes any stored clips or logs, and the model itself.
4.  **Your development data.** The recordings you collected to train and field-test the model are raw data. They need consent, secure storage, and deletion when the project ends.

The first leak is easiest to see. Here's one week of "activity" events from such a kitchen sensor, with only label and time sent:

```python run alt="Bar chart of activity events by hour of day over one week. Events cluster at 6 to 8 am, 12 to 1 pm, 5 to 8 pm, and 9 to 11 pm, with none overnight. A second small panel shows events per day, with zero events on Saturday." caption="No audio left the house, but the labels alone map out a routine"
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
events = []
for day in range(7):
    if day == 5:                                   # Saturday: everyone away
        continue
    for start, end, rate in [(6.5, 8.0, 6), (12, 13, 3), (17.5, 20, 8), (21, 23, 2)]:
        n = rng.poisson(6 if (day == 6 and start == 12) else rate)
        events += list(day * 24 + rng.uniform(start, end, n))
events = np.array(events)

fig, (a1, a2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.4), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [3, 1.3]})
a1.hist(events % 24, bins=24, range=(0, 24), color="#2a78d6")
a1.set_xlabel("Hour of day"); a1.set_ylabel("Events in the week")
a1.set_xticks(range(0, 25, 3))
per_day = np.bincount((events // 24).astype(int), minlength=7)
a2.bar(["Mon", "Tue", "Wed", "Thu", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"], per_day, color=["#2a78d6"] * 5 + ["#d64545", "#2a78d6"])
a2.set_ylabel("Events per day")
a2.tick_params(axis="x", labelrotation=45)
plt.tight_layout()
print("events per day:", per_day.tolist())
```

From labels and timestamps alone you can read wake-up time, dinner time, bedtime, and the fact that nobody was home on Saturday. The fix is to send less: counts per day instead of timestamps, or nothing at all unless a user asks. The build-along designs exactly that.

:::warning
**Power and privacy mistakes that show up late**

1.  **Measuring battery life on a development board.** LEDs, regulators, and sensors swamp the chip's sleep current. Measure the real hardware, or at least subtract what you can't ship.
2.  **Forgetting serial debug output.** A sketch that prints raw sensor values over Serial during development will happily do the same in the field. Remove raw-data prints before deployment.
3.  **Treating labels as harmless.** Timestamps on labels can reveal more than the label. Send the coarsest data the purpose needs.
:::

:::quiz
Q: A device draws 4 mA awake and 2 µA asleep, and is awake 1% of the time. Roughly what is its average current?
- [ ] 2 µA
  That ignores the awake time, which dominates here.
- [x] About 42 µA
  0.01 × 4 mA = 40 µA, plus 0.99 × 2 µA ≈ 2 µA, for about 42 µA.
- [ ] About 2 mA
  That would be the average if it were awake half the time.

Q: Your device draws 5 mA awake, is awake 0.05% of the time, and sleeps at 80 µA. Which change extends battery life most?
- [ ] Making inference twice as fast
  At 0.05% duty, active time contributes about 2.5 µA; halving it barely moves an 80 µA total.
- [x] Cutting sleep current to 5 µA
  Sleep is almost all of the average here, so it drops from about 82 µA to about 7.5 µA, roughly 10 times the life.
- [ ] Using a bigger model
  A bigger model makes active time longer, which can only shorten battery life.

Q: A doorbell runs a keyword spotter locally and sends only "doorbell pressed, 18:42". What can still leak?
- [ ] Nothing, since no audio leaves the device
  The message itself carries information.
- [x] When people come and go, from the timestamps
  A log of times reveals routines and absences even without audio.
- [ ] The full audio, through the label
  A label can't reconstruct audio; the risk is what the label and time reveal.
:::

:::takeaways
- Battery life (hours) = capacity (mAh) ÷ average current (mA), and the average weights each state by its share of time.
- Active time dominates until the duty cycle is small; after that, sleep current sets the ceiling.
- Wake on interrupts so the model runs only when something might be happening.
- Sending labels instead of raw data cuts radio traffic by thousands of times, which saves energy and protects privacy at once.
- Outputs, metadata, physical access, and development data can all still leak. Send the coarsest data the purpose needs.
:::
